After LASIK: Why the Refraction Keeps Moving and What Glasses Can Still Do

November 23, 2026 · 9 min read

The surgery changes the shape of the cornea, not the rules of optics

Refractive surgery is often described as removing the need for glasses. What it actually does is more specific: it removes a calculated amount of corneal tissue to change the curvature of the front surface, so that light converging through the cornea lands closer to the retina without help. The eye that results is still an optical system with a focal length, a tear film, a pupil that changes size, and a lens inside it that will keep stiffening with age. Everything that could go wrong optically before surgery can still go wrong after it — and a few new things become possible.

That is why glasses after laser surgery are neither a failure nor a contradiction. They are what you use when the new corneal shape does not deliver a clean image in every condition, which is common enough that most refractive surgery practices expect some patients to wear something again eventually.

Principle: flattening the centre makes the cornea oblate

The normal cornea is slightly prolate: steeper at the centre than at the edge, which is a useful shape because it partly offsets the spherical aberration the eye would otherwise have. Myopic laser treatment removes tissue from the central zone and flattens it, so the cornea becomes oblate — flatter in the middle than at the periphery. An oblate surface adds positive spherical aberration instead of compensating for it. That is a higher-order aberration: a fourth-order term in the Zernike description of the wavefront, in contrast to defocus and regular astigmatism, which are second-order.

This matters because ordinary spectacles cannot correct fourth-order error. A lens can add sphere, cylinder and prism. It cannot put back the phase relationship across the pupil that the oblate cornea destroyed. That is the single most useful thing to understand about post-surgical vision: a refraction can be plano, the letter chart can read 20/20 in a bright room with a small pupil, and the same eye can still see starbursts around headlights at night when the pupil dilates and admits the marginal rays that carry most of the aberration.

What the first months are actually doing

Three processes overlap in the weeks after surgery, and all three move the refraction.

  • Epithelial remodelling. The surface layer thickens and redistributes over the treated zone. It is a few tens of micrometres of tissue, but it sits on the steepest part of the optical system, so small changes shift power measurably.
  • Tear film instability. The corneal nerves that drive reflex tearing and blink quality are cut during flap creation or surface ablation, and they regrow slowly. An unstable tear film is an irregular optical surface, which is why vision fluctuates through the day and why the same eye can read two lines differently an hour apart.
  • Refractive regression. The intended correction partially relaxes as the cornea remodels. The residual is what a residual refractive error means: not a complication, just the difference between the target and the outcome.

A refraction measured at week three is therefore a snapshot of a moving system. Most surgeons deliberately wait for stability before offering an enhancement, and most optometrists will not write a final prescription during the fluctuation phase. If someone hands you a new prescription four weeks after surgery and it feels wrong two weeks later, the prescription is not necessarily wrong — the cornea moved.

Why a plano refraction can still blur

Patients often arrive with a refraction of plano in both eyes and a complaint that nothing is crisp. There are four usual explanations, and they are worth separating because only one of them is fixable with a lens.

  • Higher-order aberration, as above. Worst in low light, around lights, and with large pupils. A spectacle lens will not fix it; a rigid contact lens can, because it replaces the front surface with a new one.
  • Dry eye and ocular surface disease. Very common after surgery and a genuine cause of fluctuating vision. This is a tear film problem, not a glasses problem.
  • Small residual astigmatism that the patient notices even at 0.50 D, especially if it is oblique.
  • Anisometropia between the two eyes, which produces unequal image sizes and a subtle sense that the world is not quite coherent even when each eye alone is acceptable.

Common misconceptions

"Plano means the surgery worked and I am finished." Plano describes the spherical and cylindrical error at the corneal plane on the day of the test. It says nothing about aberration, tear film, or what will happen to the same cornea in ten years.

"Needing glasses again means the surgery failed." Refractive surgery sets a new baseline; it does not freeze the eye. The crystalline lens continues to change with age, presbyopia arrives on its own schedule around the forties regardless of what happened to the cornea, and a small myopic or hyperopic drift over years is expected rather than exceptional.

"The laser fixed my reading vision too." Monovision and multifocal ablation patterns exist, but they trade distance clarity or contrast for near function. Many patients who had excellent distance vision after surgery still reach for readers at 45, because the add they need comes from the lens inside the eye, not the cornea.

"Any optometrist can measure me the same way." Post-surgical refraction is harder than routine refraction: the cornea is oblate with a transition zone, the autorefractor's assumptions are less reliable, and the endpoint depends on tear film quality at the moment of testing. Tell anyone who tests you that you had surgery, and which type — flap, surface ablation or lenticule extraction all leave different signatures.

What a pair of glasses can and cannot do after surgery

Can: correct residual sphere and cylinder; correct the anisometropia that appears when one eye lands at plano and the other at −0.75; provide the reading add you now need; and provide a predictable, stable image for driving at night if the residual error is the dominant problem.

Cannot: restore the contrast the ablation removed; remove starbursts caused by fourth-order aberration; compensate for a tear film that breaks up between blinks; or replace the corneal front surface, which is what a rigid lens does and a spectacle lens does not.

One practical caveat: the cornea after surgery is thinner and its front surface is no longer the shape the eye's total power was built around — the cornea still supplies roughly 43 of the eye's 60 dioptres, but distributed differently. That is why your prescription may look modest while your vision behaves like a much stronger error, and why a careful refraction, done with a trial frame and enough time, is worth more than a fast automated reading.

When to stop adjusting the glasses and see someone

If vision is still fluctuating at three months, if one eye is consistently worse, if halos are worsening rather than settling, or if you have pain, light sensitivity or a sudden change, that is not a prescription question. Those need a clinical assessment of the cornea and ocular surface. Glasses suit a stable residual error; they do not fix an eye that is still changing.
