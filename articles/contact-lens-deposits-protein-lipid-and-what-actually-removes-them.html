Contact Lens Deposits: Protein, Lipid, and What Actually Removes Them

October 24, 2026 · 8 min read

What is actually on the lens after a month

A contact lens is the only medical device designed to sit in a fluid that is actively trying to coat it. Tears carry protein, lipid and mucin, and every one of those components has some affinity for a polymer surface. Within days of first wear, a new lens acquires a film that was not there when it left the package, and the film changes how the lens behaves: how it wets, how it feels, how much oxygen it transmits, and eventually how the eyelid reacts to it.

The two deposits that matter

Protein. Lysozyme is the most abundant protein in tears, and it is the classic contact lens deposit. It adsorbs onto hydrogel surfaces, and once adsorbed it can denature, which makes it harder to remove because the unfolded protein binds more tightly than the native molecule. Lactoferrin behaves in a similar way. Protein deposits are associated with reduced wettability and with the papillary reaction known as giant papillary conjunctivitis.

Lipid. Meibomian glands secrete lipid that forms the outer layer of the tear film. Lipid deposits are greasy rather than crusty, and they matter more for silicone hydrogel lenses, whose hydrophobic silicone domains attract lipid in a way that conventional hydrogels do not. This is the trade-off that gets lost in marketing: the material that solved the oxygen problem created a lipid problem.

The standards framework here is ISO 18369, the contact lens series; ISO 18369-4:2017 covers the physicochemical properties of contact lens materials, which is the vocabulary in which wettability, water content and related surface behaviour are described.

What removes them, and what does not

  • Surfactant multipurpose solutions plus rubbing. The mechanical action of rubbing a lens in the palm remains the most effective step, and it is the step most often skipped. No solution compensates for a lens that is only soaked.
  • Hydrogen peroxide systems. These rely on a platinum disc to neutralise the peroxide, and they are effective cleaners as well as disinfectants. They require the case to be used correctly and the disc to be replaced.
  • Daily disposables. The most reliable deposit strategy is not a cleaner at all. If the lens never ages, it never accumulates.
  • Enzyme tablets. Once a routine answer to protein, they became largely unnecessary for frequent-replacement soft lenses; the modern equivalent is a scheduled replacement, not a weekly tablet.

Why the schedule matters more than the bottle

Deposit formation is a race between accumulation and removal, and the removal that matters most is replacement. A monthly lens worn for five weeks has had thirty-five per cent more time to accumulate protein than the schedule assumes, and the extra week is not evenly distributed across the surface: deposits concentrate where the lens is thinnest and where tear exchange is lowest, which is usually under the upper lid.

Case hygiene belongs in the same conversation. A case that is rinsed but never dried, or topped up rather than emptied, becomes a biofilm reservoir that re-inoculates every lens stored in it. The standard advice — empty, rub, rinse, air-dry face down, replace every few months — is unglamorous and it is the difference between a care system that works and one that merely smells clean.

Material choice interacts with all of this. A lens that suits your eye's oxygen needs but binds lipid heavily will perform worse on a two-week schedule than a lens that is slightly less breathable and far less greasy. That trade-off is worth discussing explicitly at a fitting rather than discovering at week three.

The daily-disposable trade-off

Daily lenses are not automatically the right answer for everyone. They remove the deposit variable, but they also fix the material and the fit to whatever the daily range offers, and a wearer whose eye needs a specific curvature or a specific oxygen transmissibility may be better served by a two-weekly lens that fits properly. The question to ask is not which modality sounds cleanest, but which combination of material, fit and replacement the eye actually tolerates. A lens that fits badly accumulates deposits faster, because a loose lens has poor tear exchange under it and a tight lens traps debris at the limbus.

Misconceptions

  • "If it feels fine, the lens is clean." Deposit buildup is gradual and the cornea adapts. Discomfort arrives after the surface has already changed.
  • "Higher water content keeps deposits away." Water content and deposit affinity are separate properties, and a high-water hydrogel can bind protein as readily as a lower-water one.
  • "Saline is enough for storage." Saline does not disinfect and does not remove lipid. It is a rinse, not a care system.
  • "A deposit is only a cosmetic problem." Deposits reduce wettability, can reduce oxygen transmission, and are associated with inflammatory responses in the lid. They are a clinical issue before they are a comfort issue.

When to stop self-managing

Stop wearing and seek advice if the eye becomes red, if the lid develops a gritty or itchy reaction, if vision fluctuates between blinks in a way that has become persistent, or if a lens that used to be comfortable becomes uncomfortable within hours of insertion. Those patterns suggest either a deposit problem, a fit problem or an inflammatory response, and the three are distinguished by examination rather than by trying a different solution. Any pain, light sensitivity or change in vision with a lens in place is a reason to remove it and be seen the same day.
