Aniseikonia: When the Two Eyes See Different Sizes

October 16, 2026 · 8 min read

Two different problems that get confused

When the two eyes do not match, the mismatch can be one of two quite different things, and the distinction matters because they are fixed in different ways.

Anisometropia is a difference in power. One eye needs minus four dioptres and the other needs minus one, so the two lenses in the pair are simply not the same strength. Aniseikonia is a difference in image size: the two eyes receive images that are scaled differently, so the same object arrives larger to one eye than to the other.

The two often travel together, because one of the most common causes of unequal image size is correcting an unequal power with spectacles. But they are not the same thing, and they do not have the same fix.

Where an unequal image size comes from

A spectacle lens does not merely bend light to a focus; it also scales what you see. The scaling has two parts, and both can differ between the two eyes.

The power factor depends on the lens power and on how far the lens sits in front of the eye. It is written as 1 / (1 - dF), where d is the back vertex distance in metres and F is the power in dioptres. A higher power, or a lens placed further forward, changes the scale.

The shape factor depends on the curvature of the lens itself rather than its position, and it is the reason two lenses of identical power can still scale the world differently if they are made on different base curves or in different thicknesses.

Because the power factor is the one that changes most between a strong and a weak prescription, an anisometropic pair usually produces aniseikonia. There is a second route as well: if one lens of a pair sits further from its eye than the other, the same power produces a different scale on that side.

The arithmetic, with one worked example

Suppose the right eye is -2.00 D and the left is -6.00 D, and both lenses sit 14 mm from the eye, so d = 0.014.

  • Right: 1 / (1 - 0.014 x -2.00) = 1 / 1.028 = 0.973
  • Left: 1 / (1 - 0.014 x -6.00) = 1 / 1.084 = 0.923

Both images are smaller than life, but they are not smaller by the same amount. Dividing the two factors shows that the right eye receives an image about 5 per cent larger than the left. That gap, not the prescription difference itself, is what the visual system has to cope with.

Why four per cent is the number people quote

Aniseikonia is measured as a percentage difference in perceived size. The figure usually cited as the point where the difference becomes clinically significant is about four per cent, and smaller gaps are often tolerated because the brain can fuse two slightly different sizes without complaint.

That threshold is not a rule with a hard edge. Tolerating an unequal image is work, and how much work a particular person can absorb without noticing varies with their binocular vision, with how much near work they do, and with how long they spend in the pair.

What it feels like

Aniseikonia does not usually announce itself as double vision. Two images of different sizes can still be fused, so the symptom is the cost of fusing them: eye strain that builds through the day, frontal headaches, difficulty holding a line of print, a sense that the page is somehow unstable, and trouble with tasks that depend on precise binocular judgement, such as threading a needle, parking, or judging a step.

Some people describe a mild pulling sensation, or report that one eye seems to be doing more of the work. Others notice nothing until the prescription changes and a previously tolerable gap becomes a new one.

What can actually be done about it

The key insight is that aniseikonia is a property of the correction, not a defect in the eye. Change the correction and the size difference changes with it.

  • Contact lenses. A contact lens sits essentially on the eye, so the distance term in the power factor almost vanishes. Correcting a very unequal prescription with contact lenses removes most of the size difference.
  • Iseikonic and modified lens designs. Lenses can be chosen or designed to equalise relative spectacle magnification between the two eyes, usually by adjusting base curves and thicknesses rather than by changing the power.
  • Vertex distance and frame choice. Since the power factor depends on how far the lens sits from the eye, changes in vertex distance, and differences between the two sides of a frame, are worth checking before blaming the prescription.
  • Accepting a smaller gap. Where the difference is modest and the wearer is comfortable, doing nothing is a legitimate outcome.

Common misconceptions

  • "Aniseikonia means my two eyes disagree about the prescription." No. The prescriptions can be perfectly correct and still produce unequal image sizes, because the size difference comes from the correction, not from a measurement error.
  • "If the prescription is right, both eyes must see the same size." The opposite is closer to the truth: an unequal prescription that is correctly made will produce an unequal image scale in spectacles.
  • "Both eyes always see the same size, so any difference I imagine is not real." Unequal image size is measurable, and it is a normal consequence of an unequal or asymmetrically fitted correction.
  • "The eye with the bigger image is the better eye." Image size says nothing about retinal health or visual quality. A larger image is not a sharper one.
  • "Wearing the glasses more will fix it." Adaptation can reduce how much the difference is noticed, but it does not change the optics, and effort spent fusing unequal images is effort that shows up as fatigue.

When to raise it with a professional

Mention it if a new pair produces persistent strain, headaches, or a sense that one side of your vision is scaled differently, particularly if your two eyes need noticeably different powers. The useful information to bring is concrete: which tasks provoke it, whether it started with the new pair, and whether contact lenses feel different from spectacles. Ask specifically about the size difference between the two eyes, not only whether the prescriptions are correct. That is the question that leads to the answer.
