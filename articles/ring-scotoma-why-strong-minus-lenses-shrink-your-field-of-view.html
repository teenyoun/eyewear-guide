Ring Scotoma: Why Strong Minus Lenses Shrink Your Field of View

October 28, 2026 · 9 min read

The band at the edge that is not there

People moving from a mild prescription to something in the region of -8.00 D often describe the same first week: the centre of the world is sharp, but a band near the rim of the frame is empty, and objects beyond that band appear suddenly when they are almost beside them. This is a ring scotoma. It is not a manufacturing fault, not a sign the prescription is wrong, and not something that wearing the glasses harder will fix: it comes from the optics of the lens, not the eye.

A ring scotoma is a blind annulus in the peripheral field of a high-minus spectacle lens. It exists because a spectacle lens has a finite aperture and because the prismatic power of that lens is not constant across the aperture.

Prentice's rule: prism grows with distance from the optical centre

The prismatic effect at any point on a lens is given by Prentice's rule: prism dioptres equal the distance from the optical centre in centimetres multiplied by the power of the lens in dioptres. The relationship is linear, and that linearity is the whole problem. Twice the distance gives twice the prism; twice the power gives twice the prism again.

Take two lenses and look at the same 30 mm from the optical centre. A -2.00 D lens carries 3.0 x 2.00 = 6 prism dioptres there. A -10.00 D lens carries 3.0 x 10.00 = 30 prism dioptres. One prism dioptre displaces an image by one centimetre at one metre, so 30 prism dioptres means the peripheral image is displaced by roughly 30 cm for every metre of viewing distance. That is a doorway width. The periphery of a strong minus lens is not a slightly distorted version of the centre; it is a different view of the world.

Why the shift becomes a hole

The eye is not fixed behind the optical centre. It rotates, and as it rotates the pupil sits behind progressively more peripheral parts of the lens. The rate of change is the number that matters: because the prism slope is the lens power, a -10.00 D lens adds one dioptre of prism for every single millimetre the line of sight moves off centre. At -2.00 D the same movement adds a fifth of a dioptre. The pupil is only about three millimetres wide in daylight, so the bundle of rays it accepts is narrow, and a slope of one dioptre per millimetre is enough to swing that bundle well outside the pupil across a short run of eccentricity.

The result is a mapping from object direction to retinal position that grows steeply with eccentricity and then breaks at the lens edge. Any light arriving from a narrow band of directions is deviated so far that it misses the entrance pupil entirely: no rays, no image, and therefore no signal. That band is the ring scotoma. It sits between the usable central field and the sliver of the world that reappears beyond the rim.

The magnification sign explains a counter-intuitive detail. A minus lens minifies, pulling a wider angle of the world into the frame, so the total field through a -10.00 D lens can look broader than through a -1.00 D lens even though an annulus of it is missing. Wearers describe a scotoma less as tunnel vision than as sharp, absent, sharp again at the very edge.

Jack-in-the-box

The second half of the phenomenon is dynamic, and it is what people actually complain about. What the wearer perceives is not the absolute prism at a point but the change in prism between two points, because the eye compares the image before and after it moves. At one dioptre per millimetre, an object crossing the temporal field is displaced by several dioptres between one glance and the next: it leaps, vanishes, then reappears somewhere it should not be. On stairs and kerbs that is a hazard, not an annoyance.

What actually changes the size of the ring

  • Frame eye size. The smaller the lens aperture, the closer the rim sits to the optical centre, and the narrower the band of extreme prism. Reducing eye size from a large fashion frame to a compact one removes real millimetres of edge prism per side, and it is the strongest frame-level lever.
  • Vertex distance. Holding the lens further from the eye widens the ring and strengthens the minification. A frame that sits close to the face is optically kinder at high power, provided lashes and cheeks allow it.
  • Lens index. A 1.74 lens is thinner and lighter, and it leaves the edge prism completely unchanged, because prism depends on power and distance from the optical centre, not on how much material sits between the surfaces.
  • Surface design. Aspheric and free-form surfacing reduce off-axis aberration and flatten the curve, which improves the quality of what is left of the peripheral image. They do not remove the underlying deviation.
  • Contact lenses. A lens that moves with the eye has no fixed optical centre offset from the line of sight, so a soft or rigid lens removes the ring almost entirely. At very high myopia this is often the strongest optical argument for contacts.

The common misconception: thinner lenses, wider field

Marketing has taught buyers that a high index is the answer to a strong prescription. For weight and edge cosmetics that is true. For field of view it is a distraction: index changes how thick the lens becomes at a given power, not how much it deviates light at a given eccentricity, and the deviation is the thing creating the scotoma. A second, related error is the assumption that a smaller frame is always better. Shrinking the aperture does reduce edge prism, but it also cuts the physical window, and past a point the frame opening is narrower than the wearer's usable field, which trades a ring for a keyhole. The right frame is the one whose rim falls inside the limits of the wearer's natural field, not the smallest frame that fits.

A third misconception is that the effect proves the prescription is too strong. A -8.00 D lens produces this geometry because the eye needs -8.00 D; a weaker lens would simply be blurry. The scotoma is what correcting a high error with a lens in front of a rotating eye looks like.

When to have it looked at professionally

Have the glasses and the eyes checked if the field loss appears in an unchanged prescription, if it persists with the glasses off, if it respects the horizontal midline, if you see two images with one eye, or if it comes with new flashes or floaters. Those patterns point at the eye or the fit rather than at the lens design. Otherwise expect the ring to become less intrusive over two to three weeks as the wearer learns to turn the head rather than the eyes, and expect it not to disappear entirely.
