Striae, Orange Peel and Colour Bands: Reading a Coating Defect

November 05, 2026 · 9 min read

Most complaints about a lens surface arrive with the wrong diagnosis attached. A faint wave pattern is reported as a scratch. A patch of green in one corner is reported as peeling. Each is a different phenomenon with a different cause, and the distinction decides whether the lens should be replaced, cleaned differently, or simply accepted.

A surface defect is a signature of a process

An anti-reflective stack is built by depositing several very thin layers of different refractive index onto the lens, usually by vacuum deposition for the optical layers and by spin or dip coating for the hard coat beneath them. Each layer's job is defined by two numbers: its refractive index and its optical thickness. Nothing about the finished look is accidental, so anything that deviates from the design points back to a step in that process.

The physics that makes colour a measurement

A coated lens is not colourless for the same reason a soap bubble is not colourless. Light reflects from the top of each thin layer and from the boundary beneath it. Those two reflections travel different distances, and when they recombine they reinforce or cancel depending on wavelength. Cancellation is most complete when the round trip through the layer equals half a wavelength, which is why optical coatings are designed at a quarter-wave optical thickness: physical thickness multiplied by refractive index equals one quarter of the design wavelength.

Put numbers on it. Magnesium fluoride, a classic single-layer material, has a refractive index of about 1.38. For a design wavelength of 550 nanometres, the quarter-wave thickness is 550 divided by four times 1.38, roughly 100 nanometres. That is a ten-thousandth of the thickness of the lens, and about a fifth of a visible wavelength. Which is why interference is such a sensitive gauge: a local variation of a few tens of nanometres moves the cancelled wavelength far enough to change the colour you see.

The same sensitivity explains the residual tint: a stack of four to seven layers cannot cancel every wavelength at once, so the designer leaves a characteristic residual reflection colour, usually green, purple or blue-green.

Three defect families, three different causes

  • Thickness error shows as colour bands, blotches or a gradient across the lens. The stack is intact and the chemistry worked; the layer is simply thicker in one region.
  • Topography error shows as orange peel: fine regular dimpling under a point light. The average thickness is right but the surface is rough on a sub-micron scale, so it scatters instead of reflecting cleanly.
  • Flow error shows as striae: faint, roughly parallel wave lines along the direction the coating or solvent front travelled.

Striae: what a flow line actually is

In a wet coating process the film dries from the outside in. Solvent leaves the surface, the remaining liquid becomes more viscous, and the boundary between wet and drying material sweeps across the lens. If that front advances unevenly, or the film is too thick for its viscosity, the front leaves a shallow ridge behind it. The result is a set of evenly spaced undulations that catch light at a grazing angle and vanish when you look straight through the lens.

Striae are almost always cosmetic. They are not scratches, not cracks, and they do not propagate. The practical test is that they appear only in reflected light and do not scatter a bright point source in transmission. If they do scatter, the surface is rough enough to matter optically.

Orange peel: roughness you can measure

Orange peel comes from a coating that failed to level before it set. Surface tension pulls a wet film flat, but only if it has time and low enough viscosity. Dry it too fast, or apply it over contamination that pins the contact line, and the film freezes with a dimpled surface. The optical consequence is haze: light that should pass straight through is scattered into a small forward cone. A little haze is invisible in a bright room and obvious at night, because a headlight spreads into a halo.

Haze is a standard measured quantity assessed with a haze meter, which is why a lab can put a number on a complaint a wearer can only describe as "not quite clear".

Colour bands are not the same as stress rainbows

Lenses also show colour for an unrelated reason. Optical plastics are birefringent under mechanical stress: polymer chains align slightly, the refractive index becomes direction-dependent, and polarised light separates into two paths that recombine as colour. That pattern maps stress, typically strongest near a drill hole, a rimless mounting or a tight eyewire. Rotate the lens between two polarising filters and the pattern shifts and changes. Coating interference does not do that. It stays put under white light, and instead of rotating with a filter it moves as you change the viewing angle, because the path length through the layer changes with the angle of incidence.

That single test, rotate a polarising filter in front of the lens, separates the two explanations without any equipment beyond a laptop screen.

The claims worth correcting

  • "A green reflex means a cheap coating." Every finite stack leaves a residual colour somewhere. Green is common because the eye is most sensitive there, making it the region designers most want to suppress.
  • "A rainbow means the coating is peeling." Delamination shows a hard edge where a layer lifts, often at the rim, with a step you can feel with a fingernail. Interference colour has no edge.
  • "You can polish a coating defect out." Polishing removes the layer. What is left is bare substrate, and the lens will reflect more than before the attempt.
  • "A thicker coating is a tougher coating." Layer thickness is an optical parameter fixed by the design wavelength. Durability comes from the hard coat, the adhesion between layers and the cure.

How to document it before you ask for a replacement

Photographs taken with a flash on axis destroy the evidence. Put the lens on a dark surface, hold a small light to one side, and shoot at a grazing angle so the surface is lit rather than reflected. Note whether the pattern is visible in transmission or only in reflection. A lab can act on that description; it cannot act on "there is something on the lens".

Return the lens if a defect is visible in transmission, if haze is noticeable against a dark background or while driving at night, if there is a step at the edge that catches a fingernail, or if the pattern appeared after cleaning rather than being present from new. A faint striation visible only under a desk lamp at the right angle is a normal manufacturing signature, and replacing it usually produces something similar.

When the symptom is not the surface at all

Grainy vision, haloes around lights and a persistent film that survives careful cleaning can also come from a degraded hard coat, from a tear film that is not smoothing the front surface, or from the eye itself. If a new pair is clear and an old pair with no visible surface defect is not, the useful next step is an eye examination.
