Multifocal IOL Halos: Why the Rings Around Lights Are Built Into the Design

November 26, 2026 · 9 min read

The ring around the streetlight

People who receive a diffractive multifocal intraocular lens describe the same thing in almost the same words. The light itself is sharp enough to read a sign by, but every light source has grown a ring: a thin halo sitting at a fixed distance from the bulb, often with a fainter second ring outside it. Phone screens at night are described as glowing. Reading a menu in a dim restaurant is described as flat. None of this means the eye is damaged. It is what happens when a lens is designed to put light in two places at once.

The principle: a diffractive lens divides light instead of focusing it

A conventional spectacle lens, or a monofocal implant, takes the light that enters it and bends all of it toward one focal point. A diffractive multifocal implant does something structurally different. Its surface carries a series of concentric microscopic steps, each only a few micrometres high. Each step is a phase discontinuity, and light crossing it is diffracted into several orders rather than a single converging wavefront.

The step height is chosen so that most of the energy lands in two orders: one converging at the distance focus, one converging at a nearer focus. In a two-focus design the split is commonly quoted as roughly 40% of the light to distance and 40% to near, with the remaining energy distributed into higher orders that never come to a focus at all. Designs differ, and some aim at an even 50/50 split, but the structure of the trade is always the same: energy that is not used for either image has to go somewhere.

That somewhere is the halo. The unused light forms a defocused image of the pupil on the retina. Because it is the pupil being imaged, the halo is a disc rather than a point, and its angular size scales with how wide the pupil is. In bright light the pupil is small and the halo collapses toward the light source. At night the pupil dilates, and the same energy spreads into a ring wide enough to overlap the road ahead. The halo is not a defect added to the design; it is the design's arithmetic.

Simultaneous vision and the second cost

Both images, distance and near, reach the retina at the same moment. This is called simultaneous vision, and it is not the same as the alternating vision of a bifocal spectacle lens, where the direction of gaze selects the zone. With an implant there is no gaze direction to select. The split is fixed by the lens geometry, in every direction of gaze, at every moment.

The consequence is a loss of retinal image contrast. Every object is seen through a veil made of its own out-of-focus twin. The visual system is good at suppressing one image once the task tells it which one matters, but suppression takes time and is incomplete in low light, where the out-of-focus contribution grows as the pupil opens. This is why the complaints cluster around night driving, dim restaurants and small print under a lamp that is not quite bright enough: situations where contrast was already marginal.

It is worth separating what is measured from what is experienced. Optical bench testing of implant optics is standardised in ISO 11979-2, which defines how image quality is measured through specified apertures and spatial frequencies. Those bench numbers are real, but they describe a lens in a holder, not a person at dusk. Two eyes with identical bench figures can report very different symptoms, because pupil size, residual refractive error and neural adaptation all sit between the measurement and the complaint.

The practical consequences, in rough order of importance

  • Halos and glare at night. Expected, inherent to the design, and usually worst in the first weeks. They typically diminish as the neural system adapts, but they rarely vanish completely; most clinicians describe a residual ring that becomes ignorable rather than absent.
  • Reduced contrast in low light. More consequential than the halo itself for driving, because it affects detection of unlit obstacles rather than the appearance of lit ones.
  • Dependence on light level for near tasks. The near image is a real image, not a magnified blur, but it needs enough light to compete with the distance image.
  • Residual refractive error. Half a dioptre of uncorrected cylinder sits on top of the diffractive design and degrades both images at once. Refraction after implant surgery is properly measured once the eye has settled, commonly around four to six weeks, not in the first days when corneal swelling and wound healing are still moving the numbers.

How this differs from a progressive spectacle lens

The comparison is instructive because the failure modes are opposite. A progressive spectacle lens sends essentially all of the light that enters it to the retina, but through zones of unwanted astigmatism that grow away from the corridor, the aberration described by Minkwitz's theorem. Its cost is spatial: clear in the corridor, distorted at the edges. The cost of a diffractive implant is photometric: the same clarity in every direction of gaze, but a fixed fraction of light permanently out of focus.

That is why the two devices ask different things of the wearer, and why a person who never adapted to progressives can still do well with an implant.

Claims that do not quite hold

  • "The ring means the lens is off-centre." Centration does matter: a displaced diffractive optic induces coma and makes the halo asymmetric rather than concentric. But a perfectly centred lens still produces rings. A halo by itself is not evidence of a surgical problem.
  • "Extended depth-of-focus lenses have no halos." Extended depth-of-focus designs stretch one focal region rather than creating two discrete foci, and they generally reduce dysphotopsia. Reduced is not absent, and bench differences between designs do not translate into a guarantee for a particular eye.
  • "New glasses will remove the halos." Spectacles correct residual sphere and cylinder. They cannot recombine diffraction orders, and they do not change how the pupil sets the halo diameter.
  • "The ring is a reflection off the lens surface." Internal reflections at the edge of a square-edged implant are a real phenomenon, but they look different: localised arcs or a crescent near the periphery of the field, not a concentric ring around every light. The ring is out-of-focus energy.

When to stop reading and get seen

Most dysphotopsia is a conversation, not an emergency. Some presentations are emergencies. Seek same-day assessment for a sudden shower of new floaters, flashes, or a curtain across the vision; for pain, marked redness or light sensitivity, particularly in the weeks after surgery; or for a sudden drop in vision. Those are not halo problems and should not be managed by waiting to adapt.

For persistent, intolerable halos beyond the first few months, the useful next step is a refraction and a dilated examination, not a new pair of glasses bought online. Some residual error is correctable; some dysphotopsia is design-inherent and is managed by changing the light around the task. None of this is a diagnosis, and none of it replaces an examination by the clinician who knows the eye.
