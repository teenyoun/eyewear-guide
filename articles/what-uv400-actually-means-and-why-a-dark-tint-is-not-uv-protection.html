What UV400 Actually Means, and Why a Dark Tint Is Not UV Protection

October 02, 2026 · 8 min read

The label on the lens is a number, not a complement

"UV400" and "100% UV protection" get used as if they were grades of quality. They are not. UV400 is a specific technical claim about wavelength: it states that the lens blocks ultraviolet radiation at wavelengths up to 400 nanometres. Whether the lens is dark or almost clear, expensive or cheap, has nothing to do with whether that claim is true.

The ultraviolet bands

Ultraviolet is conventionally divided by wavelength:

  • UVA — about 315 to 400 nm. Reaches the eye in quantity; associated with long-term lens and retinal effects.
  • UVB — about 280 to 315 nm. Largely absorbed by the cornea and lens, and the main driver of sunburn on the eye surface.
  • UVC — about 100 to 280 nm. Effectively absorbed by the atmosphere and does not reach the ground in significant amounts.

So "UV400" means the lens covers the whole UVA band and all of UVB. A lens marked UV380 leaves 380–400 nm unattenuated, which is the far end of UVA. Both may be described as "UV protection" in marketing copy, which is exactly why the number matters and the phrase does not.

How much gets through

A well-made UV400 lens blocks more than 99% of ultraviolet across that range. That is achievable in clear lenses as well as tinted ones, and it comes from UV-absorbing compounds built into the lens material rather than from tint. This is worth stating plainly, because it is the single most counter-intuitive fact in eyewear: darkness and UV blocking are entirely separate properties.

Why a dark lens without UV blocking is worse than no lens

This is the mechanism that makes the cheap unlabelled pair in a market stall a genuine risk rather than merely a bad purchase.

Visible light controls the size of your pupil. In bright conditions behind a dark lens, less visible light reaches the eye, so the pupil widens. A wider pupil admits more of everything that passes through the lens — including ultraviolet. So a lens that filters visible light but not UV can deliver more ultraviolet to the lens and retina than wearing nothing at all, because it has both opened the aperture and left the UV path clear.

That is the whole argument for choosing on the UV400 marking rather than on tint darkness, on price, or on the colour of the lens.

The standards that back the claim up

A printed claim is worth what the testing behind it is worth. Two markings tell you a lens has been tested against a recognised standard:

  • EN ISO 12312-1 — the European standard for sunglasses lenses, associated with the CE marking.
  • ANSI Z80.3 — the corresponding US standard.

These standards also cover transmittance categories, optical quality and impact requirements. Note that sunglasses standards are not safety-eyewear standards: for impact-rated protective eyewear the relevant markings are ANSI Z87.1 or EN 166, which test something different. A fashion pair meeting Z80.3 is not a substitute for rated safety glasses.

The myth: darker means more protection

It is the most persistent idea in sunglasses, and it is simply wrong. Dark tint reduces how much visible light reaches the eye. It says nothing about ultraviolet. You can buy an almost-black lens with no UV rating at all, and you can buy a nearly clear lens that blocks everything a UV400 lens should.

Two related myths are worth retiring at the same time:

  • "Polarised means UV protected." Polarisation is a glare-control property: it blocks light vibrating in one plane. It is not a statement about ultraviolet at all. Many polarised lenses are also UV400, but that is a separate specification you should still look for.
  • "Expensive lenses protect better." UV400 is cheap to achieve. Price buys optical quality, coatings, impact resistance and finish — not the UV figure, which should be present at any price.

Practical checks

  • Look for the number. "UV400" or a specific wavelength claim beats "UV protection", "blocks harmful rays", or any adjective without a figure.
  • Judge on the marking, not the darkness. The two are unrelated properties.
  • Check children's and sports glasses specifically. Time outdoors is a major factor in cumulative UV exposure, and children's eyes transmit more UV than adults'.
  • Do not trust a home UV test on tint. Apparent darkness under a UV lamp tells you about visible transmission, not the 315–400 nm band.
  • Clear lenses can be UV400 too. If you want UV protection without darkening, a clear lens with a UV400 claim does the job.

When to see an optician or optometrist

Protection decisions are not medical ones, but discuss your UV exposure with an eye care professional if you have had cataract surgery or certain retinal conditions, take medicines that increase photosensitivity, work outdoors for long hours, or have a family history of eye disease. They can tell you what level of protection is appropriate for your situation rather than leaving it to a label interpretation.

UV400 means one specific thing: no ultraviolet below 400 nanometres gets through in meaningful quantity. It has nothing to do with how dark the lens is, how much it cost, or whether it is polarised — and the danger case is the opposite of the intuition, because a dark lens without UV blocking can leave your eyes more exposed than no sunglasses at all.
