Cycloplegic Refraction: Why Children's Prescriptions Are Measured Through Drops

November 09, 2026 · 9 min read

The problem: a child's eye can hide its own error

Accommodation is the eye's autofocus. The ciliary muscle contracts, the crystalline lens becomes rounder, and optical power increases so that near objects come into focus. A young eye has an enormous range of this adjustment. Using Hofstetter's commonly quoted average, maximum amplitude of accommodation is roughly 18.5 minus 0.3 times age in dioptres, which puts a ten-year-old near 15.5 D and a six-year-old near 16.7 D. Even at half that range in ordinary use, the reserve is large.

That reserve is what makes refracting a child difficult without help. A hyperopic child — long-sighted, with too little power for the eye's length — sees a blurred image and simply accommodates until it clears. The accommodation adds plus power that was not in the prescription, so a measurement taken while the child is accommodating reads less hyperopia than the eye actually has. The difference between the two readings is called latent hyperopia, and it is not a small technicality: a child who needs +4.00 D but measures +1.50 D will be given glasses that are comfortable at first and inadequate in the long run, and the residual effort can drive accommodative esotropia, where the eyes turn inward as a by-product of focusing hard.

What the drops do, mechanically

Cycloplegic agents are antimuscarinic drugs. They block muscarinic receptors on the ciliary muscle, so the muscle cannot contract and accommodation is suspended — that is cycloplegia. The same class of receptor sits on the sphincter of the iris, so the pupil also dilates — mydriasis — because the dilator muscle is left unopposed. One drug, two effects, and both of them are visible to the parent within half an hour: a wide pupil and a child who cannot read a book.

Different agents are chosen for different depths of paralysis and different durations. Tropicamide is the shortest-acting and the least complete; cyclopentolate sits in the middle; atropine is the most complete and by far the longest, sometimes used where full paralysis matters more than convenience. The choice depends on the child's age, the reason for the examination, and how much residual accommodation would spoil the result. This is a clinical decision, not a preference.

Where the numbers come from

Once accommodation is suspended, retinoscopy or an autorefractor reading reflects the eye's resting state rather than its effort. That is when the finding becomes a prescription instead of an estimate. The distinction is worth holding onto: the pre-drop measurement is a manifest refraction, the post-drop measurement adds the latent component, and both describe the same eye at different levels of effort.

A related phenomenon shows up in older children and teenagers. Long periods of close work can leave the ciliary muscle in a sustained state of contraction, producing a myopic reading that partly disappears once the muscle is paralysed. That is pseudomyopia — a refractive appearance rather than a structural change. It matters because a prescription written from the manifest reading alone can be stronger than the eye needs, and because the distinction between pseudomyopia and true myopia changes what happens next.

The misconceptions worth correcting

"The drops are just to keep the child still." They do not sedate or calm anyone. They paralyse a muscle. The child is fully alert and usually annoyed that the page in front of them has gone blurry.

"The drops are a test for eye disease." Pupil dilation is used for looking at the retina, and the same visit may include that, but the cycloplegic refraction itself is a measurement of optical power, not a screening test for pathology.

"The bigger number means the first test was wrong." A larger hyperopic figure after drops is the expected result, not a contradiction. The first measurement was the eye's effort; the second is the eye at rest. Both were accurate descriptions of different states.

"The drops will damage the eye." Cycloplegia is temporary and the effects wear off as the drug is cleared. The realistic concerns are practical: several hours of blurred near vision, pronounced light sensitivity, and rare systemic reactions that parents should know about before leaving the clinic.

Afterwards: what the next few hours look like

Near vision is the first thing to go, so homework, screens and small toys are out for the rest of the day. Bright light is uncomfortable because the pupil cannot constrict, so a hat and sunglasses help on the way home. Rubbing should be discouraged. The specific duration depends on the agent used — hours for the short-acting drops, considerably longer for atropine — and the clinic should say which one was instilled and when normal function is expected to return. If a child becomes flushed, feverish, unusually agitated or drowsy after the drops, that is a reason to contact the clinic rather than to wait it out.

When cycloplegic refraction changes the plan

The cases where this examination earns its inconvenience are fairly specific: a first prescription in a preschool child, a large difference between the two eyes, a turned eye that appears when the child looks at something close, a strong family history of high hyperopia or of myopia starting early, and a manifest result that does not explain the symptoms. In each of those, the resting refractive state is the number the prescription has to be built on.

What no article can do is decide whether your child needs the drops. That depends on age, cooperation, the reason for the visit and what the examiner needs to see — and it is a conversation to have with the person who will be writing the prescription, ideally before the appointment rather than in the chair.
