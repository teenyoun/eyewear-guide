Decentration and Minimum Blank Size: How a Lens Is Fitted to Your Frame

October 08, 2026 · 9 min read

The invisible step between your prescription and your lenses

You hand over a prescription with two numbers for each eye. Some days later you collect a pair of glasses. In between, a set of geometric decisions was made that never appear on the prescription and are rarely discussed at the counter — decisions that determine whether the optical centre of each lens actually lands in front of your pupil, how thick the edges end up, and whether the lens could even be cut to fit the frame you chose.

The two concepts at the centre of that process are decentration and minimum blank size. Understanding them explains a lot of otherwise mysterious outcomes, including why one frame costs far more than another with an identical prescription.

The principle layer: the optical centre must move to the pupil

A finished lens is manufactured as a round blank whose optical centre sits at its geometric centre. In a perfectly symmetric world, you would cut the lens so that this optical centre ends up exactly in front of the pupil, and everything would be simple.

Real frames are not symmetric. The distance between the two lens apertures — the frame pupillary distance — is a property of the frame, and it rarely equals your own pupillary distance. So the optical centre has to be shifted sideways, inwards or outwards, to line up with your eye. That shift is decentration, and it is a deliberate, calculated operation, not an accident of cutting.

The arithmetic is straightforward once you have the numbers:

  • Frame PD = A + DBL, where A is the horizontal width of one lens aperture and DBL is the bridge width (the distance between lenses). Both are usually stamped on the inside of the temple.
  • Decentration per lens = (Frame PD − your PD) ÷ 2.

Work an example. A frame marked 52–18 has a frame PD of 70 mm. If your own pupillary distance is 64 mm, the difference is 6 mm, so each lens must be decentred by 3 mm — inwards, because the frame is wider than your face.

Why blank size decides what is possible

Decentring a lens means moving the optical centre away from the blank's centre. That movement consumes material, and how much material is available is set by the blank's diameter. The controlling dimension is the ED — the effective diameter, meaning the longest straight line across the lens aperture as it will be cut, which for many shapes is a diagonal rather than the horizontal width.

A standard working rule for the smallest blank that will do the job is:

  • Minimum blank size = ED + (2 × decentration) + 2 mm

The trailing 2 mm is allowance for the edging process itself. Continue the example: if the ED for that frame is 54 mm and decentration is 3 mm per lens, the minimum blank is 54 + 6 + 2 = 62 mm. A 65 mm blank will do it comfortably. A 60 mm blank simply will not, no matter what the prescription says, and the lens would have to be made specially.

This is the mechanical reason that a very wide frame on a narrow face, or a very wide frame combined with a high prescription, can become unexpectedly expensive. The geometry demands a bigger blank, and bigger high-index blanks cost more.

How this connects to thickness

Decentration and thickness are directly linked, because moving the optical centre towards the edge of the blank means the cut lens includes more of the region where the surface curves away. For a minus lens, that is more edge thickness; for a plus lens, more centre thickness. Two people with identical prescriptions can therefore collect noticeably different-looking glasses purely because of frame choice and PD — and the person with the wider frame is usually the one with the thicker edge.

Common misconceptions

"Smaller frames mean thinner lenses, so always choose small." Smaller apertures do generally reduce edge thickness for minus prescriptions, but they can also reduce the field of view and make the frame uncomfortable. There is a balance point, and it depends on your PD as well as your power.

"The lab can always cut any lens into any frame." If the minimum blank size exceeds what is available in that material and index, it genuinely cannot be done as a standard job. This is a hard geometric limit, not a matter of effort.

"Decentration is a way of hiding a wrong PD." Decentration is calculated from a correct PD. If the PD used is wrong, decentring compounds the error — it does not correct it. This is one reason an inaccurate self-measured PD can produce glasses that feel slightly wrong even when the prescription itself is right.

"Optical centre just means the middle of the lens." It means the point of zero prism. Where it needs to be placed is determined by your pupils; where it sits by default is the middle of the blank. Those are different locations, and closing the gap between them is the whole point of the process.

When to see an optometrist or ophthalmologist

Ask for your PD and your frame measurements to be recorded properly if you are ordering glasses online, and be sceptical of a single PD copied from an old order if your frame has changed substantially.

Return for a re-check if new glasses cause a pulling or drifting sensation, if one eye feels strained while the other does not, or if you find yourself turning your head to look through the centre of the lens. Those can be centration symptoms rather than power symptoms, and they are diagnosed by measuring where the optical centres actually sit in the finished pair — something an optometrist can check directly on the assembled glasses.

If you have a high prescription, a large difference between your eyes, or a strong cylinder, mention the frame you are considering before ordering. The right time to discover that a frame demands a larger blank than usual is while there is still a choice to make.
