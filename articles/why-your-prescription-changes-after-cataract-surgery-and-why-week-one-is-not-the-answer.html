Why Your Prescription Changes After Cataract Surgery, and Why Week One Is Not the Answer

November 21, 2026 · 10 min read

Your eye is a two-element system, and one element gets replaced

The refracting power of a human eye comes from two surfaces working together. The cornea provides roughly 43 dioptres — the majority of the total — and the crystalline lens inside the eye adds about 20 dioptres, adjustable through accommodation. Total power at rest is in the region of 60 dioptres. This division of labour explains a great deal of what happens after cataract surgery, because the procedure does not touch the cornea. It replaces the second element.

A cataract is not a film on the lens. It is the breakdown of the lens's internal order: proteins aggregate, pockets of altered refractive index appear, and light is scattered instead of passing through. One change matters for prescriptions. As the nucleus hardens and its refractive index rises, the eye drifts myopic — the "second sight" of a patient who suddenly reads without glasses in their sixties, then loses it again. A refraction taken in the year before surgery may describe the cataract as much as the eye.

What biometry measures, and where the error hides

Before surgery the eye is measured in a process called biometry: axial length, corneal curvature, anterior chamber depth, and lens thickness. Those numbers feed a formula that predicts which implant power will land the eye at the intended focus. Modern formulas — Barrett, Hill-RBF, and the later generations of SRK/T — are good, but they are predictions, and the thing they predict is where the implant will eventually sit.

That position — the effective lens position — is the dominant source of error in an otherwise well-measured eye. The implant is placed in the capsular bag, the collapsed remnant of the natural lens, and the bag settles over the first weeks as swelling resolves and the capsule contracts around the implant. A fraction of a millimetre of settling changes the postoperative power. The formula assumes a position; the eye chooses its own.

The other measurable contributor is surgically induced astigmatism. Even a small clear corneal incision changes corneal shape slightly, and where the incision sits relative to your pre-existing astigmatism determines whether that change helps or hurts. Eyes with meaningful pre-existing cylinder are often offered a toric implant, which carries cylinder in the optic and must be aligned to the intended axis — alignment that depends on markings made before the eye is opened.

Why the number keeps moving for weeks after surgery

The refraction you get at the first postoperative visit is not a prescription. It is a snapshot of a healing cornea and a settling implant. Three processes are still running:

  • Corneal wound remodelling. Incision architecture and swelling resolve over weeks, and with them the induced cylinder and its axis.
  • Capsular contraction. The bag shrinks around the implant, and if the shrink is asymmetric the implant can tilt or shift slightly, changing both sphere and cylinder.
  • Posterior capsule opacification. Cells left behind migrate across the back of the capsule over months, gradually hazing the view. When it becomes visually significant it is treated with a YAG laser capsulotomy, and that procedure can itself nudge the refraction.

This is why the standard advice is to wait. Most surgeons refract for spectacles at roughly four to six weeks after uncomplicated surgery; eyes with rigid contact lens wear, previous refractive surgery or unusual axial length may take longer to stabilise. The implant is also a manufactured object — the ISO 11979 series sets optical requirements and tolerances for intraocular lenses, so a lens labelled at a given power carries a permitted manufacturing tolerance. Calculation, position and tolerance stack up; a residual quarter or half dioptre is a normal outcome, not a mistake.

What the implant choice does to your eventual glasses

A monofocal implant set for distance leaves a predictable reading addition to be handled in spectacles, usually a near add on top of a small distance correction. An extended-depth-of-focus implant stretches the usable range but does not remove the need for glasses in low light or for fine print. A multifocal implant splits light between distances, buying range at the cost of contrast and of the halos some patients notice at night. None is a failure; the glasses follow from the trade you made.

Residual astigmatism is the part patients most often underestimate. Cylinder left after surgery is fully correctable in spectacles, but its axis is a new number that does not match the axis on your old prescription. If the two eyes end up with noticeably different powers — common when only one has been operated on — you also meet anisometropia, where unequal magnification makes the world a different size in each eye. At low levels the brain tolerates this; at higher levels it produces the pulling, headachey discomfort that sends people back to the clinic. Higher powers are also physically thicker, which is worth checking with a thickness estimate before you commit to a frame.

The misconceptions that cost people a pair of glasses

The first is "after cataract surgery you won't need glasses again." Some eyes land close enough to plano that distance vision is spectacle-free, but most people still need something — readers, a small distance correction, or a pair for night driving. Promising otherwise sells an outcome the surgery never guaranteed.

The second is "my old glasses will do until I get round to it." They are not dangerous, but they will be wrong enough to cause headaches, and after unilateral surgery they can produce unequal-size double vision. Put them away.

The third, and the most expensive, is "I'll order new glasses online from the first postoperative refraction." A refraction at one week describes a cornea still changing: ordering then buys a lens for an eye that no longer exists a month later.

There is also a claim that reading glasses after surgery can be bought at a fixed strength because "everyone ends up at the same add." Adds follow working distance and residual accommodation, and they differ between people with the same implant.

When to see the surgeon, and when to see the optometrist

Two different questions need two different professionals. The surgeon owns the eye: pain, increasing redness, sensitivity to light, a sudden drop in vision, new floaters or flashes, or a shadow at the edge of vision are reasons to call the surgical team promptly rather than wait for a routine visit. These are not refraction problems, and no pair of glasses fixes them.

The optometrist owns the correction. Once your surgeon confirms the eye has stabilised, a full refraction describes the eye you now have. Bring the surgery date, the implant type if you know it, and any history of refractive surgery or rigid lens wear, because all three change how the numbers should be read. If your two eyes are very unequal, ask about a balance lens or a contact lens in the unoperated eye — sometimes the better answer is not spectacles at all. And if a new prescription feels wrong in a specific way — one side of the world larger, floor tilted, edges curved — say so at the fitting, not after the return window.
