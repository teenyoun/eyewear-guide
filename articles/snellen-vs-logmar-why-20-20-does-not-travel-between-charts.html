Snellen vs logMAR: Why 20/20 Does Not Travel Between Charts

November 04, 2026 · 9 min read

What the fraction is really describing

Visual acuity is an angular measurement. The standard 20/20 line is built on a defined geometry: an optotype whose overall height subtends five minutes of arc at the test distance, with the strokes that make up the letter subtending one minute of arc. The minimum angle of resolution — the smallest detail an eye can just resolve — is that one minute. The Snellen fraction expresses the result as a ratio between the distance at which the person recognised the letter and the distance at which a reference eye would recognise it. 20/40 means the letter had to be brought to twenty feet to be read, when a reference observer could have read it at forty.

In metric countries the same statement is written 6/6, because twenty feet is approximately six metres. The ratio is identical; only the unit changed. This is the first thing that surprises people who compare prescriptions from different countries: 20/20 and 6/6 are the same threshold, and neither is a measurement of anything except angular resolution at high contrast.

The design flaw that hides inside Snellen

Snellen's chart, published in the 1860s, was a genuine advance and remains the most recognisable medical instrument in the world. It also has three arithmetic problems that make its output hard to compare.

  • The steps are not equal. A conventional chart runs 20/200, 20/100, 20/70, 20/50, 20/40, 20/30, 20/25, 20/20, 20/15. The jump from 20/200 to 20/100 halves the letter size; the jump from 20/25 to 20/20 shrinks it by a fifth. Equal numbers of lines do not represent equal changes in acuity.
  • Rows hold different numbers of letters. Some rows have three optotypes, some have six or more. Passing a line with three letters and passing one with six are not the same achievement, yet both are scored as a line.
  • Optotypes are not equally legible. Letters differ in how easily they are confused with one another, so some rows are intrinsically harder than their nominal angular size suggests.

Add the scoring convention — usually "the last line read", with no credit for the letters read on the next line — and the result is a measurement with coarse, uneven resolution that depends on which chart was on the wall.

What logMAR changes, and why the change matters

logMAR takes the minimum angle of resolution and applies a logarithm. Zero logMAR is 20/20, or 6/6. Each 0.1 step is a line, and because the progression is logarithmic the steps are equal in ratio rather than in raw size: 0.1 logMAR is 20/25, 0.2 is 20/30, 0.3 is 20/40, and going the other way, negative values describe better-than-reference acuity. Because the arithmetic is linear in log space, an improvement of 0.1 logMAR means the same proportional change whether the starting point was 20/200 or 20/25.

The ETDRS chart, developed for clinical trials, applies the idea with five optotypes of equal difficulty per row and letter-by-letter scoring, so each letter is worth 0.02 log units. That fineness is the point: a change of one or two letters can be detected, which is why trials use it and why a "two-line improvement" on a Snellen chart is such a blunt instrument in comparison. Standardised illumination, a fixed working distance and controlled contrast turn the chart from a wall poster into an instrument.

Why a number copied from a website is not your acuity

Acuity depends on contrast, luminance, the optotype set, the scoring rule, the test distance, whether the measurement was monocular or binocular, and whether it was taken with or without correction. A phone-based test at arm's length in a living room controls almost none of those. It can be useful for noticing a change over time under identical conditions, and it is close to worthless as an absolute figure or as a comparison with a clinic result.

There is also a ceiling effect that rarely gets mentioned: charts top out. Someone who reads the smallest line on a standard chart at four metres has not had their acuity measured, only their ability to read that chart. Resolution limits vary with pupil size and with the optics of the eye, and a healthy young eye can resolve considerably better than 20/15 under favourable conditions.

Common claims, corrected

  • "20/20 is perfect vision." It is a reference threshold for high-contrast resolution at a specified distance. You can have 20/20 acuity and still struggle in low contrast, at night, or with glare — contrast sensitivity is a separate measurement, and it predicts real-world difficulty better than acuity in several conditions.
  • "20/15 is healthier than 20/20." It is better resolution, which is not the same as healthier. Acuity does not screen for glaucoma, retinal disease or a peripheral field defect.
  • "The lines convert exactly." Conversion tables assume a chart design. A 20/30 Snellen line and 0.2 logMAR are nominally equivalent, but the letter sets and scoring differ, so a converted value carries the uncertainty of both methods.
  • "A smaller chart at a shorter distance measures the same thing." Only if the angular size at the eye is preserved and the optics are unchanged. Shortening the distance changes accommodative demand, which matters in exactly the age group most likely to be tested.

Where the number stops being useful

Have your vision measured professionally if acuity has dropped by a line or more since your last check, if the two eyes differ and the gap is widening, if letters are clear but the world is not, if you need more light than you used to in order to read the same print, or if a home test and a clinic test disagree. Bring the discrepancy with you: the difference between two measurements taken under different conditions is itself informative.

Acuity is one number from one chair under one lighting condition. It is a good screening tool, a decent record of change, and a poor description of how well someone actually sees.
