Why Dust Sticks to a Clean Lens: Static Charge and Surface Resistivity

November 25, 2026 · 8 min read

The lens is clean, and every speck of dust in the room finds it

There is a specific annoyance familiar to anyone who cleans glasses carefully: the lens is clear, and within a minute it has collected a fine haze of lint and dust that was not visible before. Wiping again makes it worse. The instinct is to blame the cloth, the coating, or the air. The mechanism is electrostatic, and it explains why cleaning a lens can make it a better dust collector than leaving it alone.

This is a different property from the water and oil behaviour that hydrophobic and oleophobic coatings control. Those deal with liquids and surface energy. Dust adhesion is about electric charge.

Principle: rubbing an insulator charges it

Lens materials are dielectrics — optical plastics such as CR-39, polycarbonate and the higher-index polymers do not conduct electricity. When two dissimilar materials are pressed together and separated, electrons transfer across the interface, leaving one surface with excess electrons and the other with a deficit. This is triboelectric charging, and a microfibre cloth rubbed across a polymer lens is an efficient way to produce it.

A charged insulator holds that charge because it cannot conduct it away. The relevant material property is surface resistivity: how readily charge migrates across the surface. Untreated optical polymers sit at the high end of the insulating range, which means a charge deposited by wiping can persist for minutes to hours rather than draining away. Humid air helps, because a molecular layer of water on the surface provides a conduction path — which is why static problems are markedly worse in heated indoor air in winter, when relative humidity can fall well below 40 per cent.

The charged lens then attracts particles by electrostatic adhesion. For a small particle, this force can easily exceed gravity: a speck that would otherwise settle on a table is pulled to the lens and held there, often strongly enough that a light brush does not dislodge it. The result is the visible paradox — the cleanest lens collecting the most dust, because cleaning is what charged it.

Why the coating stack makes it worse before it makes it better

A standard anti-reflective stack ends with a hydrophobic and oleophobic top layer. Those layers are engineered for low surface energy, which helps water bead and oil wipe away. Low surface energy does not imply low charge, and the very smoothness that makes the surface easy to wipe also reduces the mechanical grip that would let a cloth sweep particles off rather than press them on.

Some premium stacks add an anti-static or conductive layer, usually a thin transparent oxide or a quaternary treatment, whose job is to bleed charge away. The effect is real but modest, and it degrades with the same abrasion that eventually removes the hydrophobic top layer. A lens that stopped repelling dust after a year has probably lost more than its water repellency.

What actually reduces the problem

  • Stop dry-wiping. Every dry pass across a lens generates charge and presses particles into the surface. Rinse first, then blot or wipe with a damp cloth.
  • Use the cloth once per side, then replace it. A cloth that has been rubbed repeatedly is both charged and loaded with particles.
  • Control indoor humidity. Static complaints cluster in dry, heated rooms. Raising relative humidity above roughly 40 per cent drains charge from surfaces passively.
  • Keep the lens in a case, not a pocket. Lint from fabric is the main particle source, and a pocket is a lint generator with a rubbing action built in.
  • Wash the cloth. Microfibre that has absorbed skin oils loses both its cleaning action and its ability to hold particles; a greasy cloth smears rather than lifts.

Common misconceptions

"Dust on the lens means the coating has failed." Coating failure looks different: a patch where water no longer beads, crazing, or a visible haze that does not wipe off. Dust that returns within minutes is charge and environment, not chemistry.

"Anti-static means dust-proof." No lens surface is dust-proof. A conductive or anti-static treatment reduces how long charge persists and therefore how strongly particles are held; it does not stop them arriving.

"A dry microfibre cloth is the safest thing for a lens." Dry microfibre is effective at lifting grease from glass and polymer, and it is also the most efficient static generator in the drawer. On a dusty lens it is the wrong first step.

"Static is only a cosmetic issue." Held particles are abrasive particles. If they are wiped across the surface while still adhering, they act as the third body in an abrasion cycle, and the scratches they leave are permanent. The dust you can see is an early warning, not just an annoyance.

When the surface itself is the problem

If one lens collects dust and the other does not, if the haze returns within seconds of cleaning, or if you can see a faint film that no cloth removes, the top layer may be contaminated or worn. Skin oils polymerised by heat, sunscreen residue and hair spray all bond to the surface and cannot be lifted by a dry cloth. In that case the answer is a proper wash with a drop of mild soap and water, drying with a clean cloth — and if the film persists, a professional clean rather than more wiping. Repeated aggressive cleaning to chase a problem that is caused by cleaning is the most common way a coating stack reaches the end of its life early.
