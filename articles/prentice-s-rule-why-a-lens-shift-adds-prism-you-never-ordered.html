Prentice's Rule: Why a Lens Shift Adds Prism You Never Ordered

November 12, 2026 · 9 min read

A unit of deviation, not of power

One prism dioptre (1Δ) displaces an image by 1 cm at 1 m. That definition matters, because prism is a statement about where the image lands, not about how strongly the lens focuses. Prentice's rule ties the two together: the prismatic effect at any point on a lens equals the power in play at that point multiplied by the distance from the optical centre, with the distance in centimetres. Labs write it as PΔ = D × d(mm) ÷ 10.

Three words carry the weight. At that point means the power in the direction of the shift: sphere for horizontal movement, cylinder along the cylinder axis. Optical centre means the axis of the lens optical system, which is neither the frame centre nor the blank centre. And distance means millimetres on the lens, not at the eye — which is why one pair can induce different prism for two people whose pupils sit differently in the same frame.

Why an off-centre lens behaves like a prism

Any spherical lens can be drawn as a stack of prisms. A plus lens is two prisms joined base to base, which is why it is thick in the middle; a minus lens is two prisms joined apex to apex, which is why it is thin in the middle. At the optical centre the surfaces are parallel, so light passes undeviated. Move 3 mm away and you are on a sloped section: the surfaces are at an angle to each other, and light leaves on a new path.

The direction of that path follows from where the material is thickest. In a plus lens the thick material is at the centre, so the base of the local prism points toward the optical centre. In a minus lens the thick material is at the rim, so the base points away from it. This one fact explains most of the confusing reports that follow a new pair: why a high myope feels the world pull inward at the edges, why a strong plus wearer notices an image shift on turning the head, and why one frame can feel fine on one face and wrong on another.

Where the number turns up in a real pair

Induced prism is almost never ordered. It arrives by accident, through five routes:

  • Pupillary distance not carried through. If the laboratory centres the lens on the frame rather than on your pupils, every lens sits off-centre by half the difference. A 66 mm frame on a 62 mm wearer puts each optical centre 2 mm nasal to the pupil.
  • Centre height left to default. Lenses are centred at a standard height unless the order says otherwise. If your pupils sit high or low, the error is vertical — and vertical prism is far less tolerated than horizontal.
  • Frame PD chosen for looks. An oversized frame is a decentration order in disguise: the wider the difference, the larger the prism at every off-centre gaze.
  • Near vision through a distant centre. At reading distance the line of sight drops and converges, so it passes through a different part of the lens than the one the centre was placed for.
  • Unequal powers between the eyes. Anisometropia means the two lenses induce different prism from the same decentration, and that difference is what the visual system must reconcile.

The arithmetic is unglamorous, which is the point. Take −5.00 D with a 2 mm nasal error: 5 × 2 ÷ 10 = 1.0Δ base out in each eye, because a minus lens throws its base away from the optical centre and the centre now sits nasal to where you look. Take +6.00 D with the same 2 mm error: 1.2Δ base in. Neither number looks dramatic on paper; both are permanent, and both are summed over every waking hour.

Why an unequal pair bothers people more than a centre error

Equal prism in both eyes shifts the world in one direction, and the visual system re-calibrates to a uniform shift reasonably well. Unequal prism is different: the two eyes receive images sitting at different points relative to their foveae, so either the eyes converge or diverge to compensate, or the brain suppresses one image. That is the mechanism behind the usual cluster after a bad pair: a pull toward one side when reading, one eye tiring before the other, and a headache that builds through the afternoon rather than arriving immediately.

Vertical differences are harsher still. The fusional range for vertical misalignment is small compared with the horizontal range, which is why half a dioptre of unwanted vertical prism can be more disruptive than two dioptres of horizontal prism. Where the two lenses differ in power, the near-zone consequences are handled by slab-off, a design decision no frame choice can substitute for.

Decentration is also the quiet variable in lens thickness: moving the optical centre toward the pupil means grinding the blank off-centre, and in a strong prescription the edge thickness on one side grows accordingly. The claims that need correcting

A prescription with no prism column carries no prism. Prescribed and induced prism are different things. The first is written on the order; the second is created by geometry in any lens whose optical centre is not where you look through it. Most people describing prism-like symptoms have no prism on their prescription at all.

Two millimetres is too small to matter. Two millimetres is 0.2 cm, so it matters in exact proportion to the power. At −1.00 D it is 0.2Δ and genuinely negligible. At −8.00 D it is 1.6Δ per eye, a working prism correction in its own right. The tolerance question is never about distance alone.

A lensmeter reading proves the lenses are centred. A lensmeter measures the power present at the point you place in the aperture. It says nothing about where the optical centre sits relative to your pupil unless that centre is marked and the pair is measured against the mark. Verification is a comparison between two positions, not a single reading.

Prism is always bad. Prism is a tool: deliberate prism relieves double vision, supports a decompensating phoria, or compensates the face-form angle of a wrapped sunglass. The problem is never prism as such, only prism nobody calculated.

The point at which to stop calculating

Prentice's rule is a first-order approximation: it assumes thin lenses, small angles and a distant object, and it drifts for very strong powers, thick lenses and large decentrations. It also says nothing about how your visual system will respond. If a new pair produces persistent double vision, a sideways pull when reading, a tilted floor, or a headache that does not settle within a couple of weeks, the useful next step is a dispensing check: optical centres marked, PD measured monocularly, centre height measured against your pupils in the frame as worn, lens powers verified. That is the only way to know whether the prism you feel is the prism the lens carries.
