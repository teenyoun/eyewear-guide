Nickel Allergy and Glasses Frames: Why Metal Temples Itch

November 14, 2026 · 9 min read

A rash that arrives a day late

Nickel allergy on a pair of glasses rarely looks like what people expect. It appears on the side of the head behind the ear, along the temple line, or across the bridge of the nose, and it usually arrives a day or two after a new frame goes on rather than the same afternoon. The delay is the clue to the mechanism, and the mechanism explains almost everything else about which frames cause trouble and which do not.

The reaction is allergic contact dermatitis, a type IV delayed hypersensitivity. Nickel in a frame is not, by itself, in a form that the skin can recognise. It has to leave the metal as Ni2+ ions, cross the outer layer of skin, and bind to the body's own proteins. Only that combined structure is read as foreign. The first time it happens nothing visible occurs, but the immune system records it. On later exposures, memory T cells respond and eczema appears roughly 24 to 72 hours after contact. Someone can therefore wear metal frames for twenty years and react for the first time at forty-five: the sensitisation happened long ago, perhaps from earrings, a watch back, a belt buckle or a key ring, and the frame is simply the most recent exposure.

Release rate, not nickel content, is the number that matters

This distinction is where most shopping advice goes wrong. The regulated quantity for consumer articles is nickel release, expressed as micrograms of nickel per square centimetre per week, not the percentage of nickel in the alloy. In the European Union, REACH Annex XVII entry 27 restricts articles intended for direct and prolonged contact with the skin to a release rate of 0.5 micrograms per square centimetre per week, with a separate and tighter limit for earrings and body piercings. The reference test method is EN 1811. Because a new frame is not a worn frame, EN 12472 describes the simulated wear and corrosion procedure applied before the release test is run. Spectacle frames, ready-to-wear readers and sunglasses have their own specific reference method, EN 16128, which exists precisely because a frame is a different geometry from a flat metal disc.

Two frames with similar nickel content can therefore behave completely differently. A passivated stainless steel contains nickel and still releases very little, because the chromium oxide layer on its surface is a barrier that re-forms when scratched. A plated zinc alloy frame, common in low-cost metal eyewear, may be a sandwich of base metal, a nickel interlayer and a decorative chromium topcoat. While the plating is intact, release is low. Once it microcracks at the ear bend, the nickel layer is exposed and the release rate at that spot can sit far above the limit. That is why the same model can be fine for one wearer and a problem for another, and why a frame that was comfortable for a year suddenly starts to itch.

Where a frame actually touches you

The high-risk zones are predictable from anatomy. Temples rest on the side of the head and the mastoid, often under hair, where sweat collects and evaporation is poor. Temple tips curl behind the ear against thin skin. Nose pad arms sit on the sides of the nose, and the bridge crosses skin that has almost no padding over bone. Occlusion matters as much as the alloy: trapped sweat lowers pH and raises chloride concentration, and chloride is exactly what drives pitting corrosion in nickel-bearing metals. Friction adds a second pathway, because abraded skin is easier for ions to cross. A heavy frame that slides, or a temple that presses hard enough to leave a mark, is therefore a worse candidate than the same metal in a well-fitted frame.

Reading a frame description without being misled

Material names are a rough guide, not a guarantee. Titanium and beta-titanium release negligible nickel, and where a titanium alloy contains nickel at all it is not at the skin interface. TR90 and acetate are polymers with no metal in the load-bearing skin-contact structure, though the core wire inside an acetate temple can still be nickel-bearing at the tip. A declaration of conformity to EN 16128, or to EN 1811 with a stated result, is worth more than any adjective, because it names a test and a number rather than an intention. The word hypoallergenic has no legal definition in eyewear and no test standing behind it; it is a marketing term and should not be read as a materials specification. Clear lacquer over a metal frame does work as a barrier while it lasts, but the lacquer at the ear bend is usually the first thing to wear through.

The misconceptions worth clearing up

The first is that stainless steel cannot be the culprit because it is surgical. Release depends on surface condition, plating integrity and the local chemistry of your skin, not on the reputation of the alloy, and a corroded plated frame is a different object from the one in the catalogue photograph. The second is that a nickel allergy rules out metal frames for life. It rules out frames that release nickel above the threshold at the contact points; titanium, lacquered frames and acetate fronts with polymer temples often work well, and a patch test tells you what you are actually reacting to instead of guessing. The third is the most expensive: that the rash will settle if you keep wearing the frame. Sensitisation does not fade with continued exposure, and repeated reactions tend to become more vigorous and to spread, sometimes to the eyelids, which are thin enough to react to nickel carried there on the fingers after handling keys, coins or a phone.

When this needs a professional rather than a new frame

If the skin is blistered, weeping or broken, or if the rash has spread beyond the contact line, stop wearing the frame and see a doctor rather than shopping for another alloy. Patch testing by a dermatologist identifies nickel sulfate sensitivity specifically, and separates it from a reaction to a coating, to a preservative in a lens cleaner, or to a rubberised temple grip. An optician or optometrist has a different job: checking that the frame is not pressing, that the temple length suits your head, and that contact pressure is distributed rather than concentrated behind one ear. In practice the two problems often arrive together, because a frame that migrates and rubs will abrade both the plating and the skin. If you are replacing it and your prescription is strong enough that lens thickness is also a factor, run the numbers before you commit to a thin metal front with nickel-free titanium frames, and check the thickness in advance with the lens thickness calculator.
