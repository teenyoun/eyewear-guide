Colour Vision Deficiency: What a Tinted Lens Can and Cannot Change

October 22, 2026 · 7 min read

﻿

Colour is a comparison, not a signal

Nothing in the world carries a label that says "red". The visual system infers colour by comparing the responses of three cone types with different spectral sensitivities. A surface that reflects mostly long wavelengths produces a large difference between the long-wavelength cones and the middle-wavelength cones, and the brain interprets that ratio as one hue rather than another. Colour vision therefore depends on differences between channels, not on the absolute amount of light. That single fact explains most of what a tinted lens can and cannot do for someone with a colour vision deficiency.

What the deficiency actually is

Congenital red-green deficiency is the common form, and it comes in two structurally different kinds. In anomalous trichromacy all three cone types are present, but one of them has a shifted sensitivity, so the difference between two adjacent channels is smaller than normal and hues that should be easily separated look close together. In dichromacy one cone type is effectively absent, and the comparison that would separate those hues cannot be made at all.

The genetics explain the pattern: the genes for the long and middle wavelength opsins sit on the X chromosome. A male has one copy, so a single altered copy is enough. A female has two, so two altered copies are needed. This is why red-green deficiency affects roughly eight per cent of men and about half a per cent of women in populations of Northern European descent, and why a woman can be a carrier with subtly altered colour discrimination without being classified as deficient.

What a filter can do

A tinted lens sits in front of the eye and attenuates parts of the spectrum. It cannot add a channel that is missing, and it cannot restore a sensitivity curve that has shifted. What it can do is change the ratio between the channels that remain: by absorbing a narrow band, a filter can increase the difference between two surfaces that would otherwise look nearly identical, or decrease it. Whether that helps depends entirely on which surfaces you need to separate and which filter you use — and the same lens that separates a red berry from green foliage can collapse a different pair.

That is why claims about colour-enhancing lenses need to be read carefully. Some filters do measurably improve performance on specific colour-arrangement tasks for some observers, and there is a real perceptual basis for that: increasing the contrast between two similar hues makes them easier to order. It is not the same as normal colour vision, and it does not generalise to every task or every pair of colours. A filter that helps one person distinguish a ripening fruit may make a warning light harder to identify.

Where it matters practically

  • Occupational screening. Some roles test colour discrimination for safety reasons rather than for aesthetics, and the test used matters as much as the result.
  • Contrast versus discrimination. A tint that raises contrast on a display or in fog can be genuinely useful even when it does nothing for hue discrimination.
  • Reading coloured displays and wiring. Colour-coded systems are designed around normal discrimination, so a person with a deficiency benefits more from redundant coding — shape, position, labels — than from a filter.

Misconceptions

  • "Colour blind means seeing in black and white." Total colour blindness, achromatopsia, exists but is rare. The overwhelming majority of deficiencies are red-green and involve a reduced range of hue discrimination, not a monochrome world.
  • "A tinted lens can give you normal colour vision." It cannot create a missing response or shift a cone's sensitivity. It can only re-weight the light that arrives, which sometimes improves one specific task.
  • "You cannot be colour deficient and drive." Licensing rules vary and usually test practical recognition of signals rather than a laboratory score, and signal lights are deliberately redundant in position and brightness.
  • "Only men are affected." Men are far more often affected, but carriers can show measurable differences, and acquired colour deficiency is a separate story that affects both sexes equally.

When to get it checked

Congenital deficiency is stable, so a change is the signal that matters. Report colour vision that has become different in one eye, colours that have dulled or shifted, or colour disturbance alongside headache, or pain on eye movement — those are reasons for a clinical examination, not a new pair of sunglasses. Anyone whose work depends on colour discrimination, and any child struggling with colour-coded learning materials, is better served by a proper colour vision test than by buying a filter and hoping.

How it is tested

Screening is usually done with plates in which a number or a shape is drawn in dots of one hue against dots of another. The design works because a person with reduced red-green discrimination cannot separate the figure from its background by hue, and the dots vary in brightness enough that guessing is unreliable. Arrangement tests go further, asking the person to order a series of caps by hue, which quantifies the error rather than merely detecting it. The anomaloscope, which requires the person to match a mixture of two wavelengths to a third, is the reference method because it measures the actual ratio at which the match occurs.

These tests answer different questions. A plate test screens; an arrangement test grades severity and type; an anomaloscope characterises the underlying defect. For occupational certification, the test specified by the employer matters as much as the score, because a screening plate and a grading test do not produce interchangeable results.
