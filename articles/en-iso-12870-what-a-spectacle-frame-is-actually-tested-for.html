EN ISO 12870: What a Spectacle Frame Is Actually Tested For

November 01, 2026 · 8 min read

A frame is a device that touches skin, and that is what gets tested

Lens standards get the attention, but a spectacle frame is a separate regulated object: a spring-loaded mechanical assembly held against the face for sixteen hours a day, in contact with sweat, sebum and hair products. EN ISO 12870, "Ophthalmic optics — Spectacle frames — Requirements and test methods", is the document that defines what a frame has to survive before it can be sold as an ordinary spectacle frame in most of the world. It is deliberately not a style or durability marketing standard. It is a list of failure modes with a test attached to each one.

Understanding the list is useful for two reasons. It explains why a frame that feels flimsy can still be compliant, and it explains why a frame that looks immaculate can still ruin your skin.

What the standard actually tests

The requirements cluster into mechanical stability, chemical resistance and biological contact:

  • Dimensional stability. Frames are exposed to elevated temperature and checked for deformation. A frame that creeps out of shape in a hot car has failed a real requirement, not just an aesthetic one, because a temple that loses its bend changes vertex distance and pantoscopic tilt.
  • Resistance to perspiration. Metal components are exposed to an artificial sweat solution and inspected for corrosion, discolouration and degradation of the surface finish. This is the clause that separates a plated frame from a properly finished one.
  • Nickel release. Nickel-releasing parts that contact skin are tested against the eyewear-specific method, EN 16128, which descends from the reference method EN 1811. Under REACH Annex XVII, items intended for prolonged skin contact must not release more than 0.5 micrograms of nickel per square centimetre per week.
  • Flammability. Frame materials must not sustain combustion in the prescribed test.
  • Construction and finish. No sharp edges, no projections at eye level, and hardware that does not come apart in normal use.

The scope has boundaries worth knowing. EN ISO 12870 covers ordinary spectacle frames. Occupational eye protectors fall under EN 166, and sunglasses as products fall under ISO 12312-1. A frame carrying an ISO 12870 statement is telling you something about the frame, and nothing at all about the lenses inside it.

The mechanism behind the green mark on your nose

The corrosion clause is not bureaucratic fussiness. Nearly every metal frame is a galvanic couple. Nickel silver — the alloy behind most "silver" frames, despite containing no silver — is copper, nickel and zinc. It is frequently joined to stainless steel screws, monel or titanium nose pads, and finished with a thin electroplated layer of nickel, palladium or gold. Sweat is an electrolyte with a pH that drifts alkaline during the day.

Put those facts together and you get a small battery at the bridge and behind the ears. The less noble metal becomes the anode and dissolves. That is why pitting appears first where the plating is thinnest, why the green stain is copper salts rather than dirt, and why the same frame can be fine for a year and then start marking your skin once the plating has worn through at a contact point. Nickel release is the same process measured chemically: the metal does not have to be visibly corroding to be releasing enough nickel to sensitise skin.

The clinical consequence has a name. Nickel is the most common cause of allergic contact dermatitis from eyewear, and it is a delayed hypersensitivity reaction — the rash behind the ear or on the side of the nose appears hours to days after contact, which is why people rarely connect it to the frame. Once sensitised, the reaction tends to persist and to spread to other nickel sources.

What "ISO 12870 tested" is worth on a label

It means a sample of frames passed the listed tests. It does not mean every unit was tested, that the frame is comfortable, that the hinge will last five years, or that the coating will not craze. Compliance is a floor for safety and basic function, and plenty of cheap frames clear it while plenty of expensive ones never bother to document it. Where the statement earns its keep is in three areas: metal frames worn by people with sensitive skin, frames sold into the EU where the nickel limit is legally binding, and frames with unusual constructions — screwless hinges, very thin rims, memory-metal temples — where the mechanical clauses are doing real work.

Four statements that get repeated and should not be

"Hypoallergenic" is a meaningful specification. It is not defined in EN ISO 12870 and has no legal test behind it in most markets. The measurable version of the claim is "nickel release below the REACH limit", ideally with a test report number.

"Titanium frames contain no nickel." Commercial pure titanium does not, but titanium alloys, solder alloys at the bridge, and nickel-containing platings on titanium frames do. A titanium frame with a nickel-plated hinge can still provoke a reaction at the temple.

"Stainless steel is nickel-free." The austenitic stainless steels used in eyewear hardware typically contain eight to ten per cent nickel or more. They are usually low-release because the passive oxide layer is stable, which is a different statement from being nickel-free.

"The CE mark means a laboratory tested this pair." The CE mark is a manufacturer's declaration of conformity, supported by a technical file. It is not a per-unit test, and for eyewear it is self-declared.

Reading a frame the way a tester would

Look at the joints first. A brazed bridge on a metal frame is a dissimilar-metal junction and the place corrosion starts; a monolithic or screwed construction has one fewer failure point. Look at the plating at the nose pads and the temple tips, which is where abrasion removes the barrier layer first. Look for the material declaration on the inner temple — "titanium", "Ti", "monel", "stainless" — and treat a blank temple as a blank specification. On acetate frames, the equivalent clues are the thickness of the material at the hinge and whether the frame has been tumbled and polished rather than merely moulded, because those determine whether it can be adjusted without stress-whitening.

When to take the frame to a professional

See an optician or a dermatologist rather than persisting with a new pair if you develop an itchy, scaly or weeping rash behind the ears or along the nose, particularly if it appears days after first wear and fades when you stop wearing the frame. Ask for an adjustment, not a new frame, if the temples have lost their bend or the frame rocks on a flat surface — those are fit problems that change the optics. And if the plating has worn through at a contact point on an older frame, the frame is now a different object chemically than the one that was sold to you; a nickel-free replacement or a coated temple tip is usually the cheaper answer than a course of steroid cream.
