Image Jump: Why a Bifocal Line Makes the World Leap

October 29, 2026 · 8 min read

The step you feel but cannot see

Anyone who has worn a lined bifocal knows the sensation: reading is comfortable, the distance is clear, and yet every time the gaze crosses the line the page seems to lurch by a few centimetres. Nothing has moved, the prescription is unchanged, and the effect does not fade with practice. The phenomenon has a name, image jump, and unlike most adaptation complaints it is fully predictable from two numbers on the work order.

The mechanism: a segment is a second lens with its own optical centre

A bifocal is not one lens with a line drawn on it. It is a distance lens with a smaller lens, the segment, fused or surfaced into its lower portion. That segment behaves exactly like any other lens: it has a power, which is the reading addition, and it has an optical centre, the point where its prismatic effect is zero. The wearer looks at near objects through the top part of the segment, just below the dividing line, and that line sits some distance above the segment's optical centre. Everything the wearer sees at near is therefore viewed through a point that is off the segment's optical centre by that distance, and an off-centre point on a lens is a prism.

The prism at the top edge of a plus segment has its base downward, because the segment thickens as you move down toward its optical centre. A prism displaces the image toward its apex, so the near image is displaced upward relative to the distance image. When the gaze crosses the line, the image does not slide, it jumps, and the size of the jump is the prism present at the line itself.

The formula is short enough to do on paper

Image jump in prism dioptres equals the reading addition multiplied by the distance from the near optical centre to the dividing line, measured in centimetres. That is Prentice's rule applied to the segment alone, and it needs no correction factors.

Take the most common configuration: a 22 mm flat-top segment, whose optical centre sits roughly four millimetres below the top line, with a +2.00 D add. The jump is 0.4 x 2.00 = 0.80 prism dioptres. Change the add to +2.50 D and the same geometry gives 1.00 prism dioptre. On the face, that is a shift of about a centimetre per metre of reading distance, which is why the page appears to move rather than to blur.

Now take a 22 mm round segment. Its optical centre is in the middle of the segment, eleven millimetres below the top of the arc, so the same +2.00 D add gives 1.1 x 2.00 = 2.20 prism dioptres, nearly three times the jump of the flat-top. This is not a subtle design nuance; it is the historical reason the flat-top displaced the round segment in most markets.

  • Executive (Franklin) bifocal. The segment spans the full width and its optical centre falls on the dividing line, so the distance from the centre to the line is zero and the jump is zero by construction. The cost is a heavy, thick lens with a visible ledge across the whole width, and a hard edge of prism at the sides.
  • Flat-top (D-segment). A compromise: the optical centre is placed close to the line, jump is modest, and the segment stays narrow enough to keep weight down.
  • Round segment. The optical centre is unavoidably far from the line, so jump is large. Round segments survive mainly where a small add and a wide reading area matter more than the transition.

Jump is not the same as displacement

Two different prism effects get confused, and they are worth separating because only one of them is a design choice. Image jump is the prism at the dividing line, and it is what the wearer notices when the gaze crosses that line. Image displacement is the prism at the actual reading point, further down the segment, and it is what makes the near image sit slightly off to one side. Both come from the same rule, but a segment can be designed to minimise one at the expense of the other. A flat-top controls jump by putting its optical centre near the line, which pushes the reading point further from that centre, so displacement at the reading point is traded upward.

Why progressives have no jump and still feel strange

A progressive addition lens has no line and no segment, so the prism has no step to jump across, and by that measure it is the cleaner design. What it substitutes is a continuous change of power down a corridor, with unwanted astigmatism growing toward the sides, which is why the first week of a progressive produces swim and peripheral blur rather than a lurch. The trade is a discontinuity in one design for a gradient in the other. Neither is universally easier; they fail in different ways.

The common misconception: the lab cut it wrong

The most persistent wrong idea is that image jump means the segment was positioned incorrectly or the prescription was misread. In fact the jump is present in a perfectly made bifocal, because it is geometry, not error. What can be wrong is the fitting height: if the line sits too high or too low relative to the lower lid, the wearer crosses it at the wrong point in the gaze and the same physical jump becomes far more noticeable. That is an adjustment of position, not of power.

A second misconception is that jump can be removed from a flat-top by asking for a larger segment. Increasing segment size moves the optical centre further from the line, which makes the jump worse, not better, and adds weight. If jump is the problem, the answer is an executive design, a lower add, or a switch to a progressive, not a bigger D-segment.

When to take it back to the optician

Return for a fitting review if the jump is accompanied by double vision at near, if the near image is clear in one position of the head only, if the line sits at or above the pupil when looking straight ahead, or if the sensation is one of the floor tilting rather than the page moving. Those point to segment height, inset or a vertical imbalance between the two eyes rather than to the jump itself. A jump of under about one prism dioptre is normal and adapts; a jump the wearer can time with a stopwatch, or one that appears in a pair that previously felt fine, is a fitting question worth thirty minutes of someone else's time.
