Why Do My Glasses Give Me a Headache? 8 Common Causes and Fixes

August 10, 2026 · 6 min read

New glasses should make your vision clearer — not give you a headache. If your head hurts after wearing new glasses, something is off. The good news: most causes are fixable without buying another pair. Here are the 8 most common reasons, from most to least likely.

1. Incorrect Pupillary Distance (PD) — Most Common Online Order Issue

If you ordered glasses online and measured your own PD, this is the #1 suspect. When the optical center of the lens does not align with your pupil, your eyes must constantly adjust to compensate — this muscle strain causes headaches, usually at the temples or behind the eyes, within 30-60 minutes of wearing.

Fix: Have your PD professionally measured at an optical shop (many do this for free). Compare to what you submitted. A difference of 2mm or more will cause noticeable strain. If your self-measurement was wrong, you need new lenses with correct PD.

2. Adaptation Period — Normal for First-Time Wearers

If these are your first glasses ever, or the first glasses after a significant prescription change (more than ±0.50 diopter change), mild headaches for 3-7 days are normal. Your brain is learning to interpret images from a lens it has never used before — the visual cortex is literally rewiring.

Fix: Wear the glasses consistently for at least 4-5 days. If headaches persist beyond 7 days, the prescription or fitting is wrong.

3. Prescription Too Strong

An over-minused prescription (too much correction for nearsightedness) forces your accommodation (focusing muscle) to work harder than necessary. This causes deep eye-ache that worsens through the day. More common with prescriptions from automated refractors than from manual refraction by an experienced optometrist.

Fix: Return to your optometrist for a re-check. Many offer free prescription re-checks within 30-60 days.

4. Frame Pressure on Temples or Nose

If the headache is concentrated at the sides of your head (above the ears) or at the bridge of your nose, the frame itself is the problem — not the lenses. Too-tight temples compress nerves; poorly-fitted nose pads create pressure points.

Fix: Adjust temple bend (gently bend outward to reduce pressure) or visit an optical shop for free adjustments. Most will adjust glasses even if purchased elsewhere.

5. Incorrect Cylinder Axis (Astigmatism)

A cylinder axis that is off by 5-10 degrees causes images to tilt — your brain tries to compensate by tilting your head, causing neck tension headaches. More common with higher astigmatism (above -1.50 cylinder).

Fix: Prescription re-check — specifically mention that images appear tilted or slanted.

6. Base Curve Mismatch

Online glasses sometimes use a different base curve than what your eyes are accustomed to. Changing base curve changes the effective magnification — your eyes must adapt. This is subtle but cumulative.

Fix: If switching between old and new glasses causes discomfort, stick exclusively with the new pair for 5-7 days to adapt.

7. Lens Material Change

Switching from CR-39 plastic to polycarbonate or high-index lenses changes chromatic aberration (color fringing) at lens edges. This can cause subtle eye strain, especially for people sensitive to it.

Fix: Request the same lens material as your previous comfortable glasses.

8. Prism-Induced Diplopia

If the optical centers are not aligned symmetrically, the lenses create an unintentional prism effect — your eyes see slightly different images and must fuse them through muscular effort. This causes the worst headaches of all, often with nausea.

Fix: This requires a thorough optician check with a lensometer to verify optical center placement. Most likely requires lens replacement.

When to See an Eye Doctor Immediately

If headaches are accompanied by: double vision that doesn't resolve, rainbow halos around lights, sudden vision loss, or eye pain (not just strain), stop wearing the glasses and see an eye doctor promptly. These can indicate serious conditions unrelated to the glasses.
