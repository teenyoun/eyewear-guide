Why Do My Glasses Give Me a Headache? 8 Common Causes and Fixes

August 10, 2026 · 6 min read

New glasses should sharpen the world, not tighten your forehead. A headache after a new prescription is one of the most common complaints in any optical practice, and it usually has a measurable cause. A lens is a precision optical component: shift it two millimetres or tilt it three degrees and your eye works against it all day. Here are the eight causes responsible most often.

The mechanism: why a lens that looks fine can still hurt

Holding one clear image requires three systems to agree: accommodation (the ciliary muscle changing the crystalline lens's power), vergence (the extraocular muscles converging both eyes on one point), and the brain's fusion of the two retinal images. A lens that is decentred, over-powered or tilted breaks that agreement, and the muscular effort of restoring it is the ache you feel behind the eyes.

The working model is Prentice's rule: Δ = c × F, where Δ is induced prism in prism dioptres, c is the decentration in centimetres — the distance from optical centre to the point you actually look through — and F is the power in dioptres. One prism dioptre displaces an image 1 cm at 1 m, or 0.57°. A −4.00 D lens decentred 2.5 mm therefore carries 1.0Δ the wearer never ordered, in every direction of gaze.

Cause 1 — A pupillary distance a few millimetres out

Pupillary distance (PD) decides where each lens's optical centre sits. A single PD assumes a symmetric face, while most faces differ by 0.5–2 mm between sides — hence dual PDs. The error feeds Prentice's rule directly: 2 mm at −4.00 D is 0.8Δ, 3 mm at −6.00 D is 1.8Δ, and most people notice around 1Δ. Fix: have a dual PD measured in person, and ask for a remake if your order differs by more than 1.5 mm.

Cause 2 — A minus power stronger than you need

An automated refractor reports the power at which the retinal image is sharpest; a subjective refraction ends at the most plus lens that still gives best acuity. Extra minus forces continuous accommodation to compensate, and the ache builds through the afternoon. ISO 8980-1 and ANSI Z80.1 limit how far a delivered lens may deviate from the ordered power, but say nothing about whether that number suited your eyes. Fix: ask for a re-check and a possible reduction.

Cause 3 — A cylinder axis a few degrees off

The cylinder axis is the meridian carrying no astigmatic correction. An axis error leaves residual astigmatism of roughly 2 × C × sin(Δθ): at 2.00 D of cylinder, 5° leaves 0.35 D and 10° leaves 0.70 D. Straight edges lean, the floor feels uneven, and the head tilts to find a clearer meridian — the source of the neck component. Fix: describe the tilt, not the pain (see why new glasses make the world curve).

Cause 4 — Frame pressure on temples or nose

Pain in a band above the ears, or at one spot on the bridge, is mechanical. Narrow temples press on the sides of the skull; pads set too high or too hard create a pressure point. It appears within 15–30 minutes, repeats every time, and usually leaves a red mark. Adult frame fronts run 130–145 mm, so a 5 mm mismatch changes pressure noticeably. Fix: adjust the temples and pads before touching the prescription (see adjusting glasses at home).

Cause 5 — A different vertex distance or pantoscopic tilt

Vertex distance is the gap from the back of the lens to the cornea, normally 12–14 mm; effective power changes as Feff = F / (1 − d × F), with d in metres. At −2.00 D a 4 mm shift is worth 0.02 D, which is irrelevant; at −8.00 D the same shift is worth roughly 0.2 D, which is not. Pantoscopic tilt, normally 8–12°, must be set together with optical centre height, or it induces astigmatism and vertical prism.

Cause 6 — A different base curve or lens material

Base curve, the front-surface curvature, sets magnification; a one-step change alters image size by roughly 1–2%, enough to matter when the two eyes disagree — the condition called aniseikonia. Material changes dispersion instead: polycarbonate's Abbe number is about 30 against roughly 58 for CR-39, so colour fringing at the lens edge is visibly worse in high powers.

Cause 7 — A normal adaptation period

A first pair, a change of 0.50 D or more, or a first progressive prescription commonly costs three to seven days of mild discomfort while the visual system recalibrates. What separates adaptation from fault is direction: adaptation is worst on day one and improves, while a fitting error is flat or worse by day five. Fix: wear them consistently, then bring both pairs back.

Cause 8 — Unwanted prism from asymmetric optical centres

If the two optical centres are not symmetric relative to the pupils — a mis-measured monocular PD, or a frame sitting crooked on an asymmetric face — the pair creates prism that differs between eyes. Horizontal disparity is well tolerated because convergence has reserve; vertical disparity is not, and about 1Δ is where symptoms typically begin. Expect headache within minutes, often with nausea. Fix: lensometer verification against the marked pupil positions.

Myths that keep people uncomfortable

"You just have to get used to it." Adaptation is real but bounded; discomfort unchanged at two weeks is a geometry error that more wearing will not fix.

"It must be the cheap online lenses." A correctly measured dual PD in a properly sized frame is comfortable at any price, and a badly measured one is not at any price.

"The blue-light coating is giving me headaches." No established mechanism links such coatings to pain; screen headaches are more often uncorrected refractive error, a reduced blink rate with dry eye, or sustained near accommodation.

A ten-minute self-check before you go back

  1. Time it. Minutes after putting them on points to fit or prism; an hour or more to power or accommodation.
  2. Locate it. Temples and brow suggest the frame, behind the eyes suggests accommodation, one side only suggests asymmetry.
  3. Cover one eye, then the other. An image that jumps or tilts points to decentration or axis.
  4. Bring the old pair. Side-by-side comparison is the fastest diagnostic an optician has.

When to stop troubleshooting and get medical advice

Most lens headaches are mechanical and benign. These are not: sudden loss or blurring of vision, rainbow halos around lights, pain in the eye rather than around it, double vision persisting with one eye closed, or headache with nausea, fever and neck stiffness. Remove the glasses and seek care promptly. Children deserve a lower threshold — a child who cannot articulate the problem may simply avoid reading.
