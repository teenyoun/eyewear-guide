How a Lensmeter Reads Your Glasses: Back Vertex Power and the Neutralisation Trick

October 26, 2026 · 8 min read

What the instrument is actually doing

A lensmeter — focimeter in British usage, lensometer in American — does not read the order form. It measures the lens in front of it, using a trick that predates electricity: collimate a target, pass that parallel light through the lens, and find the position where the lens's own focal point lands on the instrument's telescope.

The target is called a mire, and it is usually a ring of dots with a cross-line pattern inside. It sits at the front focal point of the instrument's collimating lens, so the light leaving that lens is parallel — vergence zero. The lens under test then adds its power, and the telescope, focused at infinity, sees the mire sharp only when the lens has returned the beam to parallel. Sharp mires therefore mean the lens is being read at its back focal length. A lensmeter is, in essence, a device for finding a focal point by eye.

Back vertex power is not front vertex power

By convention, and by specification in ISO 8598-1, a focimeter reports back vertex power: the power measured with the back surface facing the instrument's stop. This is not arbitrary. A prescription is a statement about the back surface, because spectacle lenses are ordered and verified at the back vertex, the surface whose position relative to the eye is fixed by the frame and the vertex distance.

For a thin lens the distinction vanishes. For a thick one it does not. The front and back vertex powers of the same physical lens differ, and the difference grows with centre thickness and with power. A lens can read measurably differently depending on which surface faces the stop, and both readings can be correct. This is a consequence of measuring at two different planes, not a defect in the lens.

The practical consequence for anyone comparing a new pair against an old one: verify them the same way round. A lens checked front-to-back against an instrument and then back-to-front against a phone app is not a comparison at all.

Reading a toric lens

Rotate the axis wheel until one set of mire lines is sharp and the perpendicular set is broken. That direction is one principal meridian, and the reading is the sphere. Turn the axis wheel 90° until the second set is sharp, record it, and the difference between the two is the cylinder while the first meridian gives the axis.

The spherical equivalent, sphere plus half the cylinder, is the single power that would blur a point source equally in every meridian. It is the number to use when comparing a toric lens against a spherical one, or when checking whether a slight undercorrection of cylinder is likely to matter at all.

Two signs are worth knowing. If the mires never sharpen cleanly in either meridian, the lens is either not seated flat against the stop or it carries prism. If the cross-lines drift apart or converge instead of staying parallel, that is prism, and the amount is read off the reticle's concentric rings, which are graduated in prism dioptres rather than in millimetres.

What a lensmeter cannot tell you

Three limits matter, and all three are the reason perfectly measured glasses can still be unwearable.

  • It measures one point. The reading is taken under the stop, wherever that happens to fall. A lens whose optical centre sits 4 mm away from where the pupil sits will still read a perfectly correct power, because the power at the optical centre is correct.
  • It says nothing about centration. Optical centre position, monocular optical centre height and the horizontal distance to each pupil are separate measurements with separate instruments. This is where most real dispensing faults live.
  • It says nothing about surface or coating quality. Transmittance, scratch resistance, UV blocking and impact behaviour are outside its scope entirely.

There is also a subtlety of definition: a lensmeter reads the power present at the point measured, not the lens you ordered. A lens ground to a slightly different base curve, or edged from a different blank, can measure correctly and still be a different optical device from the one specified.

The misconception worth killing

“If the lensmeter matches the prescription, the glasses are correct.” This is the most common wrong conclusion in optical dispensing. Power verification is one of four checks and, in practice, the least likely to fail. The other three — centration, axis orientation as mounted in the frame, and the relationship between lens and eye — are where the failures actually occur. A pair can pass a lensmeter flawlessly and still be unusable because both optical centres sit three millimetres too high or because the frame's tilt has added a cylinder that was never prescribed.

The standards behind the reading

ISO 8598-1 specifies what a general-purpose focimeter must do: measure vertex power, cylinder axis and prismatic effect, and mark uncut lenses. The instrument's own accuracy is verified against reference lenses under ISO 9342-1. A clinic's lensmeter is therefore a calibrated instrument whose readings are traceable, and the 0.25 D increments on its scale describe display resolution rather than measurement uncertainty — those are different claims and only one of them is being certified.

What this means in practice

If you take a pair back because something feels wrong, the useful request is not “check the prescription”. It is “check the centration and the axis as mounted”. Those are the measurements a lensmeter alone will not catch, and the ones that produce the edge blur, the swim, and the headache that survive a correct power reading. If the mires do read clean and the problem persists, the next question is geometric, and our article on vertex distance and optical centre height covers the numbers involved.

Why both lenses get measured separately

A lensmeter reads one lens at a time, and a dispenser will verify each separately even when the prescription is identical. The reason is that a pair is not simply two lenses: a swapped lens, a mis-paired blank, or an axis ground to the wrong side of the frame will only surface when the two readings are set side by side. Checking the right lens against the right eye's prescription, then the left against the left, takes under a minute and catches the errors that a single “looks fine” reading never will.

This is also where a customer can help. If you know your right and left powers differ, say so when you hand the glasses over. It focuses the check on the one comparison most likely to reveal a problem.
