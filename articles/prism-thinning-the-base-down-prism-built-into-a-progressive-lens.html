Prism Thinning: The Base-Down Prism Built Into a Progressive Lens

November 11, 2026 · 9 min read

A prism reading that is not on your prescription

Put a new pair of progressive lenses on a lensmeter, read it at the prism reference point, and you may find one or two prism dioptres of base-down prism sitting there. The prescription the optometrist wrote contains no prism at all. Nothing is wrong with the glasses. What you are looking at is prism thinning, a surfacing decision made by the laboratory so that a progressive lens does not become a thick, heavy wedge along the bottom edge.

It is one of the few things about your glasses decided entirely by manufacturing arithmetic rather than by how well you see, which is why it explains two questions that come up constantly: why a lens can measure prism it was never prescribed, and why an optician can say a different lens design will make the bottom edge thinner without changing your prescription by a single quarter dioptre.

Why a reading addition turns the bottom edge into the problem

A progressive lens carries the distance power at the top and an addition at the bottom. The addition is plus power, and plus power means a steeper surface curve and more material inside that curve. In a progressive the near zone sits low in the lens, so the extra material collects along the lower edge. The finished lens is thin at the top edge, correct at the fitting cross, and thickest at the bottom — the mirror image of a plain single-vision minus lens, which is thickest at the outer edge.

Two consequences follow. The bottom edge sets the maximum thickness of the lens, and with it the weight and how far the lens protrudes from the frame. And because the two lenses are mirror images, both bottom edges grow together, so a pair gets heavier without either lens looking obviously wrong. A +2.00 add is a modest optical change and a large mechanical one.

What the laboratory does about it

The fix is to surface the lens with a small amount of base-down prism. Prism is named for its base: a prism with the base at the bottom displaces the image downward and bends light in one consistent direction across the whole aperture. Grinding base-down prism into a lens means changing the relationship between the front curve and the back curve, and the practical effect is that the lens no longer uses the blank symmetrically. Material is taken off the bottom and a little more is left at the top.

The trade is deliberate and usually worth making. The bottom edge, which was the thickest part, loses material; the top edge, which had room to spare, gains a little. Maximum thickness falls, weight falls, and the lens sits better in the frame. The amount is calculated by the laboratory's surfacing software from the distance power, the addition, the frame shape and the material, and it is not something a wearer orders or refuses. Because the same direction is applied to both eyes, the prism is yoked: both eyes receive the same base direction, so the binocular system is not asked to reconcile a difference between them.

Prentice's rule, and why one millimetre is not a small thing

The link between decentration and prism is Prentice's rule: P = c × F, where P is prism in prism dioptres, c is the distance from the optical centre in centimetres, and F is the power in dioptres in the meridian concerned. A prism dioptre is defined as a displacement of one centimetre at a distance of one metre — it is a slope rather than a length, which is why it behaves identically at every viewing distance.

A worked example: a −4.00 D lens whose optical centre sits 2 mm from the point you are looking through produces 0.2 × 4.00 = 0.80 dioptres of prism. At 3 mm the same lens gives 1.20, and at −6.00 D the 3 mm figure becomes 1.80. This is the arithmetic behind most ‘the lenses were made wrong’ complaints that turn out to be a centration question, and it is the same arithmetic that makes prism thinning measurable at all: the thinning prism is real prism, in a known direction, in a known amount.

It also shows why a lensmeter reading needs interpretation. A lens can acquire prism because someone ground it in deliberately, or because the optical centre ended up in the wrong place relative to the pupil. The instrument reports the total and cannot tell you which component is which. Only the laboratory order can.

Four things prism thinning is not

  • It is not a treatment for double vision. Therapeutic prism is prescribed in a specific direction and amount to relieve a binocular problem. Thinning prism is base-down in both eyes and exists to save material.
  • It does not cancel or replace prescribed prism. Where a prescription includes prism, the laboratory works the prescribed amount into the same lens and adjusts the thinning around it. The two are separate lines in the calculation.
  • It does not mean your prescription is wrong. A lensmeter reading taken at the fitting cross of a glazed lens will include the thinning prism, so comparing that figure directly against the written prescription is an easy way to manufacture a false alarm.
  • It does not thin the whole lens. It reduces the maximum edge thickness. Centre thickness is governed by impact and mounting requirements and does not shrink because prism was added.

When the compensation is worth asking about

Occasionally the correction is noticeable. With a high plus distance power, a high addition, prescribed prism, or a large difference between the two eyes, the induced prism can be felt as a slight vertical shift when the glasses first go on, or as a difference in feel between the old pair and the new one. Most wearers settle within days, precisely because the prism is equal on both sides. If the shift persists, if one lens seems to sit higher than the other, or if you are tilting your head to find the clear zone, that is a verification question rather than proof of a bad prescription: ask for both lenses to be checked on a lensmeter at the fitting cross and compared with the order.

When you are choosing a design for a high addition, it is worth asking directly whether prism thinning is used and how much, because it changes finished thickness more than most upgrade wording does.
