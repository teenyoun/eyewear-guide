Night Myopia: Why Your Focus Drifts When the Light Drops

November 02, 2026 · 8 min read

Two different things happen when the light drops

Most explanations of night vision blur two separate mechanisms together. The first is adaptation: the retina switches from cone to rod dominance, sensitivity climbs, and it takes roughly twenty to thirty minutes in true darkness to reach the limit of that process. The second is optical, and it is the subject here. The eye's focus changes when the light goes down. That change has a name — night myopia, or twilight myopia — and it is the reason a pair of glasses that is perfect in a bright examination room can feel marginal on an unlit road.

Adaptation determines how much light you can use. Night myopia determines where that light is focused. They are independent, and only the second one can be addressed with a lens.

Where the myopic shift comes from

Three contributions stack up, and they all point the same way.

Accommodation drifts to its resting point. The ciliary muscle does not relax to optical infinity when there is nothing to look at. In a dim, low-contrast field the accommodative system settles at what is called the dark focus or tonic accommodation — a finite distance, typically somewhere around a dioptre of accommodation for many people, though it varies widely between individuals and with age. If your eye is focused at one metre while you are looking at a road sign at forty, the sign is out of focus by the difference.

The pupil opens, and the periphery of the lens is the weak part. Pupil diameter roughly doubles in the dark, from something like two to three millimetres in daylight to six or seven at night. A larger aperture increases depth of field, which is helpful, and simultaneously admits rays that pass through the outer zones of the cornea and crystalline lens, where spherical aberration is strongest. In most eyes that aberration is positive, which pushes the best focus myopic. Depth of field buys tolerance; aberration spends it.

The spectrum shifts. Rods peak in sensitivity near 507 nm, well into the blue-green, so scotopic vision is relatively more sensitive to short wavelengths — the Purkinje shift. Because the eye's chromatic aberration focuses blue light in front of red, a blue-weighted signal carries a small myopic bias. The effect is modest on its own and adds to the other two.

What the size of the shift means in practice

Half a dioptre is a reasonable rule of thumb for how much the focus moves at night, and it varies enormously with pupil size, age, accommodative tone and whether the eye is corrected at all. Half a dioptre sounds trivial until you consider what it does to a distant sign at low contrast: the blur circle grows, contrast falls, and the threshold at which you can read the sign moves closer. On a dry night with clean optics you may never notice. In rain, with oncoming headlights scattering off a wet road and a dirty windscreen, the same half dioptre is the difference between reading a junction sign in time and guessing.

Low luminance also degrades contrast sensitivity independently of focus, and stray light from headlights reduces the effective contrast of everything else in the field. Two people with identical prescriptions can therefore have very different experiences of night driving.

Why a separate night prescription is usually the wrong answer

The intuitive fix is to add minus power for night use. It generally backfires. Over-minusing pushes the far point inside infinity for daytime viewing, which forces accommodation to work harder to see distance, and in a younger eye it can trigger the very accommodative tone that caused the problem. Glasses that are correct for daylight are correct at night; what changes is the quality of the retinal image, not the required power.

What does help is unglamorous. Keep the prescription accurate rather than merely close. Keep the lenses clean and free of scratches, because scatter is worse when the signal is weak. Choose an anti-reflective coating, which matters more at night than in daylight because it removes the ghost images of oncoming lights from the lens surfaces. Avoid tints above category 1 for night driving, and avoid yellow "night driving" lenses altogether: they cut blue light, which reduces total luminance, and any contrast gain they offer to some wearers does not come from making the scene brighter.

Five claims that do not survive scrutiny

"Night myopia means you have become short-sighted." It is a shift in the eye's focusing state under low light, not a change in the refractive error measured under standard conditions. It resolves when the lighting does.

"Yellow lenses make night driving brighter." A filter cannot add light. A yellow lens removes short wavelengths, lowering the total reaching the retina, and can slightly improve contrast for some observers by removing the most defocused wavelengths. That is a trade, not a gain.

"Everyone should have night driving glasses." Most people drive at night perfectly well with an accurate prescription and clean lenses. A separate pair is justified by a measured need, not by the existence of the product category.

"Your eyes adjust to the dark in a few seconds." Cone adaptation takes a few minutes and rod adaptation continues for twenty to thirty minutes. Walking from a lit house to a dark road, you are still adapting for most of the first kilometre.

"Astigmatism disappears at night." The opposite tendency is more common: a larger pupil exposes more of the astigmatic periphery, and the axis becomes more visible as starbursts and streaking around point lights.

When the problem is not night myopia

See an optometrist rather than buying a tinted lens if you notice any of the following: halos or rings around lights rather than simple blur; starbursts that have appeared or worsened over months; ghosting or double images from one eye; a marked difference between the two eyes at night; difficulty that persists in good lighting; or a general sense that lights are too bright and scattering, which can be an early sign of lenticular change rather than a refractive one. Those patterns point to causes — cataract, corneal irregularity, uncorrected cylinder, dry eye — that no over-the-counter product will fix, and each has a different management.

If your daytime vision is comfortable and only night driving is difficult, the useful first step is a check of the prescription you already have, including how well the optical centres line up with your pupils in the frame you actually wear. A lens that is a quarter of a dioptre off, or decentred by three millimetres in a strong prescription, produces exactly the kind of marginal night vision that sends people shopping for night driving glasses when an adjustment would have done more.
