Anti-Reflective Coating vs Blue Light Filter — Worth Paying for Both?

﻿

They solve two different problems

Order glasses online and you meet the same two add-ons: anti-reflective (AR) coating and blue light filtering, frequently bundled under one label like "digital protection". They are not two grades of the same thing. One is a reflectance problem — light bouncing off the lens instead of passing through it. The other is a spectral problem — which wavelengths reach the retina.

AR coating: a 4% problem solved by interference

An uncoated lens surface reflects, and the fraction is set by the refractive index step between lens and air. At normal incidence it follows the Fresnel relation:

R = [(nlens − 1) / (nlens + 1)]²

For CR-39 (n ≈ 1.50) that works out to about 4% per surface, or roughly 8% for a finished pair. Polycarbonate (n ≈ 1.59) reflects about 5% per surface, and a 1.74 high-index lens about 7% — around 14% for the pair. Two consequences follow. A measurable share of the light you paid for never arrives, and the light that bounces off the back surface lands on your retina as veiling glare sitting on top of the image. That is why uncoated lenses fail hardest against a bright window, and at night.

AR coating cancels reflection rather than absorbing it. A film whose optical thickness is one quarter wavelength — physical thickness t = λ/(4n) — returns a reflection half a cycle out of phase with the reflection from the lens surface, and the two interfere destructively. A single layer cancels one narrow band, which leaves a purple cast; production coatings are multilayer stacks of alternating high- and low-index materials holding reflectance below about 1% across the visible range and pushing luminous transmittance above 98%.

Durability is specified rather than claimed: ISO 8980-4 defines the abrasion, adhesion and environmental tests an AR coating must survive. That is the real difference between a cheap coating and an expensive one — not the optics, which are solved, but how long the stack lasts.

Blue light filtering: absorption or reflection

Two mechanisms, with different side effects. An absorptive filter puts a pigment in the substrate or a dye in the coating that absorbs short wavelengths and converts them to heat; because absorption is broadband, the colour shift is largest. A reflective filter uses an interference coating tuned to reject the blue band, showing a blue-violet reflex on the surface. It can be narrower, but it also reflects ambient blue from the back surface toward the eye, producing a faint blue haze in bright conditions.

Both are described by a spectral transmittance curve, not a percentage. "Blocks 92% of blue light" is meaningless without knowing which band, and whether the figure is a peak, an average or an eye-weighted value — three numbers that can differ by a factor of several on the same lens.

The cost cannot be engineered away. Removing short wavelengths lowers luminous transmittance (τv) — the share of visible light reaching the eye, weighted to the photopic response — and shifts colour appearance. EN ISO 12312-1 grades tinted lenses by τv: category 0 is 80–100%, category 1 is 43–80%, category 2 is 18–43%. A filter that trims only the 415–455 nm shoulder usually stays in category 0 and loses a few points of transmission; a heavy amber gaming lens can drop into category 1, which is exactly why it looks dim under room light.

Reading the spec sheet

Three questions separate a real product from a label. Which band and what weighting: a figure for 400–500 nm and one for a 20 nm slice around 450 nm are not the same claim, and neither is comparable across brands. What is τv after coating: below roughly 80% you are buying a tinted lens, not a clear one. Does the AR meet ISO 8980-4: an unqualified "anti-glare" often means a single layer that scratches within a year.

On value, AR is usually the cheapest add-on on the menu and the one with the most obvious everyday payoff — it changes what you see from the first minute. Blue filtering is normally priced at or above it while doing its work in a two-hour window before sleep.

The one case to avoid blue filtering

Night driving. A blue-absorbing lens lowers τv and removes short-wavelength energy that rod vision depends on — scotopic sensitivity peaks near 507 nm, so the band you cut overlaps the band your night vision uses. After dark you want the opposite: maximum transmission plus AR, which removes the veiling glare that turns oncoming headlights into flares. Keep the filtered pair for the desk and a clear AR pair for the car; several jurisdictions also set a minimum luminous transmittance for lenses worn while driving, which an amber lens may not meet.

Myths worth clearing up

  • "AR coating is a blue light filter — both look purple." The reflex colour only tells you which band is being reflected; AR stacks also leave green or blue-green residuals by design. Only the transmittance curve settles what a lens does.
  • "Both together is always better." True at a desk. False in a car, where the filter costs you contrast.
  • "A higher blocking percentage protects the retina." Daylight screen exposure sits far below the photochemical exposure limits used in safety guidelines. The defensible benefit is circadian timing, not retinal protection.
  • "Blue filter coatings are as durable as AR." Different stacks: pigment absorbers are more susceptible to fading with prolonged UV exposure than an interference coating.
  • "Everyone who works at a computer needs blue filtering." The evidence for daytime strain is weak; the clean win is the evening window. If your screen time ends at 5 pm, skip it.

What to order

  1. AR coating on every pair. Best value on the add-on list, and it helps in every lighting condition.
  2. Clear lenses with AR for driving and for any pair you wear outdoors in daylight.
  3. Blue filtering only on the evening pair — the one you wear for the two hours before bed, and for gaming sessions that run late.
  4. Ask for the band and τv rather than accepting a percentage, and ask whether the coating meets ISO 8980-4.

Bottom line

AR is the upgrade that earns its price: it improves every hour of wear and every lighting condition, and the physics behind it is not controversial. Blue filtering is a narrow tool for a narrow window — the hours before sleep — and it actively works against you behind a windscreen at night. Buy AR unconditionally. Buy blue light only if you use screens late, and never on the pair you drive in.
