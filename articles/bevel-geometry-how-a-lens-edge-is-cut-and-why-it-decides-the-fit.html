Bevel Geometry: How a Lens Edge Is Cut and Why It Decides the Fit

November 02, 2026 · 9 min read

The edge is a joint, not a finish

Surfacing gets the attention: the front and back curves, the free-form generator, the polishing. But a finished lens blank is optically useless until its outline is cut and its edge is shaped to mate with the frame. That shaping is called edging, and the geometry it produces — the bevel — is a structural joint. A lens that is optically perfect and bevelled badly will sit proud of the frame, chip at the thinnest point of the shape, or crack in the groove months later.

The reason this is worth understanding is that almost every complaint about "the lens looks thick" or "the lens cracked by itself" traces back to the numeric relationship between edge thickness, frame groove and decentration, rather than to the lens material.

The three dimensions of an edge

An edged lens has an outline, a thickness at every point along that outline, and a profile across the thickness. The profile is the bevel:

  • Bevel height is how far the V rises from the front surface to the apex, measured across the edge. It has to be smaller than the local edge thickness, or the apex runs off the edge and the lens cannot seat.
  • Bevel position is where the apex sits between the front and back surfaces. It is set so that the lens sits flush with the frame front, which is what determines the effective vertex distance and whether the cosmetic result looks right from the side.
  • Bevel angle is the included angle of the V. It must match the frame's internal groove, because the two flanks of the V bear the load. A mismatch does not look obviously wrong; it just leaves the lens sitting on one flank, which is where stress cracking begins.

The groove in a metal eyewire is shallow — a fraction of a millimetre — so the margin for error is set by how much material exists at the thinnest point of the chosen shape, not by the average thickness of the lens.

Why the edge is thin exactly where you do not want it to be

Edge thickness is not uniform around the outline. For a minus lens, thickness grows towards the edge in proportion to how far that edge is from the optical centre; for a plus lens, the edge is thinnest where the shape is closest to the optical centre. Once the lens is decentred into the frame — which is what happens whenever the frame's geometric centre is not in front of the pupil — the thickness distribution shifts and rotates with it.

That is the mechanism behind "the same prescription looks thicker in these frames": change the eye size or the pupillary distance and you change the distance from the optical centre to the furthest point of the outline. It also explains the classic failure — a strong minus lens in a large angular frame is thinnest at the temporal corner, the point most exposed to knocks.

Lens blanks arrive in standard diameters — commonly 65, 70, 75 and 80 mm — and the blank has to be big enough to contain the decentred outline plus enough edge left for a bevel. That minimum blank size calculation is why an unusually large frame in a high prescription sometimes cannot be made in the thinnest material at all. Four edge profiles and what each is for

V-bevel is the standard for a rimmed frame: two flanks meeting at an apex, seating in the eyewire groove, placed to within a fraction of a millimetre so the lens sits flush.

Flat edge with a safety chamfer is used for rimless mountings, where the edge is visible and the lens is held by screws through drilled holes. A sharp 90-degree edge is fragile, so the corners are broken.

Grooved edge is used for nylon-cord mountings: a groove is cut into the edge and the cord seats in it. This needs more edge thickness than a V-bevel, because the groove removes material from the middle of the edge rather than shaping the outside of it.

Polished versus matte edge. A polished edge also acts as a light pipe. Light entering the polished edge is transmitted into the lens and can emerge towards the eye as a bright ring or as veiling glare at the periphery. A matte or slightly frosted edge scatters that light instead. Polishing is a cosmetic choice, not an upgrade.

What actually causes the failures people blame on materials

"The lens chipped on its own." Almost always an edge that was thinner than the bevel required, or a bevel apex placed too far forward so the lens seated on one flank and the other flank carried a point load. The chip appears at the thinnest part of the outline, usually the top-temporal corner.

"The lens rattles." Bevel angle mismatch, or a bevel height that does not fill the groove. The frame closes, the lens moves.

"The lens cracked in the groove after a few months." A point contact plus thermal cycling. The V-groove is a stress riser by design; if the lens is seated on one flank only, the contact line takes the entire load of the frame's spring tension and every temperature change.

"Polycarbonate is brittle." Polycarbonate is highly impact-resistant in the plane of the lens and considerably less so at a thin, bevelled edge where the material is a fraction of a millimetre thick. Edge geometry can defeat a tough material.

Four assumptions worth dropping

"A thinner lens always looks thinner in the frame." It looks thinner where the outline is far from the optical centre. In a small, round frame, the difference between two indices largely disappears; in a large rectangular frame it is obvious.

"Polished edges are premium, so always polish." On a high minus lens, polishing the edge creates the bright ring. Opticians often deliberately leave it matte for exactly this reason.

"Chipping is a coating fault." Coatings craze; edges chip. A chip that follows the edge contour and exposes bare material is a geometry problem.

"Any frame can be glazed in any prescription." Minimum blank size, groove depth and edge thickness impose hard limits. A lab that says a shape is not possible is usually reporting arithmetic, not unwillingness.

What to check when you collect a pair

Look along the frame from the side: the lens edge should sit flush with, or a hair behind, the frame front. Run a fingernail around the inside of the eyewire — a lens seated on one flank often leaves a visible gap on the other side of the groove. Check the thinnest corner of the shape for a whitish line, which is stress whitening rather than a scratch. And if the frame is rimless, confirm the screws are snug but not torqued down onto the lens.

If a lens has chipped at the edge, cracked from a drill hole or started to rattle, take it back. Reglazing with a slightly thicker edge or a less extreme shape is often the fix, and that is a fitter's decision.
