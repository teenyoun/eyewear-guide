What a Magnifier's X Number Does Not Tell You: Power, Distance and Field

November 25, 2026 · 9 min read

The number on the handle is a label, not a measurement

Magnifiers are sold by a number followed by an X: 2X, 3X, 5X, 10X. The number is not standardised between manufacturers, it does not describe what you will see at the distance you actually hold the lens, and it does not tell you the one thing that decides whether the tool is usable — how much of the page fits in the window. Two magnifiers both marked 3X can behave quite differently, and neither may match the arithmetic you would do from the lens power stamped on the rim.

There is a way through this, and it comes from the same optics that govern reading glasses. Once you know the two formulas, the marketing number becomes a rough guide and the physics becomes the decision.

Principle: magnification is a comparison, not a property

Nothing is magnified in isolation. A lens produces an image of a certain angular size, and magnification is the ratio between that angular size and the angular size the same object would have in a defined reference condition. For near vision the conventional reference is an object held 25 cm from the eye — roughly the comfortable reading distance of a young adult, and the distance at which a power of +4.00 D brings print into focus.

From that reference, two relationships follow, and they are worth memorising because they cover most low-vision arithmetic:

  • Trade magnification equals the lens power in dioptres divided by four. A +8.00 D lens gives about 2X; +12.00 D about 3X; +20.00 D about 5X.
  • Working distance in centimetres is approximately 100 divided by the power in dioptres. +8.00 D focuses at about 12.5 cm; +12.00 D at about 8 cm; +20.00 D at 5 cm.

The second formula is the one people forget, and it is the one that decides whether a magnifier is practical. High power is only available at a short working distance. At 5 cm, the lens is close enough that the page must be lit from the side to avoid shadowing it with your own hand, and both eyes cannot comfortably converge on the same point.

Equivalent viewing distance: the cleaner way to think about it

Instead of asking "how many X", clinicians ask what equivalent viewing distance a system provides — the distance at which the object would have to be placed, with no optical aid, to subtend the same angle. A +12.00 D lens used as a simple magnifier gives an equivalent viewing distance of about 8 cm, meaning the print looks the way it would if you could focus at 8 cm. This framing has two practical advantages. It compares a spectacle add, a hand magnifier and a stand magnifier on one scale, and it makes the cost of magnification explicit: every step closer is a step away from comfortable binocular reading.

It also explains why stand magnifiers often disappoint. A stand fixes the lens-to-page distance, but the wearer's eye sits some distance above the lens, and that eye-to-lens gap changes the effective power. A "5X" stand magnifier may deliver considerably less than 5X at the eye position it actually encourages, and the rated number usually describes the lens alone. Hand magnifiers have the same problem in reverse: held closer to the eye they gain field, held further they gain nothing.

Field of view is the real trade

Doubling magnification halves the linear field for a lens of the same aperture. That is not a manufacturing limitation; it is geometry. The usable area of the page visible through the lens shrinks roughly in proportion to the power, and the amount of scanning required rises with it. At 2X you can read a line without moving the lens much. At 6X you are reading two or three words at a time and moving the lens constantly, which is tiring in a way that a single reading session does not reveal but a whole afternoon does.

This is why the correct answer for sustained reading is usually the lowest magnification that makes the text legible, combined with better lighting and higher contrast, rather than the highest magnification available. Contrast and illuminance buy legibility without any optical cost. A page lit to a high level with a modest magnifier is easier to read than the same page at 6X in shadow.

Common misconceptions

"More X is always better." Beyond the point where the text is legible, extra magnification costs field, working distance, posture and binocularity, and gains nothing.

"The X number is a standard unit." It is not. Some makers quote lens power divided by four; others quote a nominal figure tied to a stand design; some round generously. The lens power in dioptres, if stated, is the reliable number.

"A magnifier replaces an eye examination." A magnifier compensates for an optical deficit; it does not identify one. A change in the magnification you need can signal a change in the eye, and acquiring stronger magnifiers instead of a test is the commonest way to lose time.

"Reading glasses and magnifiers are the same tool." A spectacle add is worn at a fixed vertex distance and keeps both hands free and both eyes usable; a hand magnifier is held and usually monocular at high power. They solve different problems, and many people need both, for different tasks.

When to ask for a proper low vision assessment

If you find yourself holding print closer than about 15 cm, if you need different magnifications for different tasks, if one eye has become clearly worse than the other, or if you are tilting your head or the page to read, that is the point to have a low vision assessment rather than another online purchase. The assessment measures acuity at the distance you actually read, tests contrast sensitivity, and matches a device to the task — reading a letter, reading a label, watching television — because the right tool for a medicine label is almost never the right tool for a novel. Bring the tasks, not just the symptom.
