Solar Eclipse Glasses: What ISO 12312-2 Actually Certifies

November 11, 2026 · 8 min read

Why the filter has to be extreme

Looking at the Sun is not like looking at a bright lamp. The retina has no pain receptors, so the exposure produces no warning signal at the moment it happens. The damage — solar retinopathy — is photochemical and thermal injury concentrated at the macula, the small area responsible for fine vision, and it typically declares itself hours later as a central blur or a dark spot. There is no established treatment. The only effective intervention is not doing it.

That asymmetry is what a solar filter is designed around. It does not need to make the Sun comfortable. It needs to reduce the light reaching the retina by enough that the exposure sits below the threshold for injury even during a long partial phase, while still transmitting enough for the Sun's disc to be visible.

The number: 0.0032 percent

The relevant document is ISO 12312-2, the standard for filters for direct observation of the Sun. Its central requirement is a luminous transmittance window: the filter must transmit no more than 0.0032 percent of visible light, and no less than about 0.000061 percent.

Those are not percentages in the everyday sense. 0.0032 percent is a transmittance of 3.2 × 10⁻⁵, which corresponds to an optical density of roughly 4.5. The lower bound works out to about optical density 6.2. So a compliant filter sits in a band roughly between four and a half and six orders of magnitude of attenuation — a factor of about fifty between the brightest and darkest permitted filter.

Both limits do work. The ceiling is the safety requirement. The floor exists because a filter that transmits nothing usable encourages the wearer to take it off, peek around it, or conclude the product is faulty — behaviours that produce exactly the exposure the standard is trying to prevent.

The standard is not only about visible light. It also sets limits on ultraviolet and infrared transmittance, and it requires the product to carry identification: the standard number, the manufacturer, and instructions. A filter that claims the number without identifying who made it has told you nothing verifiable.

Why sunglasses cannot substitute

A category 4 sunglass lens, the darkest category in the European system, transmits somewhere between 3 and 8 percent of visible light. The ceiling for a solar filter is 0.0032 percent. The gap is close to a thousandfold.

This is the number to keep in mind when someone offers a workaround. Stacking two pairs of sunglasses does not multiply attenuation in a controlled way and does nothing reliable for infrared. Exposed photographic film, smoked glass, compact discs and polarising film have no specified transmittance at all. The eye will feel more comfortable behind them, and comfort is precisely the wrong signal, because the receptors that would report damage are not the ones reporting brightness.

How to tell whether a pair is real

  • Look at the marking. A compliant viewer carries the ISO 12312-2 reference plus a manufacturer name. The number alone, printed on an unbranded card, is a claim rather than evidence.
  • Test indoors. Through a genuine solar filter you should see essentially nothing of the room — only the Sun itself, or a very intense point source such as an LED torch or a bare filament. If you can see furniture, the filter is not a solar filter.
  • Inspect the surface. Pinholes, creases, delamination and scratches are all reasons to discard. The filter works by being continuous.
  • Buy from a supplier that can name the testing laboratory. Certification is a laboratory result, not a design intention.

Common misconceptions, corrected

NASA approves eclipse glasses. NASA does not certify or approve commercial eclipse products, and no manufacturer holds a NASA endorsement. The organisation that maintains lists of reputable suppliers is the American Astronomical Society.

Old glasses expire after three years. That guidance applied to viewers made before the 2015 standard, whose UV-blocking layers could degrade. Viewers manufactured to ISO 12312-2 remain usable indefinitely provided they are undamaged — which is why the inspection matters more than the date.

During totality you still need the glasses. This is the one place where the rule reverses. During the brief total phase of a total eclipse, when the Sun's photosphere is completely covered, it is safe to look directly at the corona with the naked eye. The glasses go back on before the first bead of photosphere reappears, and the safe window is measured in the seconds around that transition, not in minutes of guesswork.

A filter on the eyepiece protects a camera or binoculars. It does not. Magnified optics concentrate energy, and an eyepiece filter receives that concentrated beam and can crack. Solar filters for optical instruments belong over the front aperture, and looking through an unfiltered eyepiece of an instrument pointed at the Sun is dangerous regardless of what is on the other end.

Any dark filter is a solar filter. Welding filters, photographic ND filters and astronomical filters are each specified for something else. Some are genuinely suitable; the only way to know is that the item states compliance with a standard for solar observation.

What to do if exposure happens

If you looked at the Sun without a compliant filter, or through one that turned out to be damaged, stop and rest the eyes. Seek assessment promptly if you notice a central blur, a dark or distorted spot in the middle of vision, distorted straight lines, or altered colour perception — particularly if it appears in the hours after the exposure rather than immediately. Distortion of straight lines can be checked with an Amsler grid, which is a screening aid rather than a diagnosis.

ISO 12312-2 is a narrow, unglamorous standard about one number and its verification. That narrowness is the point: for direct observation of the Sun, there is no safe middle ground between a certified filter and not looking.
