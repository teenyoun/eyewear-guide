What Lives in Your Glasses Case: Biofilm, Grit and Micro-Abrasion

November 24, 2026 · 8 min read

The safest place for your glasses is also the dirtiest

A case exists to protect lenses from impact and dust. What it also does is create a small, dark, humid, warmly insulated box containing skin oils, sebum, dust, and whatever the lenses picked up during the day. Close it, put it in a bag, and you have built an incubator with an abrasive floor. The damage that follows is rarely dramatic. It accumulates as a faint veil of fine scratches that appears a year later, when no single event can be blamed.

Understanding the two mechanisms — biological and mechanical — explains why the usual advice ("keep them in the case") is only half the instruction.

Principle one: a closed damp box grows a biofilm

Micro-organisms do not need a puddle. They need a surface, moisture, and a food film, and a spectacle case supplies all three. Skin cells, sebum and cosmetic residue transfer to the lenses and to the lining; moisture comes from the air, from the nose pads, and from lenses put away still damp after cleaning. On the lining and on the frame, bacteria and fungi organise into a biofilm — a community of cells embedded in a self-produced matrix of polysaccharide that adheres to the surface and resists simple rinsing. A biofilm is not removed by wiping the visible dirt away; the matrix has to be broken down mechanically or with detergent.

The optical consequence is indirect but real. A biofilm on the lining holds moisture against the frame, keeps the case humid, and provides a reservoir that re-contaminates the lenses within hours of cleaning them. For most people this is a hygiene annoyance. For anyone prone to blepharitis, recurrent styes, or skin sensitivity around the nose and temples, a contaminated case is a plausible part of the cycle, and it is the cheapest part to fix.

Principle two: grit turns a soft cloth into sandpaper

Lens plastics sit low on the Mohs hardness scale, which ranks materials by which one scratches which. Silica — the main constituent of sand, dust and many polishing compounds — is around 7. Optical plastics are far softer, in the region of 2 to 3, and the hard coat on top of them is harder than the plastic but still well below silica. A particle of quartz trapped in a microfibre cloth therefore does not need to be large to cut: it is harder than everything it is dragged across.

The mechanism has a name in tribology: three-body abrasion. Two surfaces — lens and cloth — plus a third body of particles between them. In two-body abrasion the softer surface wears predictably; with a third body present, the wear rate depends on particle size, shape and how much pressure is applied. This is why the same cloth that cleans perfectly on a clean lens can leave a ring of fine scratches after a day at the beach, and why the inside of a case with a felt lining is an effective particle trap: the lining holds grit in contact with the lens for hours.

The coating stack is thin enough that this matters more than intuition suggests. A complete anti-reflective stack plus hard coat and hydrophobic top layer is measured in micrometres — a fraction of the thickness of a human hair. A single grain of sand is commonly an order of magnitude thicker than the entire stack. Once a particle has cut through the top layer, the hydrophobic property is gone locally, cleaning becomes harder in exactly that spot, and the scratch acts as a reservoir for the next round of dirt.

What actually helps

  • Never store lenses damp. Rinse, shake off excess water, and let them air-dry open for a few minutes before closing the case. Moisture is the variable you control most easily.
  • Wash the case, not just the glasses. Warm water with a drop of mild soap, worked into the lining, then rinsed and dried fully open. Do this monthly, and immediately after any trip involving sand, dust or sunscreen.
  • Retire felt and foam linings. A lined case that has been through a beach holiday is a grit reservoir. Linings cannot be cleaned reliably; cases are cheap.
  • Keep the cloth separate. A cloth folded inside the case sits against the lenses and collects whatever the frame carries. Store it in a pocket or a separate pouch, and wash it regularly — microfibre is washable, and a dirty cloth is the most common single cause of the "mystery" scratches people blame on the coating.
  • Do not store in the bathroom. Steam, aerosol sprays and constant humidity make it the worst room in the house for both the case and the coating stack.

Common misconceptions

"The case protects the lenses, so anything stored in it is safe." The case protects against impact and light dust. It also concentrates particles against the lens surface and holds humidity. Protection and contamination are the same object.

"Microfibre cloths do not scratch." The fibre does not; the particles it carries do. Fibre quality affects how well it lifts and holds grit, not whether grit can cut.

"A lens spray is enough to clean them." Spray and cloth move contamination around. If grit is present, the first pass is the damaging one; rinse under running water before any cloth touches the lens.

"Scratches are the coating's fault." Coating failures do happen — crazing from heat, adhesion loss from solvents — but the uniform haze of fine lines on a lens used daily is usually abrasion, and abrasion needs a particle.

When the damage is past cleaning

If a lens has a localised patch where water no longer beads, or a scatter of fine lines that becomes obvious against a bright window at night, the coating has been breached and no cleaning routine will restore it. The practical test is glare: scratches scatter light, and scattered light is what turns oncoming headlights into a haze rather than a point. At that stage the decision is a replacement lens, not a new cloth. If the frame itself is corroding at the hinges or the nose pads, or if you have recurrent eyelid irritation, have the frame and the case assessed together — the two problems usually share a cause.
