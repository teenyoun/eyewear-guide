Office Progressive Lenses: Why Standard Progressives Fail at a Desk

October 05, 2026 · 9 min read

A progressive lens is built around an assumption, and the assumption is where you look

A general-purpose progressive lens is a single surface carrying three optical zones stacked vertically. The upper part is the distance zone, ground for looking at things far away. The lower part is the near zone, ground to add plus power for close work. Between them runs a narrow transition band, usually called the corridor or intermediate zone, where the power changes progressively from one to the other.

That design is a set of compromises, and the compromise that matters most at a desk is the width of the corridor. A general-purpose progressive has to give away enough of the lens to distance and near that the middle band stays relatively narrow. It works well for the way most people use glasses: look up to see across a room, look down to read. It works badly for the way most people use a computer, which is to hold a fixed gaze at an intermediate distance for hours.

Where your screen actually sits, in dioptres

Working distance and lens power are linked by a simple relationship: power in dioptres equals one divided by the distance in metres. That single equation explains the whole problem.

  • A near addition of +2.00 D is ground to bring the near point to 1 / 2.00 = 0.50 m, that is 50 cm.
  • An addition of +2.50 D corresponds to 40 cm; +1.50 D to roughly 67 cm.
  • Reading a book is typically done somewhere around 35 to 45 cm. A desktop monitor usually sits further out, commonly in the region of 50 to 70 cm, with a laptop closer and a large screen further.

So if your addition is +2.50 D and your screen is at 65 cm, the screen is not where your lens is sharpest. To read it clearly you either push the screen closer, lean in, or tip your head up to find the corridor. All three are common, and all three are uncomfortable over a working day.

What an office or occupational lens changes

Occupational lenses - sold under various names such as office, desk, near-variable or indoor progressives - start from a different assumption: that the wearer is indoors, looking at things between roughly arm's length and a few metres. The design response is to reduce the power range between the top and bottom of the lens and to spend the space that frees up on a much wider intermediate zone.

The reduced top-to-bottom power change is usually described as degression: instead of running all the way from plano to the full near addition, the lens degresses only part of the way. That buys corridor width and comfortable distance vision out to a few metres, at the cost of clear distance vision beyond that. An office lens is not designed for driving or for watching television across a room, and that limitation is the whole point of the trade.

They are fitted differently, too

An occupational lens is not simply a different design sitting in the same position in the frame. Because its zones are laid out differently, it is fitted with a different vertical position and often a different corridor length, and the height at which your pupil sits relative to the lens matters more, not less. That makes the frame part of the optics rather than a separate decision: a shallow frame may not have the vertical depth to hold an intermediate-dominant design comfortably, while an unusually deep frame can leave your habitual gaze sitting above the zone the lens was built to put there. It is the practical reason office lenses are usually discussed together with the frame rather than ordered afterwards and squeezed into a pair you already own.

Common misconceptions

  • "Any progressive works fine at a desk." A general-purpose progressive will work, but you will be using its narrowest zone for your longest-held gaze. That is why so many people describe screen work as the least comfortable part of wearing their first pair.
  • "Computer glasses means blue-light glasses." These are unrelated. An occupational lens solves a geometry problem - where the correction is placed on the lens for a particular working distance. A blue-light coating addresses a spectral question. Asking for the first will not get you the second, and a blue-light coating does not widen the corridor.
  • "A stronger reading addition will make the screen sharper." Only if the screen is at the distance that addition is ground for. Increasing the addition when the screen is too far away makes the mismatch worse, not better - the dioptre-to-distance relationship runs both ways.
  • "Office lenses are an upgrade over normal progressives." They are a specialisation. If you need one pair for everything, an occupational lens will let you down at distance.

When to ask a professional

Ask a dispensing optician if you spend several hours a day at a screen and find yourself tilting your head, leaning in, or holding a monitor closer than is comfortable. The most useful thing you can bring is a measurement rather than an opinion: the actual distance from your eye to the screen, in centimetres, measured in your normal sitting position. That number, next to your prescription and your current addition, tells the optician whether a general-purpose progressive is simply ground for a working distance you never actually use. Also raise it if you already wear progressives and notice that the screen is the one thing that never feels properly in focus, because that is a fitting question rather than an adaptation problem, and no amount of perseverance will fix a corridor that points at the wrong distance.
