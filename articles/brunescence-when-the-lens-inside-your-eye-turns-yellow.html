Brunescence: When the Lens Inside Your Eye Turns Yellow

November 15, 2026 · 9 min read

The filter you did not choose

Most conversations about blue light begin with a screen and end with a coating you can buy. There is a third filter in the system, and it sits inside the eye. The crystalline lens is a layered, living structure of protein fibres suspended behind the iris, and it is not colourless. It absorbs part of the spectrum on the way in, it does so unevenly, and the amount it absorbs is not fixed for life.

Because the change takes decades, almost nobody notices it happening. The visual system recalibrates continuously, so a filter that took thirty years to build feels like no filter at all. Knowing what the ageing lens does explains several things that otherwise look like contradictory advice: why an older driver needs more light and still complains about glare, and why stacking a strong blue blocker onto an already yellowed lens is a different proposition from doing the same thing at twenty-five.

What brunescence actually is

As lens proteins age they aggregate, compact and acquire yellow-brown pigment. The name for the resulting colour is brunescence, from the Latin for brown. It is not a stain on the surface and it is not something that can be cleaned off; it is a change in the optical density of the lens nucleus itself, and it develops slowly and usually in both eyes at similar rates.

Brunescence belongs to the same process as nuclear sclerosis, the hardening and increased scattering that eventually becomes a nuclear cataract. They are not two conditions with a border between them: colour and scatter are features of one continuum, which is why a clinician at the slit lamp looks at both at once. A gradual, symmetric change is ordinary ageing; a fast or one-sided change is a reason to be examined rather than to buy glasses.

The optics: absorption that depends on wavelength

Yellow pigment absorbs short wavelengths preferentially. Visible light runs from about 400 nm at the violet end to about 750 nm at the red end, and the band people call blue occupies roughly 400 to 500 nm. A yellow filter passes the long end and removes part of the short end, which is exactly what an ageing lens does. Two quantities describe the result.

Spectral transmittance is the fraction of light at each individual wavelength that gets through a medium. A single headline figure such as 90% transmission is an average weighted by the sensitivity of the eye, and an average hides the shape of the curve: a lens can pass 90% of green light and 60% of blue light and still be advertised as 90% transmissive. The shape of the curve, not the average, is what changes the appearance of the world.

Colour constancy is the visual system's automatic correction for the spectral bias of the light it receives. The brain takes the average colour of a scene as its reference and reports deviations from that average. It is why a photograph taken under warm indoor light looks orange on the camera screen and normal a moment later, and it is why a lens that has been yellowing for twenty years does not look yellow from the inside.

What it does to vision in dim light

The retina has two receptor systems with different spectral peaks. Cones, which carry colour and fine detail in daylight, peak in sensitivity near 555 nm, in the green. Rods, which carry vision in dim light and carry no colour at all, peak closer to 498 nm, in the blue-green. The ageing lens removes light precisely where the rods are most sensitive, so the loss at dusk is larger than the loss measured in bright conditions.

Two consequences follow. The absolute threshold rises, so an older eye needs more light to see anything at all, and the shift of sensitivity toward the blue end as light falls, the Purkinje shift, is blunted at the moment it would help. This is why a room can feel too dark for reading while the same person finds daylight uncomfortably bright: one filter and one increase in scatter produce both complaints.

What it does to colour

Blues and violets look darker and less saturated than they did, whites look warmer, and a pale blue shirt may converge with a pale grey one. None of this is a defect in colour processing: the receptors report honestly on light that lost part of its blue before it reached them. It is also why tint comparisons between people of different ages are unreliable, since one observer is already looking through a stronger yellow filter than the other.

The mistake people make about blue light and age

Blue light protection matters more as you get older, so buy a stronger filter. The reasoning sounds reasonable and the conclusion is usually backwards. A yellowed lens is already a blue filter. Adding a second one stacks two absorbers in series, and stacked filters multiply rather than add: a lens transmitting 60% at 450 nm behind a coating transmitting 70% leaves about 42%.

The dominant complaint in older eyes is rarely too much blue. It is too little light and too much scatter: contrast falls, print needs more illumination, and headlights bloom. A filter that removes more short-wavelength light attacks the first of those problems and does nothing for the second.

A second version of the same error: if colours look dull, the tint in the glasses is to blame. Tints do change colour perception, but the lens inside the eye changes it first, permanently, and no coating reverses that.

Where the blue-light argument still stands

None of this makes short-wavelength light harmless. The blue-light hazard weighting used in occupational limits concentrates concern in the short-wavelength band, and that concern is about high radiance and long exposure rather than a screen at ordinary distance. The point is not that blue light is irrelevant; it is that the age-related filter is already installed, and any extra tint has a cost in light and colour that has to be weighed against a benefit that gets harder to demonstrate as the natural filter strengthens.

Evening screens are a separate question, and the better-supported argument there is about timing rather than damage: short-wavelength light is a strong signal to the circadian system, so cutting it before sleep is sensible at any age.

When to stop reading and get examined

Book an examination rather than a new pair if the change is fast, if one eye is noticeably different from the other, if colours differ between the two eyes when you cover one and then the other, if halos appear around lights at night, if glare makes driving difficult, or if print needs much more light than it did a year ago. Those patterns point at things a lens tint cannot address.

Nothing here diagnoses or treats anything; lens change is assessed by examining the eye. For everyone else the takeaway is modest: the eye is a filter that changes, the change is gradual and invisible from the inside, and the right response to a world that looks dimmer is usually more light and better contrast rather than another layer of tint.
