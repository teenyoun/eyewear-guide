Ultrasonic Cleaners for Glasses: What Cavitation Does to Your Lenses

October 13, 2026 · 8 min read

A small machine with a strong claim

Ultrasonic cleaners have become cheap enough to sit on a bathroom shelf, and they are sold with a promise that sounds like a solution to every cleaning problem: high-frequency vibration, no scrubbing, nothing left behind. It is worth understanding what the machine actually does, because the promise is partly true, partly overstated, and partly contested even among people who fit glasses for a living.

What ultrasound is, and what it is not

The name is a definition, not a description of intensity. Ultrasound is sound above the conventional upper limit of human hearing, generally taken as around 20 kilohertz. That boundary is a fact about human ears, not about power. Some ultrasonic energy is gentle; some is violent; the frequency alone does not tell you which.

A cleaner tank works by driving a transducer that vibrates the liquid at an ultrasonic frequency. If the mechanism were simply shaking grime loose, it would be unremarkable, and it would not work nearly as well as it does in practice. The real mechanism is cavitation.

Every liquid contains microscopic gas nuclei. In the low-pressure half of the sound cycle these nuclei expand into small bubbles; in the high-pressure half they collapse, and the collapse is not gentle. The bubble implodes within microseconds, and the energy concentrated into that tiny volume produces extreme local conditions — figures commonly cited in the literature are temperatures in the thousands of kelvin and pressures in the hundreds of atmospheres at the collapse point, though only for a microscopic instant and only in a vanishingly small volume. When a bubble collapses near a solid surface it tends to do so asymmetrically and drives a fine jet of liquid at the surface. That jet, repeated across the whole surface, is what lifts oil and film out of places a cloth cannot reach.

Two consequences follow directly. First, cavitation is a mechanical process, so it is not a solvent and does not dissolve anything. Second, and importantly for glasses, the energy is delivered everywhere the liquid touches, including into gaps, under coatings whose edges are already compromised, and around the plating on a frame.

What it genuinely does well

Used on suitable metal frames, an ultrasonic tank is excellent at one thing: removing oil and residue from geometry a cloth cannot follow. The bevel where the lens meets the rim, the crevices around the nose pads, the hinges, the inside of a temple arm. Skin oil trapped in those places is exactly what a microfiber cloth leaves behind, and it is what makes a frame feel like it needs cleaning a day after you cleaned it.

On the lens surface, the answer is different. Day-to-day lens cleaning is a wiped-surface problem, and a microfiber cloth with a proper lens solution solves it. The tank adds little that a careful wipe does not already achieve, while adding risks that a wipe does not have.

The contested part: does it damage coatings?

Here the sources disagree, and the disagreement is worth stating plainly rather than resolving by preference. Cleaner manufacturers and some optical practices maintain that a properly used tank does not attack optical coatings or mounting. Many practising opticians and a large volume of wearer reports say otherwise, describing coating failure, peeling or crazing, paint and plating loss on frames, loosened stones and screws, and pre-existing fine scratches becoming more visible after a tank cycle.

Both claims can be true at once, because the outcome depends on variables the sales copy does not mention. A coating with a compromised edge, a scratch that has already broken through a layer, an adhesive-bonded stone, a plated finish, an aggressive cleaning solution, a tank run hot or long: each is a plausible route to damage, and none of them is the clean, intact lens the marketing assumes. Where practitioners and manufacturers disagree, the honest position for a wearer is caution rather than confidence.

Note also what cavitation cannot do. It cannot remove a scratch, because a scratch is material loss, not material sitting on the surface, and the jet has nothing to lift. It will not restore a coating that has worn through. And it does not sterilise: it reduces contamination, but a bathroom tank is not an autoclave, and the comparison is not close.

Common misconceptions

  • "Ultrasonic is the strongest clean, so it must be the safest deep clean." Strength is the point. The same energy that lifts oil from a hinge crevice is delivered to every coating edge and bonded joint in the tank.
  • "It will get rid of the scratches." Scratches are removed material. No cleaning process, ultrasonic or otherwise, can put material back.
  • "It sterilises my glasses." It reduces contamination substantially, but it is not a sterilisation process and should not be relied on as one.
  • "Any frame can go in." Plated finishes, adhesive joints, set stones and vintage frames are all poor candidates, and the frame is often the more expensive loss.

Before you put anything in a tank

  • Coating that has already peeled, crazed or worn is a reason not to use the tank, not a reason to try it harder
  • Use only a solution intended for the purpose. Household cleaners, alcohol-heavy products and ammonia-based solutions are a separate risk from the cavitation itself
  • Keep the run short and the temperature modest, and never run a frame you cannot identify the material of
  • Scratches that affect vision are not a cleaning problem at all, and neither is a coating failure

Vision-affecting scratches, coating failure and loose or damaged frames belong with an optician, who can also tell you whether the frame is a candidate for a tank at all. If you want a cleaning routine that is safe without judgement calls, a microfiber cloth changed regularly and a lens solution formulated for coated lenses will do more for the life of your glasses than any machine.
