Blue-Light Hazard and Circadian Light: Two Weighting Curves, One Word

November 04, 2026 · 10 min read

"Blue light" is a wavelength band, not a quantity

The phrase covers roughly 380 to 500 nanometres, and within that band two completely different biological effects are being discussed, with two different action spectra, two different dose metrics and two different exposure conditions. Almost every argument about blue light glasses collapses because one side is talking about retinal damage and the other about sleep timing, and both are calling it blue light.

An action spectrum is the mechanism that makes this concrete. It is a weighting function: for each wavelength it states how effective that wavelength is at producing a particular effect, normalised to a peak of one. Multiply the light source's spectrum by the weighting function and integrate, and you get the biologically effective quantity. Same lamp, two weighting functions, two different numbers. That is the entire structure of the problem, and it explains why a lens can meaningfully reduce one effect while barely touching the other.

Curve one: the blue-light hazard

The blue-light hazard describes photochemical injury to the retina from short-wavelength visible light. Its weighting function peaks in the region of 435 to 440 nanometres and falls off steeply on both sides, which is the opposite of what most people assume when they picture "blue light" as a broad hazard stretching to the edge of violet.

The relevant regulatory framework in Europe is EN 62471, the photobiological safety standard for lamps and lamp systems. It sorts sources into risk groups — exempt, RG1, RG2 and RG3 — according to measured radiance against exposure limits that shorten as the source gets brighter. Two details in that sentence carry the practical weight. First, the metric is radiance rather than total output, so a small, intense source is far more demanding than a large, dim one delivering the same lumens. Second, the limits are time-dependent: the hazard is a dose, and the exposure limit for a source viewed briefly is far higher than one for a source stared at for an hour.

Curve two: the circadian system

The second effect has nothing to do with damage. A subset of retinal ganglion cells contains melanopsin and responds to light directly, without borrowing a signal from the rods or cones. These cells project to the circadian pacemaker, and their sensitivity is the basis of the melanopic action spectrum standardised in CIE S 026. That function peaks near 480 to 490 nanometres — noticeably longer in wavelength than the hazard peak — and the resulting quantity is usually expressed as melanopic equivalent daylight illuminance, in lux, measured at the eye.

Note what is being measured and where. Hazard is about radiance entering the pupil from a source; circadian effect is about sustained illuminance at the cornea over time, with duration and timing doing as much work as intensity. Evening exposure delays the timing of melatonin release and shifts circadian phase; morning exposure advances it. The magnitude depends on how much light, for how long, and at what hour — which is why a bright screen at midnight and the same screen at noon are not equivalent events, even though the screen has not changed.

Why the two curves do not move together

Because the peaks are roughly 50 nanometres apart, the position of a filter's cut-on wavelength decides which effect it addresses.

  • A lens that absorbs strongly below about 450 nm sits close to the hazard peak. It reduces the blue-light-hazard-weighted dose efficiently, while leaving much of the 480 to 490 nm region — where the melanopic function is strongest — largely intact.
  • A lens with a broader cut that removes wavelengths up to 500 nm will reduce the melanopic quantity substantially, and will also reduce hazard, but at a visible cost: colour discrimination degrades, blue objects disappear, and the scene takes on an amber cast.
  • A notch filter centred on a narrow band reduces one curve with unusual efficiency and leaves the rest of the spectrum alone, which is optically elegant and behaviourally strange, because the world keeps its colours except where it does not.

This is the mechanism behind a claim that sounds contradictory but is not: a pair of lenses can be excellent at one of these jobs and mediocre at the other, and the marketing number printed on the box — usually a percentage of "blue light blocked" over an unspecified band — cannot tell you which.

What a lens in front of the eye can and cannot do

A filter reduces retinal illuminance at the wavelengths it absorbs. That is all it does, and it is worth being precise about the consequence in each case.

For the hazard, the honest answer is that display luminance is low compared with the radiance levels at which the exposure limits in EN 62471 begin to bite. The hazard concept exists for lamps, welders, and intense small sources; it is not a description of a monitor at normal viewing distance. A lens cannot reduce a dose that is already negligible in the sense the standard means, and claims that screen use causes retinal damage through this mechanism are making an assertion the framework does not support.

For the circadian system, the honest answer is different and more useful. The melanopic quantity responds to any reduction in short-wavelength illuminance at the eye, and it also responds to dimming, to distance, and to turning the screen off. A lens is one way to lower evening melanopic exposure; it is not a uniquely effective one, and it is competing with the far larger effect of simply reducing brightness or stopping earlier.

Claims worth checking before you buy

  • "Screens emit dangerous blue light." Screens emit short-wavelength light. Whether a given exposure is hazardous depends on radiance and duration against a defined limit, and normal display use sits far below the levels the hazard function was designed to police.
  • "Blue light glasses prevent macular degeneration." No controlled evidence supports that claim for ordinary screen exposure, and the statement confuses a hazard metric with a disease process.
  • "Blocking more blue is better." Strong short-wavelength filtration degrades colour naming, dims blue signal lights, and reduces the light available for pupil control and night vision. More is not a direction, it is a trade.
  • "The percentage on the box is a protection rating." A percentage over an unstated band is not comparable between products. A filter that blocks 40% below 450 nm and one that blocks 40% below 500 nm have different effects despite identical labels.
  • "Blue light filters fix eye strain." Strain during screen work is dominated by accommodative and vergence demand, blink rate and working distance. A tint does not change any of those.

When to see an eye care professional

See one if light sensitivity is new or worsening, if you need sunglasses indoors, if colours have changed in one eye, if evening screen use reliably prevents sleep and simple dimming has not helped, or if you have a retinal condition and are being offered tinted lenses as a treatment. Sleep problems, headache and visual fatigue all have causes that a lens cannot diagnose. Nothing here is a prescription, and the two curves described above are measurement tools rather than instructions.
