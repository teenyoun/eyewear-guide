The Q Factor: Why Two Equally Dark Sunglasses Are Not Equally Legal to Drive In

November 28, 2026 · 8 min read

Two pairs, same darkness, different legality

Put two sunglasses side by side on a counter, one with a warm brown lens and one with a neutral grey lens, and they can look equally dark. Measure them and they can land in the same filter category. Yet one may satisfy the requirements a sunglass has to meet for road use while the other does not, because of something no shop assistant can see by eye: how each lens treats the narrow bands of light a traffic signal emits.

This is not a detail for pedants. If you drive, the difference between a lens that passes and one that fails is the difference between seeing a red light at 150 metres and seeing a dull spot you have to interpret. The rule that separates them is written into ISO 12312-1, the international sunglass standard adopted in Europe as EN ISO 12312-1, and it is expressed as a single ratio: the Q factor.

The principle: a tint is a filter with a shape, not a dimmer

Every tinted lens is described publicly by one number, its luminous transmittance. That figure is not a simple average of the light passing through. It is a weighted integral: the lens's spectral transmittance is multiplied, wavelength by wavelength, by the eye's photopic sensitivity curve and by a standard solar spectrum, then divided by the same weighting applied to the bare spectrum. The result is a percentage that answers one question — how much of the daylight that would have reached your eye still reaches it through this lens.

Notice what that calculation deliberately throws away: the shape of the curve. A lens can be uniformly grey, cutting every visible wavelength by the same proportion. Or it can be selective, letting red through generously while suppressing blue, which is what a brown or amber tint does. Both can be built to the same luminous transmittance. The number on the label cannot tell them apart, and that is exactly why the standard needs a second test.

What the Q factor measures

The Q factor, formally the relative visual attenuation quotient, is the transmittance of a specific signal light divided by the lens's luminous transmittance:

  • Signal transmittance is computed the same weighted way as luminous transmittance, but weighted by the spectral power distribution of a red, yellow, green or blue traffic signal instead of daylight.
  • Dividing by luminous transmittance normalises the result, so a darker lens is not penalised for being dark. Q asks a narrower question: does this lens attenuate this colour more than it attenuates the scene in general?

A neutral grey lens tends to sit near a Q of 1.0 for all four signals, because it attenuates everything equally. A lens that is heavy in the blue end will show a depressed Q for green and blue signals. A lens with a pronounced notch in the red will drop Q for red below the limit even when the overall tint is unremarkable.

The standard's driving requirements, for a lens to be sold as suitable for road use, are concrete: luminous transmittance above 8 percent, a Q of at least 0.80 for the red signal, and at least 0.60 for yellow, green and blue. There is also a spectral floor: between 475 and 650 nanometres, transmittance must not fall below 0.2 times the luminous transmittance, which prevents a manufacturer from carving a deep absorption band exactly where signals live while keeping the headline figure respectable. Where a lens falls below 75 percent luminous transmittance, the standard's guidance is that it should not be used for driving at dusk or at night.

Where the categories fit in

Filter category is a separate axis, set purely by luminous transmittance. The boundaries are fixed by the same standard: category 0 above 80 percent, category 1 from 43 to 80, category 2 from 18 to 43, category 3 from 8 to 18, and category 4 from 3 to 8 percent. Because the driving floor is 8 percent, category 4 is excluded from road use outright — a rule that surprises people who assume the darkest lens is the safest one.

This is why category alone is an incomplete shopping guide. A category 3 lens is dark enough for bright midday driving and, if its Q values comply, entirely legal for it. A category 3 lens with a badly shaped absorption curve is not. Conversely, a category 1 lens can be perfectly suitable for driving at dusk, where a category 3 lens would be a mistake for the opposite reason: too little light reaching the retina when the scene is already dim.

Common misconceptions

"Darker means more protection." Protection from ultraviolet radiation is a separate property entirely, and a dark lens with no UV absorber is worse than a clear one, because it dilates the pupil while letting UV through. Darkness buys glare reduction, not safety, and past a point it costs contrast.

"Polarised means legal for driving." Polarisation removes reflected glare from wet road and bonnet; it says nothing about spectral transmittance or Q. A polarised lens still has to meet the same driving requirements, and a cheap polarised lens with a heavy blue absorption can fail them.

"Mirrored lenses are not road legal." The mirror coating is on the front surface and reflects light before it enters the lens. What matters for driving is the transmittance of the substrate and its spectral shape, which is why mirrored category 2 and 3 lenses are routinely compliant.

"The label's UV400 claim covers it." UV400 describes the ultraviolet cut-off at 400 nanometres. It is silent on visible-light behaviour, and the two properties are tested differently.

There is a market reality behind all this. A spectrophotometric survey of 232 sunglass lenses sold on one national market found roughly a quarter unsuitable for driving, with failures split between category 4 lenses, lenses failing the red Q limit, and lenses with luminous transmittance so low they fell outside the categories altogether. None of those failures would have been visible to the buyer in a shop mirror.

Reading a label, and when to ask for help

A compliant product should state its filter category and, where the maker intends it for road use, confirm conformity with the standard. Treat "UV400" and "polarised" as separate claims rather than as driving certification, and be sceptical of very dark lenses marketed for night driving — the physics does not support the idea, since a tint can only remove light from an already dim scene.

Some situations deserve an optometrist rather than a shopping decision. If oncoming headlights bloom into large discs, if you avoid driving after dark because lane markings disappear, or if you have early cataract changes, the limiting factor may be stray light in your eye rather than the lens in front of it. The same applies if you have a colour vision deficiency and are choosing a tint for signal recognition, or if you need a prescription sunglass where the tint and the correction have to be built together. Those are measurements, not preferences — and a Q factor printed on a box is no substitute for them.
