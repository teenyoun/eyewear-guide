Double Vision in One Eye: Why Monocular Diplopia Is an Optics Problem

November 22, 2026 · 10 min read

One test that splits double vision into two different problems

Double vision sounds like one symptom. It is at least two. Cover your left eye: if the two images collapse into one, the doubling came from the two eyes pointing at slightly different places. Now cover the right eye instead. If the doubling survives, only one eye is open and it is still producing two images. That is monocular diplopia, and it belongs to a completely different list of causes — almost all of them optical rather than neurological.

The distinction matters. Binocular diplopia that appears suddenly can signal a nerve palsy or a brainstem event and needs urgent assessment. Monocular diplopia is, in almost every case, a problem with a lens: the one in your glasses, the one in your eye, or the tear film in front of it.

The optics: one eye, one image, two focal planes

A spherical eye focuses a point of light to a point. An astigmatic eye cannot: it has two different powers in two meridians at right angles, so a point of light becomes two short lines at different distances, known as the Sturm interval. The separation between those two lines, measured in dioptres along the optical axis, is numerically equal to the cylinder in the prescription. Zero cylinder means the two lines coincide; two dioptres of cylinder means the eye is genuinely focused at two different planes two dioptres apart.

At either focal line the other meridian is out of focus and forms a blur patch. Small patches read as softness or a faint halo; large ones stop merging into one percept and become a distinct second image, usually offset along the cylinder axis. Small amounts of residual astigmatism therefore produce ghosting rather than true doubling: the same mechanism at a different magnitude.

Acuity gives the scale. A 20/20 letter subtends five arcminutes and its stroke about one, so a second image displaced by a few arcminutes is visible as a separate edge while high-contrast acuity stays normal. That is why monocular diplopia and a normal letter chart coexist so often.

The tear film is a lens you rebuild with every blink

Before light reaches the cornea it crosses the tear film, a layer a few micrometres thick with its own surface. Smooth, it is optically neutral. When it breaks up — dry patches, mucus strands, an irregular lipid layer from meibomian gland dysfunction — the front surface of the eye becomes locally uneven, and an uneven surface acts as a weak prism and a weak cylindrical lens at the same time.

One home test separates this cause from the others. Blink firmly several times and look again immediately. If the ghost clears for two or three seconds and returns, the tear film is the culprit: rebuilt by the blink, breaking up again. Ghosting worst late in the day or in air conditioning fits the same pattern.

The lens inside your eye, going cloudy

A cataract is not a film over the eye; it is a change in the optical properties of the crystalline lens itself. In nuclear sclerotic cataract the centre of the lens becomes denser and its refractive index rises, creating a second refracting element inside the same lens — and a second element means a second image. Patients describe a shadow or duplicate edge on high-contrast targets such as road markings, often worse in one eye.

Cortical cataract, with its radial spokes of altered index, does something similar at a smaller scale: local steps in refractive index scatter and deflect light, producing ghosting and glare rather than a clean double image. Both can be present while the letter chart still reads 20/25, which is why "the chart was fine" does not exclude them. Cataract is a diagnosis made at the slit lamp, not by acuity alone.

Your glasses are an optical system too

Before assuming the eye is at fault, rule out the lens in front of it. Four ordinary faults do this.

  • Decentration. Looking through a point away from a lens's optical centre adds prism, and prism displaces the image. The displacement follows Prentice's rule: prism in dioptres equals decentration in centimetres multiplied by power in dioptres. Four millimetres off centre through a minus six lens is 2.4 dioptres of prism — enough to smear an edge into a second one near the rim of the frame.
  • Tilt. A lens not perpendicular to the line of sight, because of excess pantoscopic tilt or a wrapped frame, induces cylinder that was never prescribed. The eye then has astigmatism the prescription did not account for.
  • Surface damage. Deep scratches, crazed coating, or a roughly polished edge on a strong minus lens all scatter light in a directed way, and directed scatter reads as a ghost.
  • Progressive corridor edges. The prism reference and the change of power along the corridor create zones where a straight edge appears doubled, particularly just outside the corridor at the reading level.

Two checks settle most of these. First, does the ghost move when you look through a different part of the lens? Second, rotate the glasses ninety degrees while keeping your head still: if the ghost's direction rotates with the lens, the lens is responsible; if the direction stays fixed relative to your eye, it is not.

Less common causes worth knowing

Keratoconus and other irregular astigmatism produce ghosting no spectacle prescription fully removes, worse at night when the pupil is large, often with frequent prescription changes. A corneal scar or a pterygium across the visual axis does the same by presenting an irregular surface. A chalazion can press on the cornea and induce temporary astigmatism that resolves with the lump. Macular disease usually distorts rather than doubles, and true cerebral polyopia is rare.

The misconception: "double vision means something is wrong in my brain"

Monocular diplopia is an optical symptom, not a neurological one, in nearly every case that reaches an optometry practice. The dangerous error is the reverse: treating a sudden binocular double image as tired eyes. Binocular doubling that appears over hours, is accompanied by headache, eyelid droop, unequal pupils, weakness, or slurred speech, or that persists in one direction of gaze, needs same-day medical assessment.

The second misconception is that a new pair of glasses fixes any ghosting. If the cause is cataract, keratoconus, or a tear film that breaks up within seconds, a new prescription changes nothing — or sharpens the first image and leaves the second in place.

When to have it looked at properly

  • Ghosting that persists through the day and survives a firm blink.
  • New or worsening doubling in one eye, especially with glare or halos at night.
  • Straight lines that bend or curve — this points at the macula and should be assessed promptly.
  • Frequent prescription changes alongside the ghosting.
  • Any sudden binocular double vision, with or without the symptoms listed above.

A proper assessment separates these causes in one visit: refraction to quantify residual cylinder, slit-lamp examination of the tear film and lens, corneal topography if the astigmatism is irregular, and a lensmeter check of the glasses themselves. That is a diagnosis, and not one to make by ordering a stronger pair online.
