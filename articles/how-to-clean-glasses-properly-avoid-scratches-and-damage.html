How to Clean Glasses Properly — Avoid Scratches and Damage

August 3, 2026 · 7 min read

Most scratched lenses were not dropped. They were scratched during cleaning — by a cloth dragged across a surface that still had grit on it. The damage is rarely one dramatic gouge but thousands of shallow marks that scatter light, accumulating quietly until headlights bloom at night.

What is physically happening when you wipe a lens

A modern lens is a stack, not a single material. Underneath sits the substrate — CR-39, polycarbonate, Trivex or a high-index plastic. Above it is a hard coat a few micrometres thick, then an anti-reflective stack of alternating high- and low-index layers, each deposited to a quarter-wave optical thickness:

d = λ / (4n)

For 550 nm light in a layer of index 1.38 that gives roughly 100 nm per layer, and the whole stack is a few hundred nanometres deep. Above it, the hydrophobic topcoat is thinner still. Everything you can touch is measured in nanometres.

Dust is not soft. Wind-borne grit is largely silica, and quartz sits at 7 on the Mohs hardness scale — harder than the polysiloxane hard coat beneath your cloth. Trap a particle of that between fabric and lens and press, and you have three-body abrasion: two surfaces sliding with a hard particle between them, where the softer surface loses. The lens is the softer surface.

The damage is measured optically, not by feel. Standards describe it as haze — the share of transmitted light scattered more than 2.5 degrees from the incident beam, read on a haze meter before and after a controlled abrasion cycle. ISO 8980-5 uses that quantity to set the minimum requirement for surfaces claimed to be abrasion-resistant. A lens can pass that requirement and still pick up haze in a coat pocket, because the standard is a repeatable comparison and a pocket is not.

The correct sequence, and why each step exists

Step 1: Wash your hands first

Skin oil and hand cream transfer straight onto the lens, and an oily lens invites harder rubbing. Lotion-free soap, then dry your hands.

Step 2: Rinse before you touch anything

Hold the frame by the rim and run lukewarm water over both surfaces. This is the step that prevents scratches: it removes loose grit before cloth or finger makes contact. Keep the water comfortably warm rather than hot: the coating stack and the substrate expand at different rates, and repeated hot-water cycles are a common route to fine crazing at the lens edge.

Step 3: One drop of lotion-free dish soap

Surfactants do the work. Clean water has a surface tension near 72 mN/m at room temperature; a dilute surfactant solution drops it to roughly 30 mN/m. That lower tension lets liquid wet into the gap between lens and frame instead of beading over it, and lifts oil rather than pushing it around. One small drop, fingertips only — never nails — over both sides of both lenses, the nose pads, the bridge, the hinge corners. Skip anything labelled moisturising or creamy; the additives leave a film.

Step 4: Rinse until the water runs clear

Residual soap is the usual cause of a lens that looks clean and streaks as it dries.

Step 5: Dry with a clean microfiber cloth

Microfiber is not simply soft fabric. Its fibres are split to a few micrometres across, giving capillary channels that wick oil off the surface and hold it inside the cloth instead of redistributing it. Blot and sweep lightly. With the grit gone, a dry cloth is safe here; it is dangerous only on a dry, dirty lens.

The cloth matters more than the spray

A microfiber cloth is a grit reservoir. Every wipe deposits particles into the fibres, and a cloth carried loose in a bag collects more. Wash it every week or two, on its own, with no fabric softener — softener coats the fibres and destroys the wicking that makes them work. Replace it when it feels slick or sheds lint. A dirty microfiber is worse than a clean shirt: it holds grit close and presses it down evenly.

What to stop using

  • Paper towels and tissues: wood fibres and mineral fillers, both harder than the hard coat, abrading in straight lines that scatter light.
  • Your shirt hem: fabric softener residue smears, and the weave carries the day's grit.
  • Ammonia-based glass cleaner: the chemistry that cuts kitchen grease attacks the bond between coating layers and can cloud an AR stack permanently.
  • Vinegar or lemon juice: acid attacks the inorganic layers and leaves its own residue.
  • Repeated isopropyl alcohol on coated lenses: fine in a lab on bare surfaces; risky on a coating, and it attacks the adhesives in rimless mountings.
  • Saliva: enzymes and bacteria; the film dries into a smear you then scrub.
  • A dry cloth on a dirty lens: the single most common way glasses get scratched.

Myths that cost you a pair of lenses

“Hot water sterilises them.” It does not, and it stresses the coating. Warm water and soap remove the oil bacteria cling to.

“Microfiber cloths clean themselves.” They hold what they pick up. An unwashed cloth is a polishing tool loaded with abrasive.

“Breathing on the lens and polishing is the field method.” Condensation turns grit into slurry and your sleeve supplies the pressure.

“If I cannot feel a scratch, it does not matter.” Haze is optical, not tactile. A lens can feel smooth and still scatter enough light to cut contrast in low-light driving.

“More pressure cleans faster.” Pressure is the variable that turns grit into scratches. Surfactant and time do the work.

How often, and when ultrasonic cleaning is worth it

Daily: rinse and blot with a clean cloth. Weekly: the full soap wash. Monthly: an ultrasonic clean for nose pads and hinge crevices, where body oil and sunscreen collect and no cloth reaches. Ultrasonic tanks work by cavitation — microscopic bubbles form in the liquid and collapse against surfaces, dislodging soil from crevices without contact. Cavitation removes soil, not scratches, and it cannot restore a coating that has already hazed.

When a lens is past cleaning

If the surface stays foggy after a proper wash, the scatter is in the coating, not on it. Home polishing compounds strip the topcoat along with the scratch and leave the AR stack exposed, which usually makes the lens worse within weeks. If one lens hazes and the other does not, the cause is usually the cloth kept in that particular pocket — change the habit before you replace the pair.
