Gradient and Mirror Sunglass Tints: What They Actually Do

October 09, 2026 · 9 min read

Darkness and tint distribution are two different things

When people compare sunglasses, the conversation usually collapses into one question: how dark are they? That is only half of what a tint does. A tint is not simply a level; it has a distribution across the lens and, if there is a mirror finish, a reflectance at the surface. Two lenses darkened to the same average level can behave very differently on a road, on water, or at a desk, because the light reaching your eye depends on where the tint sits and how much the surface sends back.

The measurable quantity is luminous transmittance: the fraction of visible light that passes through. Sunglasses are sorted into five filter categories by that figure, from category 0 (roughly 80–100 per cent) through category 3 (roughly 8–18 per cent) to category 4 (roughly 3–8 per cent). Category 4 is too dark for driving. Those ranges describe a single number for the whole lens — and that is exactly where gradients and mirrors complicate the picture.

Three ways a tint can be built

A fixed tint is uniform. The same transmittance applies whether you look through the centre or the edge, which makes the lens predictable and is why it is the default for driving.

A gradient tint is not uniform. The colour is applied more heavily along one edge and fades toward the other, usually darkest at the top. This has a practical logic: the top of the lens covers the sky and the road ahead, where oncoming brightness dominates, while the lower part stays lighter for the dashboard, a phone or a map. Read the label carefully, though, because a gradient lens does not have one transmittance — it has a range down the lens. A single category number describes it only approximately, which is why a gradient and a fixed lens both marked category 3 can feel quite different in use.

What a mirror coating actually changes

A mirror finish is a reflective coating applied over the tint. The common assumption is that it adds protection. It does not add anything chemical: it changes what happens at the surface. Most light still passes through, but a portion is reflected back, which is precisely why the lens looks like a mirror from the outside. Because reflected light does not reach your eye, a mirror coat does slightly add to the apparent darkening — but it is a surface effect on top of the tint, not a replacement for it.

This is where a standard number becomes useful. Under EN ISO 12312-1, sunglasses advertised as having an anti-reflective coating must keep luminous reflectance below 8 per cent. A mirror is the opposite intent: it deliberately raises reflectance so that the wearer sees a mirror image instead of their own eyes. A mirrored lens and an anti-reflective lens are therefore not variations on one idea; they pull the same surface property in opposite directions. You cannot have both at the same time on the same surface.

Gradients, mirrors and the driving question

Driving exposes the difference. A dark fixed tint in category 3 cuts the overall brightness of the scene evenly, including the instrument panel. A gradient keeps the lower part of the lens lighter, which generally helps with the dashboard and reduces the sense of looking through a letterbox. A mirror coat rejects part of the light arriving from the front, which can feel comfortable on a bright motorway but makes the lens more reflective of glare arriving from behind and above.

None of this changes the legal limit. The reason category 4 is not road-legal is not the mirror or the gradient but the transmittance: at 3–8 per cent too little light reaches the eye for safe driving, particularly in poor conditions. Tint distribution is a comfort and usability decision; the category is the safety line.

Common misconceptions

  • "A mirror coating means more UV protection." UV absorption comes from the lens material and the absorbers put into it, not from the reflective top layer. A mirrored lens with weak UV absorption is still a weak UV filter.
  • "Darker always means better." Above a point, extra darkness only makes the pupil open wider while the same amount of UV and glare still arrives — comfort can fall even as the lens looks more serious.
  • "A gradient is just cosmetic." It genuinely changes which part of the lens you look through for the dashboard versus the road, which is why some drivers prefer it and others find it distracting.
  • "The category tells you the tint pattern." It does not. The category is a transmittance figure; how that transmittance is distributed across the lens is a separate design choice.

When to ask a professional

Tint and mirror choices are low risk, but three situations deserve proper advice rather than a website: if you need sunglasses over a prescription, because the tint interacts with your correction and with any photochromic or polarising layer already in the lens; if you drive at night or in poor visibility, where even a light tint can be the wrong call; and if you have a condition that makes you unusually light sensitive, because the right answer may be a specific filter rather than a darker one. An optician can also confirm the actual category rather than relying on a label or a product photo.

How the tint gets into the lens

There are three routes, and they explain why two lenses of identical darkness can age differently. A hard-coated tint is a dyed layer applied to the surface; it is flexible and cheap, but it sits on top of the lens and can be scratched through. A mass-tinted lens has the dye mixed into the material itself, so the colour is the same all the way through and cannot be worn off — the classic approach for acetate and for many plastic sun lenses. A vacuum-deposited tint, used in mirror and some reflective finishes, is a thin metallic layer laid down in a chamber, which is what produces the sharp mirrored appearance.

This matters for gradient quality in particular. A mass-tinted gradient has to be produced by controlling how deeply dye penetrates across the blank, while a surface-applied gradient depends on how evenly the coating was laid down. Either way, the transition should be smooth. A visible step or blotching in the gradient is a manufacturing defect, not a design feature, and it is one of the few tint faults you can genuinely inspect by eye before buying.
