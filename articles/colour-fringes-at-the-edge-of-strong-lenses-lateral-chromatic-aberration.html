Colour Fringes at the Edge of Strong Lenses: Lateral Chromatic Aberration

November 20, 2026 · 9 min read

Colour at the edge of a strong lens

Look through the outer part of a strong minus lens at a bright window frame and you may see a thin blue edge on one side and a warm red-orange edge on the other. The lens is not dirty and the coating is not failing. You are seeing lateral chromatic aberration, the same dispersion that splits white light in a prism, and it follows directly from the material's Abbe number and the prism power the lens carries away from its centre.

Dispersion and the Abbe number

No transparent material refracts all wavelengths equally. Blue light, being shorter in wavelength, is bent slightly more than red. The Abbe number quantifies this: it is (nd - 1) divided by (nF - nC), the refractive index at the helium d line divided by the difference in index between the hydrogen F and C lines. A high Abbe number means low dispersion and cleaner off-axis colour.

The spread across common materials is wide. Crown glass sits around 59, CR-39 around 58, Trivex around 45, 1.60 plastic around 42, 1.67 around 32, polycarbonate around 30, and 1.74 around 33. Two lenses of identical power in CR-39 and in polycarbonate are optically different objects, and the difference shows up first and most obviously as colour at the edge.

Off-axis, every lens is a prism

At its optical centre a lens has no prism effect, because the two surfaces are effectively parallel at that point. Move away from the centre and the surfaces diverge in angle, so the lens behaves like a prism of increasing power. Prentice's rule gives the amount: prism in dioptres equals the distance from the optical centre in centimetres multiplied by the lens power in dioptres. A -8.00 D lens viewed 20 mm off centre carries 8 x 2 = 16 prism dioptres. That is a substantial prism, and prism is where dispersion becomes visible.

Why the fringe scales as prism divided by Abbe

A prism's deviation is proportional to (nd - 1) times its angle, and its dispersion is proportional to (nF - nC) times the same angle. Divide one by the other, the angle cancels, and what remains is the ratio of dispersion to deviation, which is exactly one over the Abbe number. In practical terms, the colour separation across an edge is roughly the prism power divided by the Abbe number. A 16 prism dioptre edge in a material of Abbe 30 separates blue from red by about 0.53 prism dioptres, around a third of a degree: small as an angle, entirely visible as a fringe on a high-contrast edge.

Why high power and high index make it worse together

The two effects compound in the least convenient way. Higher power means more prism at any given distance from the centre, so the numerator grows. Higher index usually means a lower Abbe number, so the denominator shrinks. This is why the lenses sold as thinnest can be the least comfortable at the edge, particularly for a high myope in a wide frame who turns the eyes rather than the head. It is also why a moderate-index material is often the better optical choice between about -3.00 D and -5.00 D, where the cosmetic gain from 1.67 is small and the dispersion penalty is not.

Design can reduce the prism, not the dispersion

Aspheric and free-form designs can flatten the base curve, reduce off-axis prism and cut oblique aberration, which genuinely reduces the colour a wearer sees. What no design can do is change the material's dispersion. Once the prism at a given point is fixed, the fringe is fixed by the Abbe number. This is the honest boundary between lens design and material physics, and it is why a well-made 1.60 lens often looks cleaner off-axis than a cheaply made 1.74.

Myth: blue edges mean a defective coating

An anti-reflective coating does leave a residual colour, usually a faint green or purple sheen, but it is visible across the whole surface in reflected light and does not move when you move the lens. Chromatic fringing appears on one side of an edge with a complementary colour on the other, follows the direction of the prism, and grows as you move further from the optical centre. Coating residue does none of those things, and a coating fault usually looks like a patch, a haze or a peeling rim rather than a fringe.

Myth: rainbows and colour fringes are the same problem

They are three different faults. Chromatic aberration is dispersion in the material and exists in every lens to some degree. Stress birefringence shows as broad rainbow bands when a lens is viewed between crossed polarisers, and comes from mounting stress or internal strain in the material. A diffraction effect from fine scratches or a damaged coating shows as a starburst around point sources rather than clean colour on one side of an edge. Only the first is a material and design issue, and only the first improves with a different lens choice.

What actually helps

Choose the lowest index that gives an acceptable edge thickness, particularly if you are sensitive to colour. Keep the frame small enough that your gaze does not spend its time at the far edge; a 56 mm eyesize on a -8.00 D prescription guarantees strong peripheral prism. Have the optical centres placed accurately, because decentration error adds prism you never ordered. And when you look at something off to the side, turn your head as well as your eyes, which helps with peripheral distortion as much as with colour.

When to see an optometrist

Colour fringing in the centre of the lens, fringing in one eye only, or fringing that persists on a low-power prescription is not lateral chromatic aberration and deserves an examination. Sudden changes in colour perception, halos with eye pain, or a fringe that stays in the same place when you rotate the lens are all reasons to be checked rather than to order a different material.

Lateral colour is a small, honest tax on high power and high index. Understanding it turns an alarming defect into a predictable trade-off you can choose deliberately.
