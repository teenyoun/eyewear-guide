IOL Biometry: Why Axial Length Is Measured to a Fraction of a Millimetre

November 28, 2026 · 8 min read

Three numbers decide what you see after surgery

Cataract surgery replaces a clouded crystalline lens with a clear acrylic implant, and the implant has to be chosen before the eye is opened. Nobody measures the required power directly, because the required power depends on the eye that will exist after the surgery, not the one being measured. What gets measured is geometry: how long the eye is, how steep the front surface is, and where the new lens will eventually sit.

The whole exercise is called biometry, and its error budget is unusually tight. A millimetre of axial length is worth about 2.5 dioptres of implant power in the standard regression relationship, so the difference between a good and a poor measurement is a fraction of a millimetre. That is why the same eye measured on two different instruments can produce two different lens orders — and why understanding the chain explains most "refractive surprises".

The principle: solving for the lens that puts focus on the retina

Treat the eye as two refracting elements separated by a distance. The cornea provides roughly two thirds of the eye's power as a fixed air-to-tissue interface. The crystalline lens, suspended behind the iris, provides the rest — an average of about +23.70 dioptres in the natural adult eye, a figure established in the 1980s and still used as a reference point. The separation between cornea and retina is the axial length.

Replace the second element and the arithmetic becomes a vergence problem: light arrives at the cornea, is bent by the corneal power, travels the remaining distance, and must arrive at the retina with zero vergence left. Solve for the implant power that satisfies that condition and you have the calculation. The classical regression form still taught for its transparency is P = A − 0.9K − 2.5AL, where P is implant power, K is average corneal power in dioptres, AL is axial length in millimetres, and A is a lens-specific constant. Read the coefficients: a 1 dioptre change in K shifts the answer by about 0.9 dioptre, and a 1 millimetre change in AL shifts it by 2.5 dioptres.

Term one: axial length

Axial length is defined as the distance from the anterior corneal surface to the retinal pigment epithelium — not to the inner retinal surface, which matters because ultrasound reflects from the internal limiting membrane while optical methods reference different layers. Modern optical biometry uses partial coherence interferometry or swept-source optical coherence tomography to measure that distance by timing reflections from tissue interfaces, with no contact and no corneal compression.

The older alternative, contact ultrasound, presses a probe against the anaesthetised cornea. That pressure shortens the eye slightly during the measurement, which biases axial length low and implant power high. Immersion ultrasound avoids the compression but gives up some control of probe alignment. These are not academic distinctions: they are systematic errors with a predictable direction.

Axial length also decides which formula family is trustworthy: eyes of average length behave well under almost any modern formula, while long myopic and short hyperopic eyes are where the formulas diverge, because the assumed link between axial length and implant position breaks down at the extremes.

Term two: corneal power, and the assumption hidden inside it

Keratometry does not measure corneal power. It measures the radius of curvature of the anterior surface by the size of a reflected image, then converts radius to power using an assumed refractive index step. Standard instruments also assume the posterior corneal surface is exactly 1.2 millimetres steeper in radius than the anterior one, because a conventional keratometer cannot see the back of the cornea at all.

That assumption is normally harmless, since it is baked into the same constant used for everyone. It becomes a real error after corneal refractive surgery, where the front surface has been flattened and the normal front-to-back relationship no longer holds. Eyes with a history of laser vision correction therefore need a modified approach — measured posterior corneal power, or a formula designed for the post-refractive case.

Term three: effective lens position

The effective lens position is where the implant's principal plane will sit behind the cornea once it is in the capsular bag. It is the only variable in the calculation that cannot be measured before surgery, and almost every formula generation since the 1980s has been an attempt to predict it better.

  • First-generation theoretical formulas treated it as a fixed constant.
  • Regression formulas absorbed it into the A-constant, which is specific not just to the lens model but to the surgeon's incision and placement technique.
  • Later formulas estimate it from measured anterior chamber depth, and some add age and preoperative refraction as inputs.

Because the A-constant varies roughly one-to-one with implant power, a constant that is wrong for a given surgeon-lens combination produces a systematic offset across every case, not random scatter. This is why centres track their own outcomes rather than trusting a printed value.

Common misconceptions

"The implant power is measured from your glasses prescription." Your spectacle refraction describes the whole eye's error but not its geometry. Two eyes with identical refractions can need different implants, because one is a long eye with a weak cornea and the other a short eye with a steep one. The prescription may be used as a cross-check, never as the input.

"A half dioptre of residual error means someone made a mistake." Modern formulas do not promise emmetropia; they promise a distribution of outcomes, and the majority of eyes land within half a dioptre of target. The implant comes in 0.50 dioptre steps, so the choice is quantised before any measurement error is added. A small residual is the normal cost of a prediction, and it is correctable with glasses or a thin prescription.

"The scan is just a photograph of the eye." Optical biometry is an interferometric distance measurement, not an image to be eyeballed. Its precision is why dense cataracts, which scatter the measuring light, can force a fallback to ultrasound.

"The most expensive implant gives the best vision." A multifocal divides incoming light between distance and near foci — a design decision with optical consequences, not a quality upgrade.

What to ask, and when to see a professional

If you are preparing for cataract surgery, the useful questions are concrete: which measurement method was used, whether both eyes were measured, which formula was chosen and why, and what residual refraction the surgeon is targeting. If you have had laser vision correction in the past, say so explicitly — the corneal assumptions described above do not apply to you, and the calculation has to be handled differently.

After surgery, do not judge the result in the first week. Corneal swelling, tear film instability and a shifting refraction all distort the picture, and a prescription taken too early measures recovery rather than final state. If your vision is still not what you expected once things have settled, the answer is a refraction and a discussion about the target that was chosen — not a pair of glasses ordered on a guess. Residual error is common, measurable and usually correctable: a number to be found, not a verdict.
