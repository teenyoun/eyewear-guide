Maximum Plus, Maximum Acuity: Why Your Eye Is Deliberately Fogged

November 17, 2026 · 9 min read

The sharpest lens is not the right lens

Most people imagine refraction as focusing a camera: the examiner tries lenses until the letters look clearest, then writes that number down. A healthy eye can accommodate, which means it can add power of its own, and an eye that can add power will make several different lenses look equally sharp. Go hunting for the sharpest lens and you will not find the correct one — you will find the most minus lens the eye is willing to tolerate.

Clinical refraction runs on the opposite rule: maximum plus to maximum visual acuity, usually shortened to MPMVA. The number on your prescription is the most plus, or least minus, lens that still produces the best acuity the eye can give. Fogging, quarter-dioptre steps and the red-green check are not ceremony around the measurement. They exist to defend that endpoint.

What fogging does to the eye

Fogging means making the eye deliberately myopic with plus lenses, so that accommodation cannot take part. Add +1.00 D in front of an emmetropic eye and its far point — the furthest distance at which it is focused without effort — moves to one metre. The eye is now focused for something inside the room. To read a chart at six metres it would have to relax past infinity, which it cannot do. Accommodation is not switched off by instruction; it is made irrelevant.

That is the whole trick. A fogged eye cannot help the examiner, and a patient who cannot help is a patient who gives a clean answer. The examiner then withdraws plus in 0.25 D steps — the smallest increment standard trial lenses and phoropters offer — and keeps the last step that actually improved acuity. The next step is refused. The prescription that results is deliberately the weakest lens that still works, not the strongest one that still passes.

Why a quarter of a dioptre is the unit of the argument

Refraction and lens manufacture both work in 0.25 D increments, and the reason is perceptual rather than mathematical. Most people cannot reliably separate a quarter dioptre of defocus on a high-contrast letter chart. Finer steps would be measuring the patient's patience, not the eye's focus. The increment also explains why prescriptions look oddly precise: -2.75 D is not a measurement carried to two decimal places, it is the middle of a bracket that was tested at both ends.

The step size matters clinically, not just cosmetically. At four dioptres of myopia, one step of extra minus is a small blur that most people will not notice at distance and a small amount of avoidable accommodative work at near. Repeated across reading, screen work and late evenings, that is the difference some people feel as eye strain and others never notice at all.

The red-green check and what it protects

Longitudinal chromatic aberration gives the examiner a second opinion that does not depend on the patient's judgement of "clearer". Shorter wavelengths are refracted more strongly, so green light comes to focus slightly in front of red. If the eye is over-minused it is focused short of the retina, and the red target — the longer wavelength, focused further back — reads better. If it is over-plussed, green wins. The endpoint is a balance point, and the test reliably catches the last quarter dioptre of over-minus that a patient will happily accept, because a slightly over-minused eye genuinely does look crisp for the ten seconds it is being tested.

Where unwanted minus comes from

  • Residual accommodation. A young patient can hold focus through a light fog, especially if the fog was applied for a moment rather than allowed to settle.
  • The instrument's aperture. A phoropter's small viewing hole acts like a pinhole and flattens the effect of defocus, making two different lenses look more alike than they are.
  • Preference for contrast. Asked to choose, many people pick the darker, higher-contrast image, which is usually the more minus one.
  • Autorefractor output treated as a prescription. The machine produces a starting point, and it is biased toward minus in eyes that accommodate during the measurement.
  • Copying an old pair. Ordering the numbers from a five-year-old prescription skips the entire process described above.

Common misconceptions

"The lens that looks sharpest is the correct one." Sharpness is not a single-valued function of lens power. Within roughly a quarter dioptre, several lenses produce an image the eye calls clear, and the eye's own focusing system chooses which of them it prefers on the day. The correct lens is defined by a rule, not by a feeling.

"A stronger prescription means stronger vision." Beyond the correct point, more minus does not add resolution. It moves the far point in front of the retina and forces accommodation to compensate, which is work the eye did not need to do. In a myopic child, sustained over-correction is one of the things clinicians try to avoid, because the eye's growth responds to where focus actually lands.

"Fogging is a trick to sell stronger glasses." It does the opposite. Fogging limits how much minus the examiner can justify, and the endpoint of the procedure is the least minus that works. A refraction without any control for accommodation tends to produce more minus, not less.

"An app can do this at home." A phone can measure how you respond to a lens. It cannot tell whether your answer came from your retina or from your ciliary muscle, and that distinction is the entire difficulty of the job.

When a prescription deserves a second look

Question the numbers rather than living with them if distance vision is crisp but close work produces a dull ache behind the eyes within twenty minutes, if the world looks noticeably smaller or larger than it did in your previous pair, if one eye feels strained while the other does not, or if the same pair reads differently at nine in the morning and nine at night. Those patterns point at the balance of the prescription rather than at the lenses themselves.

Get a proper examination rather than a re-order if the change was sudden, if it happened in one eye, if you are over forty and near vision has shifted in months rather than years, or if a child's prescription has moved quickly. Fogging and MPMVA are how a careful examiner protects you from a lens that is one step too strong; they are not a substitute for finding out why the eye changed.
