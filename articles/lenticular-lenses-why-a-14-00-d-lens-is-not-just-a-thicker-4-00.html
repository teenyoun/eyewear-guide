Lenticular Lenses: Why a -14.00 D Lens Is Not Just a Thicker -4.00

October 19, 2026 · 8 min read

The problem is at the edge, not the centre

High minus lenses do not fail in the middle. A minus lens is thickest where it is furthest from the optical centre, and the thickness of that edge is set by three things: the power, the size of the finished lens, and the curvature of the two surfaces. Nothing about the prescription itself guarantees a wearable edge. A -2.00 D lens 58 mm across is a normal pair of glasses. A -14.00 D lens the same size is a disc of plastic with a rim you could stand on.

The geometry is worth doing once, because it explains every design decision that follows. For a spherical surface, the sagitta (the depth of the curve, i.e. how far the surface rises from its edge to its centre) is s equals r minus the square root of (r squared minus half the diameter squared), where r is the radius of curvature and the diameter is the lens diameter, both in the same units. Radius comes from power through r equals 1000 times (n minus 1) divided by F, with F in dioptres and r in millimetres.

Take a 1.67 index material, a +2.00 D front base curve and a -16.00 D back curve, which together give about -14.00 D. The front radius is 1000 x 0.67 / 2.00, or 335 mm. The back radius is 1000 x 0.67 / 16.00, or about 41.9 mm. Over a 60 mm diameter, the front surface sags 1.35 mm and the back surface sags 12.66 mm. With a 1.5 mm centre thickness, the edge is 1.5 + 12.66 - 1.35, which is about 12.8 mm. That is a real number for a real lens, and it is why a -14.00 D lens in an ordinary frame looks the way it does.

What a lenticular design changes

A lenticular lens does not try to correct the whole blank. It corrects a defined central zone, called the optic or bowl, and the surrounding material is reduced to a carrier curve that exists only to reach the frame. In a full myodisc the carrier has essentially no power and the junction is visible as a ring. In a blended lenticular the transition is smoothed so that the eye does not catch a hard line.

Run the same arithmetic with a 30 mm powered zone. Over a 15 mm semi-diameter the front sag falls to 0.34 mm and the back sag to 2.78 mm. With the same 1.5 mm centre, the edge is 1.5 + 2.78 - 0.34, or roughly 3.9 mm. The edge has dropped from about 12.8 mm to about 3.9 mm without changing the prescription or the material at all. That is the entire idea: the correction stays in the middle, the material stops pretending it needs to be thick at the edge.

What you give up

  • Field of view. Outside the bowl there is no correction. Look through the carrier and the world is blurred by roughly the full prescription. On a -14.00 D lens that means the usable visual field is genuinely small, and the wearer must turn the head far more than an ordinary glasses wearer.
  • The junction. A blended transition is a compromise: smoothing the ring means putting some unwanted prism and astigmatism into the blend zone. A hard myodisc junction is optically cleaner but cosmetically obvious and can produce a ring scotoma.
  • Cosmesis. A myodisc looks like a lens inside a lens. Some wearers prefer the thick edge to the bullseye, especially in a rimless or thin metal frame where the junction ring is fully visible.
  • Frame freedom. Once you choose lenticular, the frame is a technical constraint rather than a style decision. Small eyesize, round or oval, well-centred on the pupil, and deep enough to hold the bowl.

Four things that are not true

  • "Just use a higher index and you will not need lenticular." Index helps, but the geometry dominates at these powers. Going from 1.60 to 1.74 reduces the radius by about nine per cent, which reduces sag by a similar amount, not by a factor. Meanwhile the Abbe number falls, so colour fringing increases and the lens feels thinner but not necessarily better. Index is one lever; optical zone size is a much bigger one.
  • "My lens is defective because the edge thickness varies." A minus lens is meant to be thin in the middle and thick at the edge. That is not a defect, it is the definition of a concave surface. The relevant question is whether the edge is thicker than the design predicted.
  • "Lenticular is an obsolete design from the glass era." It remains the standard solution above roughly -10.00 D and is now produced by free-form surfacing, with the junction blended rather than cut.
  • "Thinner is always better vision." A thinner lens with a smaller bowl gives less field. Cosmetic gain and functional cost move together, and the right balance depends on whether the wearer drives, reads, or mostly cares how the glasses photograph.

What to ask for at the counter

  • Monocular pupillary distance and fitting height, not a single binocular number. In a bowl of a few centimetres, a 2 mm centration error is a large fraction of the usable zone.
  • A small eyesize frame. A 60 mm lens in a 46 mm eyesize frame wastes less material than the same lens in a 56 mm frame. This single choice often saves more thickness than an index upgrade.
  • Aspheric or atorispheric front curves where the lab offers them, to keep the bowl flatter and the edge thinner.
  • Edge treatment. Polished edges look better and reduce scattering; a safety bevel prevents chipping on a thin rim.

When this needs a professional, not a shopping decision

Go back to your optometrist if the bowl edge falls inside your normal gaze when reading or driving, if you notice a ring or a "jack-in-the-box" effect where objects appear and disappear at the edge of the lens, if the two lenses look visibly different in thickness when your prescription is similar in both eyes, or if you have had a fall or a near-miss while wearing them. Extreme minus corrections also deserve a conversation about contact lenses and about retinal risk, which is a clinical matter and not something a frame choice can solve.
