Wavefront Aberrations: Why Two People With the Same Prescription See Differently

October 12, 2026 · 9 min read

The prescription is only the beginning

Two people can sit for an eye examination on the same afternoon, receive prescriptions that are identical to the last quarter dioptre, and walk away seeing the world differently. One reads signs crisply at distance; the other reports halos around headlights, a faint smearing of contrast, and letters that seem to have a shadow. Nothing about the examination was wrong. The prescription simply does not describe everything the eye does to light.

What it describes is the low-order part of the eye's error: sphere, which is focus, and cylinder, which is focus that differs between meridians. Those two numbers account for the great majority of blur in a typical eye, which is why correcting them produces the dramatic improvement most people notice on the first day. What remains is a smaller, more varied set of imperfections called higher-order aberrations, and they are not captured by any pair of numbers on a prescription form.

Thinking in wavefronts

The useful way to picture this is to stop thinking about where light lands and start thinking about when it arrives. A perfect eye would deliver a wavefront, a surface of light travelling together, as a perfect sphere converging on a single point. A real eye delivers it slightly distorted, some parts of the wavefront running ahead of others. That distortion is the wavefront error, and it is measured in units of length, usually micrometres.

Length turns out to be a natural unit here because light has a wavelength. For green light, a wavelength is roughly 0.55 micrometres in air. That makes a useful yardstick: a wavefront error of about one micrometre means light is arriving around two wavelengths out of step. The eye is an extraordinarily good optical instrument precisely because its errors are usually a fraction of a wavelength, which is the same order of precision that a well-made camera lens is built to.

To describe the shape of that distortion, researchers use Zernike polynomials, a set of mathematical building blocks each representing a characteristic pattern of error. They are grouped by order. Second order covers defocus and astigmatism, which is to say ordinary sphere and cylinder. Third order includes coma, a comet-shaped smear that gets worse away from the centre, and trefoil, a three-lobed pattern. Fourth order includes spherical aberration, in which rays passing through the outer part of the pupil focus at a different point from rays through the centre. The combined size of the third- and higher-order terms is often reported as a single number, the higher-order root mean square.

Why pupil size changes everything

Here is the consequence that matters most in daily life. Spherical aberration does not scale gently with pupil diameter. For a given eye, its magnitude grows with roughly the fourth power of the pupil radius. Widen the pupil from two millimetres to four and the aberration does not double; it climbs by something like sixteenfold.

That is why an eye can be comfortably sharp in daylight and visibly poor at night. In bright conditions the pupil is small, the outer zone of the optics is unused, and the wavefront error stays tiny. After dark the pupil dilates to let in more light, the eye starts using the very region where its imperfections are largest, and the image degrades exactly when the available light is already scarce. Halos and starbursts around bright points at night are the classic signature. It also explains why two people with the same prescription can differ so much behind the wheel after dark.

What can and cannot be done about them

Higher-order aberrations cannot be written onto a standard prescription, but they are not entirely beyond influence either.

Where the eye's own optics produce the error, a lens in front of it cannot cancel it, because a spectacle lens sits several millimetres away and the wavefront error is generated inside the eye. What a lens can do is avoid adding to the problem. Aspheric front-surface designs are used partly for this reason, since they shape the way oblique rays pass through the lens and can reduce the aberrations a strong spectacle correction would otherwise introduce at the edges.

Contact lenses, being closer to the eye, have more leverage. Rigid gas permeable lenses in particular can neutralise irregularity arising at the corneal surface by replacing it with a smooth new front surface, which is why they remain useful in conditions where the cornea itself is irregular. Beyond that, wavefront-guided corrections exist and are used in some surgical and specialty contexts, but they are a specialist decision rather than a routine upgrade, and they depend on a measurement of the individual eye rather than a catalogue choice.

It is worth being precise about what a premium lens can claim. A lens can be made with tighter tolerances and with a design that behaves better off-axis. It cannot remove an aberration that originates behind the pupil. Marketing language frequently blurs that line.

Common misconceptions

  • "A better lens will fix my night vision." If the halos come from the eye's own optics, a lens cannot undo them. Night vision complaints deserve an examination, not a shopping decision.
  • "Higher-order aberration means something is wrong with my eye." Every eye has them. They are a normal feature of biological optics, and only the size and the pattern determine whether they matter.
  • "20/20 means the optics are perfect." Acuity is measured on high-contrast letters under good lighting, the conditions in which aberrations are least visible. Passing the test says nothing about the dark.
  • "My prescription changed, so that explains the night halos." Halos that persist behind an accurate prescription usually point somewhere other than sphere and cylinder.

When to have your eyes examined

  • New or worsening halos, starbursts or ghosting around lights, especially at night
  • Vision that is sharp by day but noticeably poor in dim light
  • Two people with the same prescription and very different visual quality, where one is struggling
  • Symptoms that began suddenly, or that came with pain, redness, flashes or a change in one eye only

Symptoms are the trigger for an examination, not for a lens purchase. An optometrist can measure more than sphere and cylinder, and in some cases a corneal topographer or wavefront measurement will explain in ten minutes what an acuity test alone will never show.
