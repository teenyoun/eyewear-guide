How to Check New Glasses at Home: Optical Centre, Lens Height and Tilt

October 26, 2026 · 8 min read

What you can genuinely check at home, and what you cannot

There is a real asymmetry here, and starting with it saves a lot of wasted effort. You can check the geometry of your glasses — where light goes through the lens relative to your pupil, whether the two sides are level, whether the reading zone sits where you actually look. You cannot check power, transmittance, UV blocking or impact resistance without instruments, and phone applications that claim to measure power are not substitutes for a calibrated focimeter.

The geometry is worth checking because it is where most faults occur, and because it can be measured with a marker, a ruler and ten minutes.

Check one: the focal distance of a plus lens

Take the glasses off and hold one lens over a page of text at increasing distance from the page. For a reading lens, the text comes into sharpest focus at the lens's focal length, which by definition is 1 / F in metres. A +2.50 D lens should bring the page into focus at about 40 cm. Measure that distance and invert it in metres to get the power you are actually holding.

The sensitivity of this test is better than people expect. At +2.50 D the focal distance is 0.400 m; at +2.75 D it is 0.364 m. One 0.25 D step moves the sharp point by 36 mm, which is easily distinguishable with a ruler. At +1.00 D the same step moves it by 200 mm, so the test gets more precise as the power rises and less useful for weak lenses.

For minus lenses the test inverts: there is no real focal point on the page side, and you check minification instead. A strong minus lens makes text look smaller, and the amount scales with power and with how far the lens sits from the page. This is the same relationship that makes high prescriptions minify the world, and it is why comparing “how big things look” between contacts and glasses is not a fair test.

Check two: finding the optical centre

This is the most useful home check and the one almost nobody does. Hold the lens a few centimetres in front of one eye and look through it at a distant vertical line — a door frame works. Move the lens slowly left and right. The line appears to jump and slide, except at one position, where it stops moving and sits still.

That still point is the optical centre, because at the optical centre there is no prism, and no prism means no displacement. Mark it with a dry-erase marker on the lens. Do the same for the other lens. Now put the glasses on, look at a distant point, and have someone mark the position of each pupil on the lens. The distance between your pupil mark and the optical centre mark is the decentration error, and it is the number that matters.

Why it matters is Prentice's rule: prism in dioptres equals decentration in centimetres multiplied by power. Two millimetres of error is 0.2 cm, which in a −6.00 D lens is 1.2 Δ of unintended prism. That is enough to produce edge blur, a pulling sensation when you look sideways, and the dull frontal headache that gets blamed on “adjusting”.

Check three: lens height above the pupil

For a single-vision lens the optical centre should sit at or just below the pupil, because that is where the eye looks when viewing straight ahead and when reading. For a progressive lens the fitting cross, marked by the faint engraved cross on the lens, should sit on the pupil. Check by looking straight ahead at a mirror and seeing where the cross falls.

The usable rule: if the engraved cross is more than 2 mm above the pupil, the near corridor starts too low and the reading zone will fall short of where you look. If it is below the pupil, the distance zone is being shortened. Both are adjustments of the frame, not of the lens, which is why they are the cheapest thing to fix.

Check four: tilt and face form

Set the glasses on a flat table and look along the temples from the side. The front should tilt back by roughly 8 to 12 degrees; this is pantoscopic tilt, and it exists so the line of sight meets the lens close to perpendicular when you look slightly down. A front that stands vertical, or one that tilts more than about 15 degrees, changes the effective cylinder and adds prism. Look down from above and the two lenses should sit roughly in one plane; a strongly wrapped front adds base-in prism at the temporal edge.

What you cannot verify at home, and should not pretend to

  • Exact power. A phone app has no calibrated reference and no defined measurement plane. It can be off by a quarter dioptre or more, which is the entire step size.
  • Axis orientation as ground. You can see whether the lens is mounted straight in the frame, but not whether the cylinder was ground to the ordered axis.
  • UV blocking and transmittance. “UV400” is a claim about spectral transmission below 400 nm. It cannot be seen, and a darker tint proves nothing about it.
  • Impact resistance and coating adhesion. Both require destructive or instrumented testing; a scratch test at home damages the lens to prove a point.

The misconception to drop

“I can see clearly, so the glasses must be right.” Clear central vision is the easiest thing to achieve and the least informative. A pair can be perfectly clear straight ahead and still be 3 mm off in optical centre height, 4 degrees off in pantoscopic tilt and mounted with the reading zone 2 mm low. All three of those produce symptoms that only appear after an hour of use, which is exactly the window in which people decide the glasses are fine.

When to take them back

Take them back, with the measurements, if you find any of the following: an optical centre more than 1 mm from the pupil mark, a fitting cross more than 2 mm off the pupil, a frame that rocks on a flat surface, or prism you can feel as a jump when you slide the lens sideways. Describe what you measured rather than what you feel; “the optical centre is 3 mm nasal of my pupil” is a request a dispenser can act on, while “these feel wrong” is not.
