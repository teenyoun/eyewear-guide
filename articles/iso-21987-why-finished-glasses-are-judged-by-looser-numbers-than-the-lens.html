ISO 21987: Why Finished Glasses Are Judged by Looser Numbers Than the Lens

November 23, 2026 · 9 min read

Two documents, two different questions

Ask a lab what tolerance applies to a pair of glasses and you will usually get a number for an uncut lens — the ISO 8980 series, which governs finished lenses before anyone touches them. A different document governs the pair on your face: ISO 21987:2017, which specifies requirements and test methods for mounted spectacle lenses relative to the prescription order. That last phrase carries most of the meaning. The question is no longer "is this lens the power it claims to be" but "does this assembled pair deliver what was ordered, once it has been cut, edged, drilled and set into a frame".

The distinction is not bureaucratic. Between the two documents sit every step that can move a lens: decentration, edging stress, frame flexure, bevel geometry, and the fact that a focimeter looks through a lens along one axis while your eye looks through it along another.

Principle: a lens is not measured the way it is worn

A focimeter against the back surface measures back vertex power — the vergence leaving the back surface at one point, along the instrument axis. That is well defined, and it is not the power your eye experiences. Worn, the lens sits at a vertex distance from the cornea, is tilted forward by roughly eight to twelve degrees, and the visual axis passes through a point that may be a millimetre or two from where the instrument measured. Each difference shifts the effective power slightly.

ISO 21987 handles this with a term worth knowing: the verification power, the value the manufacturer calculates and declares as what a focimeter should find, which need not equal the ordered power. The standard says plainly that the two can differ, because the ray path through a lens on a focimeter differs from the ray path in the as-worn position, and because of physiological effects at the eye. Every tolerance table is then applied to the verification power. The standard accepts that the number on your prescription and the number a lensmeter reads are different things by design — and it tells you which one it judges.

What the numbers actually are

The optical requirements are checked on a focimeter conforming to ISO 8598-1, at a reference wavelength defined in ISO 7944, with tolerances applying at 23 °C ± 5 °C. For single-vision and multifocal lenses, the tolerance on back vertex power in each principal meridian is ±0.12 D for higher meridional powers between 0.00 and 6.00 D, widening to ±0.37 D above 20.00 D. Cylinder power has its own column, from ±0.09 D in the low-power corner to ±0.37 D at the extremes. Addition power is allowed ±0.12 D up to +4.00 D and ±0.18 D above it.

Read those as a description of what a good laboratory can hold, not as a licence. A pair sitting at the edge of every tolerance conforms and can still feel wrong, because the errors do not sit in isolation: a quarter of a dioptre in one lens, a degree or two of unwanted prism, a millimetre of vertical misplacement — each permitted, together noticeable.

Why the axis tolerance is wider on a mounted lens

ISO 21987 states in a note that its cylinder-axis tolerances have generally been increased over those in ISO 8980-1 and ISO 8980-2, specifically to allow for some tolerance in mounting. Once a lens is edged to a frame shape and seated in a groove or drill mount, small rotations are permanent, and no edging process can hold an axis to a fraction of a degree. Rather than declare a share of well-made glasses non-conforming, the standard absorbs rotation into the specification.

The optical cost is not negligible, which is why the tolerance is graded by cylinder power. Rotating a cylinder leaves a residual cross-cylinder of roughly twice the cylinder power multiplied by the sine of the rotation. A 2.00 D cylinder rotated four degrees leaves about 0.28 D of uncorrected cross-cylinder at forty-five degrees to the intended axis; a 0.50 D cylinder leaves about 0.07 D, which few wearers notice. Hence the tightening as power rises, and hence no axis requirement at all below 0.12 D of cylinder.

Prism imbalance: the requirement nobody asks about

The clause that matters most in daily wear is the one almost no wearer has heard of: prism imbalance, or relative prism error. The standard does not require each lens to be free of prism. It requires the difference between the two lenses — after any prescribed prism has been neutralised or allowed for — to fall inside a table. The logic is physiological. A little prism in front of both eyes, pointing the same way, is largely compensated by eye movement. A little prism pointing opposite ways, or a vertical difference between the lenses, cannot be compensated: the eyes are asked to diverge or to sit at different heights, and the result is one eye straining with a pull that appears only after twenty minutes of reading.

Prism imbalance is rarely a design fault. It is the arithmetic residue of decentration. Moving a lens off its optical centre to put the pupil behind it generates prism by definition, and the amount depends on power and on how far the lens moved. Two lenses of different power, decentred by the same amount, generate different prism.

The misconception worth clearing up

The common belief is that a lens is either right or wrong, and that a lensmeter reading matching the order proves it right. Both halves are wrong. A lens can read the ordered power at its centre and still make a poor pair of glasses, because that reading says nothing about where the optical centre ended up, how much prism the decentration created, whether the two lenses balance, or whether the front sits where the prescription assumed. Conversely, a lens a tenth of a dioptre off, correctly centred and balanced, often performs better than a nominally perfect, vertically mismatched one.

A second misconception: that tighter tolerances always mean better vision. A tolerance describes the worst pair that will be accepted. What determines how glasses feel is the interaction between lens, frame and face — vertex distance, tilt, optical centre height, front alignment. A precision lens in a frame sitting three millimetres low is a worse instrument than an ordinary lens in a frame that fits.

What to do when the numbers feel wrong

Bring the glasses back with a description, not a diagnosis: the left lens is sharp and the right soft at distance; the floor tilts on one side; print pulls sideways after ten minutes. Ask for the pair to be measured on a focimeter, then for the optical centre positions and the prism imbalance to be checked against the frame as fitted, not against the uncut lens. "Within tolerance" and "comfortable" are two different claims, and the second is the one you bought.
