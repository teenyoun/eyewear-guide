How Anti-Reflective Coatings Work: The Physics Behind the Purple-Green Sheen

October 06, 2026 · 9 min read

Why an uncoated lens throws light back at you

Every time light crosses from one transparent medium into another, part of it bounces back. How much depends on the size of the jump in refractive index between the two materials: the bigger the mismatch, the stronger the reflection. A plastic lens with an index near 1.5 meets air at about 1.0, and that difference sends roughly 8 per cent of the light arriving at each surface straight back towards the wearer. Two surfaces, front and back, and you have two sources of unwanted reflection before you have even considered scratches.

You have seen the consequences. The faint image of your own eye floating over the page you are reading. Halos and streaks around headlights at night. A bright window reflected in the middle of a screen you are trying to see past. None of that is dirt, and none of it is a defect. It is the physics of an index mismatch, and it cannot be wiped away.

The cure is interference, not absorption

An anti-reflective coating does not soak up the reflected light. It cancels it. The trick is to place a thin transparent layer on the surface so that light reflected from the top of that layer and light reflected from its underside emerge out of step with one another and extinguish each other.

The governing idea is the quarter-wave condition. If a layer's optical thickness - its physical thickness multiplied by its refractive index - equals one quarter of the wavelength of the light, then the two reflections come back half a wavelength out of phase and cancel. Note that the condition is stated in optical thickness, not physical thickness. A layer a quarter-wave thick for green light is not a quarter-wave layer for red, which is the whole reason the colour you see in the coating matters.

Two refinements turn this from a curiosity into a workable product. First, the layer's own refractive index should sit between that of air and that of the lens, roughly in the middle, so that its two reflected waves are of comparable strength. Waves of very different strength cancel only partly, however perfectly they are aligned in phase. Second, a single quarter-wave layer can only be tuned to cancel well near one wavelength. Practical coatings are therefore multilayer stacks: several layers of different indices and thicknesses, each tuned to a different part of the visible spectrum, so that residual reflection stays low across the whole range instead of only in the middle of it. Requirements and test methods for these coatings are specified in the ISO 8980 series; ISO 8980-4 deals specifically with anti-reflective coatings for uncut finished spectacle lenses.

Why the coating looks purple, green or blue

That colour is the residue, not the design. Because cancellation is never perfect at every wavelength at once, the light that survives is whatever was not cancelled - typically a narrow band in the violet-green-blue region. That is exactly why an AR-coated lens shows a faint purple or green sheen when you tilt it under a light. The sheen is a signature of the interference stack, and a good coating is judged by how faint and neutral that residue is, not by its absence.

Why the layers are stacked in a particular order

The order is not arbitrary, because each layer has to bond to the one beneath it and survive the ones above. The hard coat goes directly onto the lens substrate, since its job is to give a soft polymer surface a durable base. The anti-reflective stack is built on top of the hard coat. The hydrophobic and oleophobic layer goes last, because it must present the outward-facing surface - and would prevent anything else bonding to it if it were buried underneath. Layer thicknesses are controlled to fractions of a wavelength of visible light, which makes coating the most tolerance-sensitive step in making a lens, and means a coating line is judged on consistency rather than on its best sample.

What an anti-reflective coating cannot do

It does not make a lens scratch-resistant, it does not protect against impact, and it does not filter ultraviolet radiation. Those are separate functions: the hard coat beneath it, the lens material itself, and a specific UV-absorbing treatment. An AR coating is also not the same thing as a blue-light filter. Anti-reflective coatings work by cancelling reflections across the spectrum; a blue-light filter deliberately transmits less light in a chosen part of the spectrum. They solve different problems, even though both are described to shoppers using the word filter.

Common misconceptions

  • "The green sheen means the coating is cheap." Not a reliable test. Some visible sheen is expected on any interference coating. What matters is whether reflection is genuinely reduced when you look through the lens.
  • "AR coating blocks glare from headlights." It reduces reflections and the halos they cause, which does help at night, but it does not dim a bright light source.
  • "AR coating is what makes lenses easy to clean." Conventional stacks usually end with a hydrophobic and oleophobic layer for exactly that reason, but they are distinct layers and each can exist without the other.
  • "The coating is permanent." Coatings wear with time and cleaning. A coating that is hazing, crazing or lifting at the edges is a coating failure worth raising, rather than evidence that you cleaned it wrongly.

When to ask a professional

Ask if night driving glare remains a persistent problem despite coated lenses, because the cause may be an uncorrected prescription or another optical factor rather than coating quality. Ask too if coatings on previous pairs failed early - the right answer is often a different lens material or coating specification rather than simply replacing the lenses again. And if reflections are worst when you are reading at a screen, mention your working distance and screen position as well, since cutting reflected light and reducing the effort your eyes are making are two different interventions.
