Polycarbonate vs Trivex for Children's Glasses: Toughness, Optics and the Trade-Off

October 30, 2026 · 9 min read

Two materials that both pass the impact argument

Children's glasses have one non-negotiable requirement: the lens must not break into something sharp. Two materials meet that requirement and are sold interchangeably, and the differences between them are optical and mechanical rather than a matter of safety. Polycarbonate and Trivex are both high-impact plastics, both used in sport and safety eyewear, and both are routinely recommended for children. Choosing between them comes down to four numbers and one manufacturing habit.

Why these two resist impact at all

Impact resistance is not hardness. Glass is hard and brittle; polycarbonate is soft and tough. Toughness here means the ability to absorb energy by deforming rather than by propagating a crack, and it comes from the polymer's long, entangled chains. When a polycarbonate lens is struck, it yields locally, spreads the load and recovers. A brittle material concentrates stress at the impact point and releases it by cracking.

This is why the phrase bulletproof polycarbonate is misleading and why it does not matter: what matters is that the material fails by deformation rather than fragmentation, and both candidates do that. The safety standards test the finished lens in a frame, not the resin on its own.

The numbers that separate them

  • Refractive index. Polycarbonate is about 1.586, Trivex about 1.532. At the same power, polycarbonate produces a thinner lens, which is the reason it dominates high prescriptions.
  • Abbe number. Polycarbonate is around 30, Trivex around 43 to 45. The Abbe number measures dispersion, so polycarbonate spreads colours roughly 40 percent more than Trivex at the same power. On a child's first pair at a low power, nobody notices. At higher powers, and especially off-axis where the prism effect grows, the difference is visible as colour fringing on high-contrast edges.
  • Density. Polycarbonate is about 1.20 grams per cubic centimetre and Trivex about 1.11, so a Trivex lens of equal geometry is lighter, which partly offsets its greater thickness.
  • Drill behaviour. Polycarbonate is notch-sensitive: a drilled hole or a sharp rim edge concentrates stress, and cracks start there. Trivex tolerates drilling and grooving far better.

What that means for a child's pair

For a full-rim frame at a low to moderate prescription, polycarbonate is the sensible default: thinner, cheaper, abundant, and the optical penalty is small because the power is small and the lens is small. For a rimless or semi-rimless frame, or for a child whose prescription is climbing, Trivex earns its price: fewer cracks at the mounting points, better colour control, and a slightly lighter lens at the same size.

There is a third consideration that rarely appears on a spec sheet: how the child treats the glasses. A lens that survives a fall survives it because the material deforms and recovers. Repeated bending of a thin polycarbonate lens in a loose frame introduces internal stress, and stress in a transparent polymer shows up later as crazing around the drill holes or as a faint haze at the edge. Trivex is more forgiving of that treatment, which in practice is what a school bag does to a pair of glasses.

The frame is half the decision

Lens material gets the attention, but a child's pair fails at the frame more often than at the lens. A frame that fits loosely lets the lens move, and a moving lens loads the mounting points with leverage that no material was designed for. Temple length matters for the same reason: temples that are too short pull the front against the cheeks, tilting the lens and pushing the optical centre away from the pupil, which is a refractive problem rather than a comfort one.

Frame size also sets how much the material choice can matter. A small eye size puts the lens edge close to the optical centre, so thickness stays low even in a thick material, and the wrap angle stays gentle, so stress stays low. That is why a well-fitted small frame in polycarbonate usually outperforms a large fashion frame in a premium material, and why the fitting appointment is worth more than the upgrade.

Three misconceptions

Both materials are the same because both are safety lenses. They are both impact-resistant and they are not optically equivalent. The index decides thickness and the Abbe number decides colour control, and those two numbers differ by 40 percent on dispersion.

Higher index is always better for children. Index buys thinness at the cost of dispersion. For a child at a low power, a 1.59 polycarbonate is already thin, and paying for a higher index buys nothing while adding colour fringing.

Polycarbonate eventually turns yellow, so it should be avoided. Polycarbonate does yellow slightly with long ultraviolet exposure, but the effect takes years, and it is not a reason to avoid the material for a lens that will be replaced before it matters.

What to ask for at the counter

Ask which material the lens will be made from, not which brand name, and ask why. If the frame is rimless or drilled, ask whether the lab recommends Trivex for the mounting. If the prescription is above about three dioptres, ask whether the chromatic difference is worth the thickness. Those three questions get a real answer from anyone who surfaces lenses, and they are more useful than a brochure.

When to have the pair checked

Bring the glasses back if a crack appears at a drill hole, if the lens haze grows, if the child reports colour fringes on white text, or if the frame is loose enough that the lens flexes when the temples are opened. None of those are reasons to change material on their own, but together they suggest the mounting, not the resin, is the weak point.
