Six Metres, Twenty Feet: Why Test Distance Is Part of the Result

November 16, 2026 · 8 min read

A prescription is a statement about parallel light

Refraction is built on an idealisation. Light from a point at infinity arrives as parallel rays, so a relaxed eye needs a specific power to focus it, and that power is what a prescription records. Nothing in a consulting room is at infinity. The chart is at the end of the room, the room is six metres long if you are lucky, and the difference between six metres and infinity is a small, calculable amount of focusing that the eye has to supply.

The convention exists because the amount is small. It is not zero, and knowing its size explains why test distance is specified, why a short room produces a slightly different answer, and why a mirror is sometimes worth more than a longer corridor.

Vergence: light that has not had time to straighten out

Light leaving a point spreads. The measure of that spreading is vergence, expressed in dioptres as the reciprocal of the distance in metres. At one metre the vergence is 1.00 D. At two metres it is 0.50 D. At six metres it is 0.17 D. At infinity it is zero.

Optical infinity is the practical threshold at which vergence is small enough to ignore. In ophthalmic optics it is conventionally taken as six metres, or twenty feet, which is 6.1 m. The two figures are close enough that the difference between them, 0.17 D and 0.16 D, does not change a prescription.

Accommodation is the focusing the eye does to overcome vergence. At six metres a relaxed eye is 0.17 D away from perfect focus for a target at that distance, which is well inside the depth of focus for most people. At four metres the figure is 0.25 D, and a quarter of a dioptre is no longer negligible: it is the smallest step printed on a prescription.

The arithmetic of a short room

  • 6 m: 0.17 D of vergence, conventionally ignored.
  • 5 m: 0.20 D, still small, and the standard distance in many metric countries.
  • 4 m: 0.25 D, exactly one prescription step.
  • 3 m: 0.33 D, more than one step, and enough to matter in a borderline case.
  • 1 m: 1.00 D, which is near testing, not distance testing.

The error runs in a predictable direction. Testing closer than infinity adds convergence to the target, and a patient who can accommodate will accommodate to meet it, which biases the result toward more minus and less plus. A young hyperope tested at three metres may look less hyperopic than they are; a myope tested at three metres may look slightly more myopic. Neither error is large, and both are the reason a test distance is specified rather than left to the furniture.

Why mirrors are not a compromise

A three-metre room with a mirror at one end and the chart beside the patient produces a six-metre optical path, because the light travels to the mirror and back. The room is short, the optical distance is not, and the chart appears at the correct scale if the mirror is plane and the chart is sized for the doubled distance. This is a legitimate solution used in small practices, and it is worth knowing about if a room feels cramped: the constraint is optical distance, not floor area.

The mirror does introduce two practical requirements. It has to be flat, because a curved mirror adds power of its own, and it has to be clean, because a dirty mirror scatters light and reduces the contrast of the target. Both are easy to check and easy to neglect.

What this means for the numbers you are given

A refraction is a measurement with a stated condition attached, and the condition includes the distance. Two practitioners using six metres and five metres will usually agree within a quarter of a dioptre, which is the tolerance the whole system is built around. A practitioner working at three metres without compensating is measuring a different thing, and the difference is systematic rather than random.

The same logic explains a question people ask about their own prescriptions: why a reading addition is described in terms of a distance at all. A +2.00 add is a statement about a 50 cm working distance, because 1 divided by 0.50 m is 2.00 D. The add, the test distance and the reading distance are all the same arithmetic seen from different ends.

Three beliefs worth correcting

The machine is accurate, so distance does not matter. An autorefractor has its own internal target distance and its own assumptions, and it is a starting point rather than a result. The subjective refraction that follows is where distance becomes explicit.

If I read the same line at three metres as at six, my prescription is the same. Acuity can be identical while the refractive endpoint differs, because accommodation covers small vergence differences in one person and not in another.

Twenty feet and six metres are different tests. They differ by 10 cm and 0.01 D. The convention treats them as the same distance for the sound reason that they are.

When the distance question is worth raising

Ask about test distance if your prescription has changed by a quarter of a dioptre in a way that does not match your experience, if two recent tests disagree in a consistent direction, or if a new pair is clear at the optician's and soft at home. Those are questions about method, and they are reasonable ones to ask.

See an eyecare professional rather than adjusting a prescription yourself if vision is blurred at all distances, if the change is sudden or one-sided, or if the same pair reads differently from day to day. A quarter of a dioptre of vergence is an interesting detail; a change in how you see is a reason to be examined.
