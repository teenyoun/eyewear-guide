Polarized vs UV400 — What's the Difference and Do You Need Both?

August 12, 2026 · 6 min read

'Polarized' and 'UV400' are the two most common labels on sunglasses — and they mean completely different things. A pair can have one, both, or neither. Here is what each actually does, when you need them, and when you do not.

The Short Answer

UV400 protects your eyes (health). It blocks 99-100% of ultraviolet radiation that causes cataracts, macular degeneration, and eye surface damage.

Polarization protects your vision quality (comfort). It eliminates reflected glare from water, roads, snow, and car windshields.

Everyone needs UV400. Polarization is a lifestyle choice.

UV400: The Health Essential

UV400 means the lenses block all ultraviolet light up to 400 nanometers wavelength — covering both UVA (315-400nm) and UVB (280-315nm). This is not about comfort; it is about preventing cumulative eye damage. UV exposure contributes to:

  • Cataracts — the leading cause of preventable blindness worldwide
  • Macular degeneration — UV damage to the central retina accumulates over decades
  • Pterygium (surfer's eye) — a growth on the eye surface from UV + wind exposure
  • Photokeratitis (snow blindness) — acute UV burn of the cornea

UV400 is cheap to manufacture. It adds roughly $0.05 to the cost of a lens. Any sunglasses without UV400 protection are either incredibly cheap (sub-$3 manufacturing cost) or fraudulent. The $10 gas-station sunglasses with a UV400 sticker are likely providing the same UV protection as $200 designer shades.

Polarization: The Comfort Upgrade

Polarized lenses contain a chemical film that filters light based on orientation. Light reflecting off flat surfaces (water, road, snow, car hoods) becomes horizontally polarized — aligned in one direction. The polarized filter blocks this specific orientation, eliminating glare without darkening everything else.

The effect is dramatic: driving on a sunny day, the road surface goes from a washed-out white blur to visible asphalt with clear lane markings. On water, you can see below the surface instead of a mirror reflection. But polarization has downsides:

  • LCD screens become unreadable at certain angles: Most car navigation screens, gas pump displays, and some phone screens use polarized LCDs that turn black through polarized glasses
  • May reduce depth perception on snow/ice: Polarization removes the glare that helps you distinguish ice from wet pavement or ski slope texture
  • More expensive: $10-40 premium over non-polarized lenses

What to Buy by Activity

  • Driving: Polarized + UV400 — glare reduction is a safety feature
  • Beach / Water: Polarized + UV400 — water reflection is intense
  • Hiking / Walking: UV400 is sufficient; polarization optional
  • Skiing / Snow: UV400 essential (snow reflects 80% of UV); polarization controversial — some skiers prefer non-polarized to see ice patches
  • Fashion / Indoor-outdoor: UV400 minimum; skip polarization if you look at LCD screens frequently
