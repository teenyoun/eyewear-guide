The FDA Drop-Ball Test: What 21 CFR 801.410 Asks of Every Lens

November 12, 2026 · 9 min read

A federal rule, not a marketing claim

In the United States, impact resistance in eyewear is not a premium feature. Title 21 of the Code of Federal Regulations, section 801.410, requires that eyeglasses and sunglasses be fitted with impact-resistant lenses, with a narrow clinical exemption. The rule covers prescription and non-prescription eyewear alike, and it explicitly excludes contact lenses. Every pair of glasses sold in the US is therefore supposed to satisfy a test with a published protocol, a published projectile and a published pass criterion.

That is worth understanding for two reasons. It tells you what the phrase "impact resistant" actually certifies, and it separates a baseline federal requirement from the occupational and sports standards that people routinely confuse it with.

The test itself

The regulation defines a referee test: a 5/8-inch steel ball weighing approximately 0.56 ounce is dropped from a height of 50 inches onto the horizontal upper surface of the lens. The ball must strike within a 5/8-inch circle at the geometric centre of the lens, and it may be guided by a tube extending to within about 4 inches of the surface, but it may not be restricted in its fall. The lens rests on a support tube of 1 inch inside diameter and 1¼ inch outside diameter, roughly 1 inch high, bonded to a rigid base plate weighing at least 27 pounds.

The numbers are small; the energy is not trivial. A 5/8-inch steel ball is about 15.9 mm across and roughly 16 grams, and a 50-inch fall is about 127 cm. That combination delivers close to 0.2 joule into a 5/8-inch circle of lens — a blow concentrated on a spot the size of a fingertip.

Passing is defined narrowly. The lens must not fracture, and fracture has a specific meaning here: a crack running through the entire thickness, including any laminate layer, across a complete diameter into two or more separate pieces, or any lens material visible to the naked eye becoming detached from the ocular surface. A lens that chips at the edge but holds together has not failed the test as written. A lens that splits in half has.

Who tests what, and why that matters

The regulation distinguishes between lens types, and the distinction explains a lot about how eyewear is made. Each finished impact-resistant glass lens intended for prescription use must be tested individually. Plastic prescription lenses and all non-prescription lenses may be tested by statistically significant sampling from each production batch, provided the samples represent the finished form as worn, including the thinnest forms and any treatment used to impart impact resistance. Records of testing and distribution must be kept for three years.

Individual testing of glass is not bureaucratic fussiness. Glass acquires its impact resistance from tempering or chemical strengthening, both of which create a compressed surface layer balanced by internal tension. Cut through that layer and you cut the strength with it. This is why a tempered glass lens cannot be re-edged to a smaller frame the way a plastic lens can, and why a glass lens that has been drilled or grooved is a different object from the one that passed.

What the rule does not say

It does not say the lens is unbreakable. It sets a minimum demonstration with one projectile at one energy. A stone thrown up by a lawnmower, a fall onto a kerb, a door edge at head height — these are not the reference test.

It does not regulate fit or coverage. A compliant lens in a frame that sits away from the eye leaves the orbit exposed, and lateral impact can reach the eye without touching the lens at all. Coverage is a frame geometry question, not a lens compliance question.

It does not replace occupational or sports standards. ANSI Z87.1 covers workplace eye and face protection and adds a high-impact test that fires a much smaller projectile at high velocity against the complete device, frame included. ASTM F803 covers specific racket and ball sports and tests the assembled protector. A pair of dress glasses passing 801.410 tells you nothing about either.

The claims that need correcting

Impact resistant means shatterproof. The regulation deliberately uses the language of resistance. Glass that passes can still break; what changes is how it breaks, and whether the material crossing the failure lands in pieces large enough to be defined as fracture under the rule. The practical benefit is real, but it is a reduction in risk, not a guarantee.

Any lens marked impact resistant has been tested in the frame you bought. The regulation tests lenses — individually for finished prescription glass, by sampling for plastics. It is a lens-level requirement. A rimless mount holds a lens by drill holes, and a grooved or drilled lens concentrates stress at those points, which is a mechanical property of the mounting rather than a certification of the lens.

Children get the same protection from any compliant lens. Compliance is a floor. Polycarbonate and Trivex are used in eyewear for children not because other materials fail the regulation but because their failure behaviour and their resistance to cracking at thin sections are better suited to how children actually treat glasses.

The exemption means some people wear unsafe lenses. The rule allows a physician or optometrist to direct, in writing, the use of other lenses where impact-resistant ones will not meet a patient's visual requirements, with written notice to the patient. It is a documented clinical decision, not a loophole for the optician to choose.

What to do with this

If you are choosing between materials for a child, for sport, or for a workshop, the useful question is not whether a lens is impact resistant but which standard applies to the activity and what that standard actually tests. For ordinary dress wear, the federal baseline applies and is met by nearly everything on a legitimate shelf. For a specific hazard, the standard number on the product is the thing to read.

One point belongs with a clinician rather than with a regulation. If an eye has already been struck, or if you notice a change in vision after an impact — pain, light sensitivity, a shadow at the edge of the field, floaters that appeared suddenly — that is a same-day assessment, not a question about lens compliance. A lens that survives the drop test says nothing about the eye behind it.
