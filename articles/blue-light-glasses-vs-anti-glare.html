Blue Light Glasses vs Anti-Glare: What's the Difference?

Updated July 18, 2026 · 6 min read

"Blue light" and "anti-glare" are two of the most common lens coating options you'll see when buying glasses — whether prescription glasses online or over-the-counter readers. They're often confused, sometimes marketed as the same thing, and frequently bundled together. But they do very different things.

Here's the short version: blue light filtering blocks a specific wavelength of light. Anti-glare (AR) coating reduces reflections across all visible light. Most people benefit from anti-glare. Whether you need blue light filtering depends on when and how you use screens.

Deep Dive: Blue Light Filtering

Blue light filtering lenses contain a pigment or coating that absorbs a portion of high-energy visible (HEV) light in the blue-violet spectrum (380-500nm). The filtering can range from 10-90% depending on the lens type.

  • Daytime lenses (40-60% blocking): Minimal color distortion for office use.
  • Evening lenses (70-90% blocking): Noticeable amber tint; used 2-3 hours before bed for sleep benefits.
  • Clear blue light lenses (10-20% blocking): The "barely-there" option; limited evidence of benefit.

Deep Dive: Anti-Glare Coating

Anti-reflective (AR) coating is a microscopically thin multilayer film applied to lens surfaces. It works by causing light reflecting off the lens to interfere with itself (destructive interference), canceling out reflections. A good AR coating reduces surface reflections from ~8% (uncoated) to less than 1%.

  • Standard AR: Basic reflection reduction. Included with most online glasses.
  • Premium AR: Includes oleophobic (oil-resistant) and hydrophobic (water-resistant) top layers. Much easier to clean and stays clearer longer.
  • Super-hydrophobic AR: Top-tier coating that actively repels water and smudges. Worth the upgrade if you clean your glasses frequently.

Can You Get Both?

Yes. Many glasses — especially from online retailers like Zenni and EyeBuyDirect — let you add both blue light filtering and anti-glare coating to the same pair. This is ideal if you:

  • Use screens for hours in a brightly lit office (AR reduces overhead glare; blue light filter helps with evening screen use)
  • Drive at night (AR reduces headlight glare; blue light filtering may reduce glare sensitivity)
  • Want the clearest possible vision in all conditions

Which One Should You Get?

Bottom line: Get AR coating on every pair of glasses — it's cheap and universally beneficial. Add blue light filtering only if you use screens within 2-3 hours of bedtime or find yourself sensitive to bright screens in the evening.

For most people, the upgrade priority is: AR coating > high-index lenses (if prescription is strong) > blue light filtering > photochromic (Transitions).
