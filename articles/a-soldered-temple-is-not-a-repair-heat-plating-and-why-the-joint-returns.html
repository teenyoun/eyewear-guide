A Soldered Temple Is Not a Repair: Heat, Plating and Why the Joint Returns

November 24, 2026 · 9 min read

The joint that fails twice

A temple snaps at the hinge, or the bridge parts from the rim, and the frame is otherwise perfect. Somewhere in the building is a person with a soldering iron who will fix it for a small sum. Three weeks later the same spot is loose again, or the metal beside the joint has turned dull, or the plating has lifted in a ring around the repair. The frame is not unlucky. It has been through a process that changes the metal, and the process is the reason the repair does not hold.

Understanding what heat does to a spectacle frame explains three things at once: why a soldered joint is weaker than the temple it joins, why the failure usually migrates to the edges of the repair, and why some frames cannot be soldered at all.

Principle: soldering is a low-temperature join between dissimilar metals

Solder does not melt the parts it joins; it wets them and freezes, forming a metallic bridge. That is the whole advantage — the frame never reaches its own melting point — and the whole limitation. Soft solders used in frame repair melt between roughly 180 °C and 230 °C. Tin-lead near the eutectic composition, the alloy ratio that melts at a single temperature rather than over a range, is liquid at about 183 °C; common lead-free tin-silver and tin-silver-copper solders need around 217 °C to 221 °C. Silver brazing alloys sit far higher, in the six-hundreds, which is why brazing is a different process with different risks.

Those temperatures are modest for metal and severe for everything else on a pair of glasses. Plating systems — nickel, palladium, gold, chrome, and thin decorative layers over them — are measured in fractions of a micrometre to a few micrometres. At soldering temperature they oxidise, blister or diffuse into the underlying metal. Lacquer and powder coatings degrade outright. Stainless steel develops temper colours in the same range, the pale straw-to-blue tint familiar from exhaust pipes, which is a visible signature of heat and an invisible one of altered surface chemistry.

Why the metal beside the joint fails next

Most frame metals are supplied work-hardened. Nickel silver and monel are rolled and drawn to gain stiffness in thin sections; stainless is cold-worked for the same reason. Heating a work-hardened alloy above its recrystallisation range removes that hardening. The result is a heat-affected zone: a band a few millimetres wide on either side of the joint that is now softer than the rest of the temple. When the frame flexes during normal handling, that band bends first and bends permanently. The solder joint may hold perfectly while the metal beside it fatigues, which is why a repaired temple often droops a week later rather than snapping.

There is a second, slower mechanism. Solder and frame metal are different alloys with different electrochemical potentials, and sweat is an electrolyte. In a chloride-rich environment the solder acts as the anode and corrodes preferentially — galvanic corrosion. Add flux residue, which is often acidic and hygroscopic, sitting in the crevice between solder and metal, and you have a small corrosion cell that stays active long after the repair. This is the origin of the greenish or whitish crust that appears around old repairs, and of the pitting that eventually undermines them.

Why some frames cannot be soldered at all

Titanium is the clearest case. It oxidises instantly and tenaciously in air, and that oxide layer prevents solder from wetting the surface, so a soft-soldered titanium joint is a mechanical smear rather than a metallurgical bond. Titanium also melts around 1668 °C, so any genuine joining process is welding under inert gas or with a laser, done by someone equipped for it. A jeweller with a torch and tin-lead solder is not equipped for it.

Cast zinc alloy components — common in low-cost fronts and in decorative endpieces — melt in the region of 380 °C. That is close enough to soldering temperature that a thin casting can sag or lose its shape before the solder flows. Aluminium is worse: it conducts heat away from the joint and forms its own refractory oxide. Acetate, TR90 and other polymer frames have no soldering route at all; they are repaired by solvent bonding or replacement.

Common misconceptions

"Any metal can be soldered by anyone with an iron." Wetting depends on the specific alloy, its oxide, and the flux. Monel, nickel silver and stainless are solderable with the right flux; titanium, aluminium and thin zinc castings are not.

"A soldered joint is as strong as the original." Soft solder is far weaker in tensile strength than the steel or nickel alloy it joins, and its strength falls further as it creeps under sustained load. A joint that survives a static test can fail under the repeated small flexing of putting glasses on and taking them off.

"If it looks fine, it is fine." The visible problem is the joint; the invisible one is the annealed band beside it and the corrosion cell underneath. Both appear later.

"Repair is always cheaper than replacement." For a hinge screw, yes. For a fracture in a thin drawn temple, the repair costs a finish, a heat-affected zone and a corrosion site, and it usually returns. The arithmetic often favours a replacement temple from the same model, or a new frame.

What a good repair actually looks like

  • Match the process to the metal. Stainless and nickel alloys: silver solder or laser welding, not plumbing solder. Titanium: laser or TIG under shielding gas. Zinc castings: replacement, not heat.
  • Remove the finish first, restore it after. Plating must be stripped locally, the joint made, then re-plated or the area refinished. A repair that leaves bare metal exposed will corrode at that spot.
  • Neutralise the flux. Residual flux is the slow half of the failure. Any repair that is not rinsed and neutralised will come back.
  • Respect the geometry. A temple that has been heated and bent loses its original curve, and the fit changes. A repair that restores the joint but not the fit has moved the problem to your ear.

When to stop repairing

Replace rather than repair when the fracture is in a thin drawn section, when the frame is titanium or an unknown alloy presented without documentation, when the same joint has already failed once, or when the repair quote approaches the cost of a replacement frame. If the frame is under warranty, soldering voids it — and if the break happened while the glasses were on your face, the fit is worth reviewing too, because a frame that is too tight at the temples loads the hinge on every removal.
