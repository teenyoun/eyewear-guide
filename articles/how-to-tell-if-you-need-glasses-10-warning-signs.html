How to Tell If You Need Glasses — 10 Warning Signs

July 31, 2026 · 7 min read

Refractive error develops so slowly that the brain rebuilds its expectations around it. You do not notice that faces have become slightly soft, or that the television has crept closer — you notice the day someone hands you their glasses and the world snaps into focus. Each of these ten signs has a physical reason behind it.

What “needing glasses” actually means

Refractive error, or ametropia, is a mismatch between the optical power of your eye and its length. Power is measured in dioptres, where one dioptre is the reciprocal of focal length in metres:

D = 1 / f

The far point is the furthest distance at which a relaxed eye sees clearly — at infinity for an emmetropic eye, a point in front of the face for a myopic one, which is why distance vision blurs first. Hyperopia is the opposite: optically too weak for its length, and a young hyperope pulls the image forward by accommodating with the crystalline lens. Astigmatism is different again: the two principal meridians focus at different distances, giving two focal lines instead of one point.

Prescriptions are written in 0.25 D steps because that is roughly the smallest change most people can reliably detect in a side-by-side comparison, not because the eye has a 0.25 D threshold built into it.

Why blur happens at all

An out-of-focus point of light becomes a disc, not a fuzzy point. Its retinal size scales with pupil width and defocus:

b ≈ 17 mm × a × ΔD

where a is pupil diameter in metres and ΔD is defocus in dioptres. A 4 mm pupil with 1.00 D of uncorrected defocus produces a blur disc about 0.07 mm across, against a resolution limit near 0.005 mm. That is why even a quarter of a dioptre can be worth correcting, and why the same prescription bothers you more at night: a dark-adapted pupil of 6–7 mm widens the blur disc by half again while the retina is working with less light.

It also explains squinting: narrowing the lids shrinks the effective aperture and the blur disc while increasing depth of field, which is what a pinhole does deliberately.

The ten signs, and what each one means

  1. Frequent squinting. Aperture narrowing, as above. Squinting to read, or to recognise a face down a corridor, is the commonest first symptom of myopia or an under-corrected prescription.
  2. Headaches after reading or screen work. Sustained accommodation loads the ciliary muscle the way holding a weight loads an arm. Uncorrected hyperopia and low astigmatism are the classic causes: the eye compensates, until it cannot do so comfortably for an hour.
  3. Holding things further away. This is presbyopia, not disease. Accommodative amplitude falls predictably with age; Hofstetter's minimum expected amplitude is 15 − 0.25 × age, giving 5 D at 40 and 2.5 D at 50. Near work that once sat inside your range moves outside it.
  4. Road signs sharp only at the last moment. A far point sitting a few metres in front of the face. It can appear at any age, and often shows up first as trouble reading signs in rain or at dusk, when contrast drops.
  5. Halos and starbursts around lights at night. Astigmatism scatters light along the interval between its two focal lines; early lens opacity scatters it in all directions. Both worsen as the pupil dilates after dark, which is why this is a night symptom.
  6. Double vision, even occasional. Two images mean the eyes are not pointing together consistently — a decompensating phoria or a tropia. This deserves an appointment, not a wait-and-see.
  7. Slow adaptation between bright and dark. Rod-driven dark adaptation takes time by design, but a marked change from your own baseline can point to retinal or lenticular change.
  8. Closing one eye to read. A habit suggesting the two eyes contribute unequal images — anisometropia, where refractions differ by a dioptre or more, or long-standing suppression.
  9. Rubbing your eyes often. Usually fatigue or allergy, but persistent hard rubbing is associated with progression of keratoconus, a corneal thinning that distorts vision in ways glasses struggle to fix. Stop the habit before you know the cause.
  10. Sitting closer to the television. In children this is often the first visible sign of myopia: a child has no baseline to compare against and simply moves until the image is big and sharp enough.

The signs that mean “today, not next month”

Sudden loss of vision, a curtain or shadow across part of the field, a shower of new floaters with flashes, eye pain with redness and halos, or straight lines that look bent. None of these is a refractive error, and none of them is improved by new glasses. They need an examination now.

Myths worth clearing up

“20/20 means my eyes are healthy.” Visual acuity is one measurement. A 20/20 letter subtends five arcminutes at six metres, each stroke one arcminute; that tests central resolution and nothing else. Contrast sensitivity, visual field, colour vision and retinal health are separate, and glaucoma can take field before it takes acuity.

“Reading in dim light ruins your eyes.” It causes temporary strain, not damage; no mechanism turns low light into permanent refractive change.

“Glasses make your eyes lazy.” The refractive error is unchanged either way. What changes is that clear vision is easier to prefer, which people experience as dependence.

“Children grow out of needing glasses.” Axial length changes with growth and some low hyperopia does reduce. That is an observation, not a plan: untreated refractive error in a child can leave a permanent deficit.

“I would know if I needed glasses.” Hyperopes in particular compensate for years, invisibly. This is the commonest reason a first pair lands as a surprise.

What an eye exam actually measures

A comprehensive examination is not a machine reading your prescription. It is a history, an acuity measurement, an objective estimate by autorefractor or retinoscopy, then a subjective refraction — the “which is better, one or two” sequence — refined to binocular balance so both eyes carry a similar load. Near vision and an add follow if you are presbyopic, then a health check of the anterior segment and fundus, with dilation where indicated. Bring your current glasses and a medication list; both change what the examiner looks for.

Published schedules move from roughly every two years in mid-adulthood to annually from about sixty, sooner with diabetes, a family history of glaucoma or a high prescription. Our guide to exam intervals by age sets out the reasoning.

Can an app do this instead?

App-based refraction can reasonably renew a prescription you already have, where the law allows it, for someone with no symptoms and no risk factors. It measures sphere and cylinder under controlled conditions and nothing else: it cannot see your optic disc, macula or intraocular pressure, where the conditions that threaten sight live. If any of the ten signs is new, an app is the wrong instrument.
