Welding Shade Numbers: What the Shade Scale Actually Measures

November 07, 2026 · 8 min read

What the number on the filter actually labels

A welding shade number is not a brightness dial and it is not a measure of ultraviolet protection. It is a label for how much visible light the filter passes, expressed on a scale that runs from nearly clear at the bottom to very dark at the top. The scale and the tolerances attached to it are defined in filter-lens standards: EN 169 in Europe, and the shade tables carried into ANSI Z87.1 and referenced by the US Occupational Safety and Health Administration in 29 CFR 1910.133. Everyday welding filters occupy roughly shade 2 through shade 14; the steps are coarse, and one step up is a large reduction in transmitted light rather than a fixed percentage.

That distinction matters because the protection is doing two different jobs at once. The darkness controls how much visible light reaches the retina, which is a comfort and adaptation question. The ultraviolet and infrared absorption is a materials question, set by the filter's composition and specified separately for every shade. A filter can be dark enough to weld with and still fail the ultraviolet requirement, which is precisely why welding filters are certified as filters rather than sold as "dark glass".

How the number is chosen

Three inputs drive the choice: the process, the current, and for some processes the electrode size or plate thickness. OSHA's Table I-1 in 1910.133 sets a minimum protective shade of 10 for shielded metal arc welding with electrodes from 1/16 to 5/32 inch, and common arc practice spans shade 10 to shade 14 as amperage rises. Gas welding and cutting sit much lower on the scale, because the arc is absent and the luminous intensity is far lower. The standards themselves offer the practical rule: start with a shade too dark to see the weld zone clearly, then step down until the work is visible without glare. Starting dark and lightening protects the eyes during the search; starting light and darkening does not.

Auto-darkening helmets add a second, independent number. Their light state is typically around shade 3 to 4 so the wearer can see to position the torch, and the filter switches to the working shade when the arc strikes. Switching speed and the number of sensors are separate specifications from the shade itself, and a helmet can have an excellent dark state and a sluggish switch.

The error: treating shade as a protection rating

The most widespread mistake is reading the shade number as though it were a safety score — the assumption that shade 14 protects best and shade 9 is for people who do not care. It does not work that way. Every compliant filter blocks ultraviolet and infrared to the limits set for its shade, and the visible-light figure is chosen for the task, not maximised. Welders who run a shade far darker than the process needs tend to compensate by moving their head closer to the arc, or by lifting the helmet to see the puddle — both of which increase exposure. The correct shade for low-amperage work is the one that lets you see the puddle and the joint without squinting.

The second error is assuming that any dark lens will do. Sunglasses, even very dark ones, are not welding filters: they are not required to absorb the infrared load that an arc produces, and their tint is not specified against a shade scale. Welding with tinted sunglasses is a way to accumulate a retinal and lenticular dose while feeling that the glare has been dealt with.

Infrared is the slow part

Ultraviolet damage to the front of the eye is fast and painful. Infrared damage is the opposite: it is cumulative, painless, and associated with occupational exposure over years rather than a single event, which is why the infrared transmittance limits in the filter standards exist at all. The classic occupational picture is a lens opacity in people who spent careers beside hot sources without an adequate filter. It is also the reason a welding filter cannot be judged by eye: an infrared-transparent filter looks perfectly dark and feels perfectly comfortable.

Arc eye arrives hours later

Ultraviolet burn of the cornea — photokeratitis, or arc eye — typically does not hurt while it is happening. Symptoms appear some hours after exposure and include a gritty foreign-body sensation, tearing, marked light sensitivity, and sometimes a dull ache that makes the eyes hard to open. The delay is the trap: welders assume that because nothing hurt at the time, nothing happened, and are surprised that evening. The cornea's surface layer regenerates, so most episodes settle over a day or two, but a severe burn, a retained foreign body, or any drop in vision needs to be assessed rather than waited out.

Where the standards stop and judgement starts

A shade table answers the question "what filter for this process at this current". It does not answer "is this helmet the right one for the way I actually work" — grinding next to the arc, a colleague welding two metres away, a reflective surface behind the workpiece, or a helmet left on a bench with a solar cell in shadow. Those are the exposures that the shade number never sees. If your work involves welding, cutting, or brazing alongside others, the filter has to be matched to the brightest source in the room, not only to your own torch.

If you have had an episode of arc eye, or notice that one eye sees a duller or yellower image than the other, that is an eye examination question rather than a shopping decision. Filter selection is a standard-driven calculation; the state of your cornea and lens afterwards is a clinical finding.
