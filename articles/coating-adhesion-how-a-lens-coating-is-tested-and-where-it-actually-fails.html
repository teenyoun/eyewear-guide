Coating Adhesion: How a Lens Coating Is Tested, and Where It Actually Fails

November 21, 2026 · 9 min read

Why a coating fails at a boundary, not in the middle

A modern spectacle lens is not one material. It is a substrate carrying a stack: a hard coat for abrasion, a multilayer anti-reflective stack, then a hydrophobic top layer. The anti-reflective layers are a fraction of a wavelength thick — tens of nanometres each — while the hard coat is measured in micrometres. Every layer is bonded to the one below it, and every boundary between two chemistries is a potential failure plane.

Adhesion is the sum of the forces holding that boundary together: chemical bonding where the deposited oxide can react with available groups on the substrate surface, van der Waals attraction, and mechanical interlocking where the surface has been etched to give the coating something to grip. Failure is described by where it happens. Adhesive failure means the coating separated from the layer beneath it, leaving that layer intact. Cohesive failure means a layer split within itself. The distinction matters because the two point at completely different causes: a contaminated interface versus a stress or chemistry problem inside a layer.

The reason polymer substrates are hard to coat is thermal expansion. An optical polymer expands roughly two orders of magnitude more than the inorganic oxides deposited on it, so the coating sits under permanent mechanical mismatch: rigid, thin, and glued to something that moves. Add water, which migrates along the interface and hydrolyses silane bonds, and heat, and you have the three ingredients of almost every peeling complaint.

What the test standards actually ask for

The international framework for optical coatings is the ISO 9211 series. Part 3 defines environmental durability categories — the severity classes a coating is being asked to survive. Part 4 specifies the test methods themselves: abrasion, adhesion, and resistance to water. Part 5 sets minimum requirements for antireflecting coatings. In other words, Part 5 says what performance is required, Part 3 says under what conditions, and Part 4 says how to measure it so two laboratories can disagree productively rather than arbitrarily.

The adhesion method in ISO 9211-4 is the tape test. A pressure-sensitive tape of specified type is pressed onto the coated surface, then peeled at a controlled angle and rate. In the cross-hatch variant a grid of cuts is scribed first, so the test samples many small areas and the result is scored by how much of the grid lifted. A pass means no coating transferred to the tape, or no squares removed. It is deliberately crude: designed to be repeatable across labs and operators, not to reproduce your cleaning habits.

The abrasion method is equally blunt: a loaded eraser or a pad of cheesecloth is stroked across the surface a set number of times, and the change in haze is measured before and after. Water resistance is tested by soaking the sample and repeating the adhesion check, because the interesting failure is not water alone but water plus a bond that was marginal to begin with.

Where real lenses actually fail

Ask any optical lab where peeling starts and the answer is rarely the centre. It starts at the edge, for three compounding reasons. Edging cuts through the stack and leaves an exposed cross-section where water can enter the interface sideways. The bevel and the frame put the lens under bending stress, and a coating that is rigid does not enjoy being bent. And the edge is the region most often touched, wiped and knocked.

The common failure signatures sort themselves by cause:

  • Lifting or peeling from the edge inward — adhesive failure driven by water ingress and edge exposure. Usually progressive, and usually noticed first as a faint line near the rim.
  • Crazing, a web of fine cracks — cohesive failure from stress, thermal cycling or solvent attack. Frequently appears after a hot car, a hot rinse, or an alcohol-based cleaner left to soak.
  • Pinholes and isolated spots — contamination during deposition. A particle on the substrate prevents local bonding and the coating simply did not attach there.
  • Iridescent blotches or a milky cloud — water or chemistry inside the stack, changing how the layers interfere with light rather than removing them.

Three of those four are use conditions, not manufacturing errors — the uncomfortable part of the topic.

The misconception worth correcting

The most persistent claim is that if a coating peels, the lab did it wrong. Sometimes that is true — a skipped caustic etch, an expired bath, a contaminated vacuum chamber will all produce coatings that fail the tape test. But a coating that passed adhesion testing can still delaminate in service, because the tape test applies a peel force for a second and your face applies heat, moisture, salt, sunscreen and friction for two years. The mechanism that actually kills coatings is cumulative: a scratch breaches the top layer, water enters at that breach, hydrolysis weakens the interface, and thermal cycling levers the weakened area apart.

A second misconception is that the hard coat and the anti-reflective stack are the same thing, so paying for "better AR" should also buy scratch resistance. They are separate layers with separate jobs, tested by separate methods in the same standard. A lens can have excellent adhesion and poor abrasion performance, or the reverse, and neither result tells you about the other.

What this means when you are choosing and caring for a lens

Because edge exposure dominates, two practical decisions matter more than any claim on a specification sheet. First, avoid oversizing the lens into a frame that flexes it; a lens that is squeezed into a rim is pre-stressed before you wear it. Second, treat the edge as the vulnerable part: rinse with water and a drop of neutral soap, blot with a clean microfibre cloth, and skip dry wiping entirely when grit is present. Solvents, hot water and long soaks accelerate it fastest.

If your prescription is high, the exposed edge area is larger and the coating has more perimeter to defend — one of the less obvious costs of a high-power lens, and a reason to check how thick the edge will be before comparing quoted prices for the same coating name.

When to stop cleaning and see someone

Coating damage is not an eye emergency, but it is an optical problem long before it is a cosmetic one. Scatter from a crazed or lifting coating reduces contrast, and the effect shows up first at night, in the form of halos and starbursts around lights. If you are noticing new glare, ghost images, or a hazy patch that moves with the lens rather than with your eye, have the lenses inspected before you assume your prescription has changed.

If the damage appeared within the warranty period, photograph it before touching it again and ask whether the failure is at the edge or across the whole surface — the pattern changes who carries the cost. Persistent blur after cleaning, or discomfort, redness or a foreign-body sensation, is a matter for an optometrist rather than a coating discussion. Nothing here diagnoses a lens or an eye; it explains the physics so the conversation at the counter starts from the right place.
