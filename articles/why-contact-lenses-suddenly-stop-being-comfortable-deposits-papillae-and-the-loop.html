Why Contact Lenses Suddenly Stop Being Comfortable: Deposits, Papillae and the Loop

November 27, 2026 · 9 min read

The lenses that used to be fine

Contact lens intolerance rarely arrives as a single event. It usually shows up as a slow negotiation: the lenses that were comfortable for six hours are comfortable for four, then the wearer starts carrying the case everywhere, then removal at the end of the day produces itching that was never there before. The instinct is to blame the lens material and ask for a different brand. Sometimes that works. Often the material was never the problem, and the cause is a chain of events that started on the lens surface.

The principle: a deposit film changes what the eyelid is rubbing against

A soft lens worn on the eye accumulates a biological film within minutes. Tear proteins and lipids adsorb onto and into the material, and the quantity depends on the polymer, the water content, the care system and the individual tear film. This film is not visible and does not by itself make a lens uncomfortable.

What it does is change the surface that slides under the upper eyelid with every blink, roughly ten thousand times a day. A coated lens is rougher and immunologically more provocative than a clean one, and the tissue it rubs against, the upper tarsal conjunctiva, is the part of the lining that reacts. The response is papillary hypertrophy: the conjunctiva thickens into bumps. When those bumps exceed about a millimetre in diameter they are described as giant papillae, and the condition is called giant papillary conjunctivitis.

The chain then closes on itself. Roughened conjunctiva produces more mucus and more mechanical irritation, which destabilises the lens and increases deposit formation, which provokes more papillae. This is why intolerance that is allowed to run often worsens faster than the wearer expects: the mechanism is a loop, not a slope.

What the loop looks like in practice

  • Itching on removal rather than during wear. This is one of the more specific signs, because the eyelid has been in contact with the lens all day and the inflammatory mediators are released when it stops.
  • Increasing lens awareness. The lens that used to disappear is now felt, especially at the edge.
  • Mucus and coating. A lens that comes out with a greasy film, or a string of mucus at the inner corner, points at the deposit pathway rather than at a bad prescription.
  • Lens movement and decentration. A thickened conjunctiva changes the lid-lens interaction, so a lens that was well fitted starts riding high or rotating. For a toric lens that means the axis drifts and vision fluctuates, which is often misreported as "the prescription stopped working".
  • Reduced wearing time that does not recover after a break. Short breaks help early on; once the papillae are established, a week off may not be enough.

The other causes that get mistaken for a bad material

Deposits and papillary response are the most common explanation, but not the only one, and the difference matters because the fix is different.

  • Oxygen supply. The cornea has no blood vessels to deliver oxygen, so it draws it from the air through the lens. A lens that is too thick, worn too long, or worn overnight reduces the oxygen flux, and the resulting corneal swelling degrades vision and comfort. This is a lens-and-schedule problem, not a cleaning problem.
  • Tear film. A lens divides the tear film into a pre-lens and a post-lens layer. An unstable tear film produces fluctuating vision and end-of-day discomfort that no lens brand will fix, because the surface underneath is the issue.
  • Care-product sensitivity. Preservatives in multipurpose solutions are a recognised cause of contact-lens-associated conjunctival irritation. A hydrogen peroxide system, which neutralises to saline, removes that variable entirely — one of the few genuinely decisive tests available.
  • Fit. A steep lens tightens on the eye and traps debris; a flat lens rocks and chafes. Both can be mistaken for an allergy.
  • Systemic change. Pregnancy, thyroid change, some acne medications and shifts in medication can all alter tear film or lens tolerance. A change in tolerance with no change in lens is a clue.

Claims that mislead

  • "Silicone hydrogel solved intolerance." Higher oxygen transmission removed one cause; it did not remove deposits, papillary response or tear film problems.
  • "Daily disposables cannot cause it." Lower deposit load reduces risk. It does not eliminate mechanical or tear-film causes, and a poorly fitting daily lens still chafes.
  • "If it stings, I am allergic to the lens." Immediate stinging on insertion is usually the solution or a damaged lens, not the material. True material allergy exists but is uncommon.
  • "Just switch brands until one works." Brand-hopping without changing replacement frequency, care system or fit is a random walk. If the cause is the deposit loop, a new material simply starts the loop again.

What actually breaks the loop

The sequence that works is unglamorous: stop wearing the lenses until the eye settles, replace the case, move to a shorter replacement interval or a daily disposable, and if preservative sensitivity is suspected, switch to a peroxide system. Care products are tested to ISO 14729 for antimicrobial performance, which tells you the regimen is capable of meeting a defined microbiological bar — it does not tell you the wearer will tolerate the preservative.

For people who need to restart after a break, a short course of daily disposables with a review at two weeks is a common way to separate a deposit problem from a fit problem. If the papillae are established, the conjunctiva needs time, and restarting early simply restarts the loop.

When to stop managing it yourself

Get the eye examined rather than the lens replaced if you have: itching that persists after lens removal, mucus that returns within days of a new lens, redness that does not settle overnight, pain, light sensitivity, or a drop in vision that remains after the lens is out. Papillae need to be seen under the upper lid with the lid everted — something no amount of at-home trial and error can do. Corneal changes from chronic hypoxia also need a slit lamp, and some of them are silent until they are not.

None of this is a diagnosis, and none of it replaces an examination. It is the mechanism, which is worth knowing because most of the levers — replacement frequency, case hygiene, care system, wearing schedule — are in the wearer's hands.
