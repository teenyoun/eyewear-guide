Straylight: Why Headlights Bloom Even With the Right Prescription

November 10, 2026 · 8 min read

Blur and scatter are different failures

Two people can have identical prescriptions, identical visual acuity on a chart in a bright room, and completely different experiences of a car headlight at night. The difference is usually not refractive. It is straylight.

Refractive error is a geometry problem. Every ray entering the pupil is aimed slightly wrong, and the result is a uniformly blurred image — the whole picture is soft, and a lens fixes it by re-aiming the rays. Straylight is a scattering problem. Most of the light travels correctly and forms a sharp image, but a small fraction is deflected at large angles by inhomogeneities in the optical path. That fraction lands where it should not, spreading a dim veil across the retina. The image is sharp at its core and surrounded by glow.

The physical description is the point spread function — the distribution of light a single point source produces. A perfect eye puts nearly all of it into a tight core a few minutes of arc across. In a real eye there is also a wide, low tail extending degrees away from the core. Straylight is that tail. It is why a bright source degrades the visibility of everything near it, and why the effect scales with the brightness of the source rather than with the size of the object you are trying to see.

The number: log(s)

Straylight is quantified by the straylight parameter, written s, and reported as its base-ten logarithm, log(s). The parameter expresses how much light from a glare source outside the line of sight ends up veiling the retinal image, normalised for the angle between the source and the target. Because it is logarithmic, small-looking differences are not small: 0.3 log units is a factor of two in scattered light.

Population values give the scale. In young, healthy eyes the average is around 0.90 log(s). By the mid-sixties the average has risen to roughly 1.20 log(s) — a doubling of straylight for an eye that may still read the 6/6 line. That single figure explains a great deal of the complaint that vision has become difficult at night while the prescription has barely moved.

Measuring it requires care because straylight cannot be inferred from acuity. Clinical instruments use a compensation comparison method: a test field is split into two halves, one flickering, and the subject reports which side flickers more strongly while a glare ring illuminates the eye. The subject is not asked to judge brightness in absolute terms, only to compare, which removes most of the calibration problem that ruins subjective glare testing.

What actually raises it

The dominant sources are inside the eye, which is the part most people do not expect.

  • Corneal haze, scarring or oedema — any loss of optical homogeneity in the front window.
  • Nuclear sclerotic cataract, which is a gradual increase in protein aggregation in the lens and a textbook cause of disabling glare with preserved acuity.
  • Post-refractive surgery changes in corneal shape and the optical zone transition.
  • Intraocular lens design, particularly multifocal and some diffractive designs, which deliberately split light and therefore produce halo and glare as a by-product of their function.
  • Vitreous changes with age.

Outside the eye, the list is shorter but entirely within the wearer's control: scratches, degraded or crazed coatings, dust, fingerprints, and a smeared tear film. A spectacle lens cannot remove straylight generated behind it, but it can add to it. Surface quality matters most for high-power lenses, where the edge thickness and the steep curves present a larger scattering surface at oblique angles.

Common misconceptions, corrected

Halos mean the prescription is wrong. Sometimes they do, especially if the change is sudden, unilateral, or arrived with a new pair. But a stable prescription with progressive halos is far more often a scatter story. A refraction retest is a reasonable first step because it is cheap and non-invasive; it is not a complete explanation.

Straylight is a lens problem. For most people most of it is in the eye. A perfect spectacle lens restores focus and contributes almost nothing to the veil, which is exactly why a new pair can feel disappointing for night vision even when the acuity is better.

A darker tint will fix the glare. Tint reduces the total light reaching the retina, including the veiling light, so contrast ratio is broadly unchanged. It may improve comfort. It does not reduce the scattering that causes the halo, and heavily tinted lenses reduce the light available for the task, which is its own problem at night.

Anti-reflective coating removes straylight. AR coatings reduce reflections at the lens surfaces, which is why they cut ghost images and improve light transmission. That is a real and measurable benefit. But the veiling glare from an oncoming headlight is dominated by scatter in the eye, and no coating reaches it.

Everyone sees halos at night, so nothing needs checking. A little is normal — a wide pupil admits more aberrated light, and diffraction at the pupil edge is unavoidable. A change in the pattern, halos in one eye only, or halos accompanied by pain, redness or a step down in vision is a reason to be examined rather than reassured.

What to do, in order

Start with the cheap variables. Clean the lenses properly with a lint-free cloth and a suitable cleaner rather than a shirt tail, and inspect them against a light for scratches and coating crazing. If the coating has begun to craze — the fine network of cracks that follows thermal stress — the lens is a permanent scattering surface and replacement is the only fix. Check that the frame is not sitting low, which changes the effective optical centre at night when the pupil dilates and you look through the upper part of the lens.

Then have the eye examined if the problem is new, asymmetric, or worsening. Slit-lamp examination and, where available, a straylight measurement separate the causes properly, and the distinction matters: a corneal problem, a cataract and a lens-surface problem have three different answers, and only one of them is solved at the dispensing bench.

Straylight is the reason two people with the same prescription can disagree about whether their glasses work. It is a measurable property of the whole optical system — cornea, lens, vitreous and the spectacle lens in front of them — and it is the missing variable in most conversations about glare.
