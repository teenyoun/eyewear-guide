Goldmann Tonometry: Why Corneal Thickness Moves the Eye Pressure Reading

November 10, 2026 · 8 min read

The measurement is a force balance

Eye pressure is not measured directly. It is inferred, and the instrument that has inferred it for most of the last seventy years is the Goldmann applanation tonometer, mounted on a slit lamp. Understanding what it assumes explains almost every surprising reading.

The principle is the Imbert-Fick law: for a sphere, the pressure inside equals the force needed to flatten an area of its surface divided by that area. Goldmann's instrument flattens a circular area 3.06 millimetres in diameter, which is 7.35 square millimetres, and is calibrated so that 0.1 gram of applied force reads as 1 millimetre of mercury.

To get from that law to a usable instrument, three assumptions have to hold: the eye is a perfect sphere, its wall is infinitely thin, and its surface is dry and perfectly elastic. A real cornea is none of these. It is about half a millimetre thick, it has real bending stiffness, and it is covered by a film of tears.

Two of those failures are large and nearly opposite. The stiffness of the cornea means extra force is needed to bend it, which inflates the reading. The surface tension of the tear film meniscus around the applanating prism pulls the prism towards the eye, which reduces the force needed and deflates the reading. In an average cornea the two errors very nearly cancel. That cancellation is not physics being kind; it is the calibration. The instrument was adjusted so that the errors offset at a particular corneal thickness and rigidity, and every reading since inherits that choice.

Why thickness shifts the reading

Change the thickness and the balance moves. A thicker cornea needs more force to applanate, so the instrument reports a pressure higher than the one actually inside the eye. A thinner cornea bends more easily, so the reading comes out lower than the truth. The direction is not in dispute; it follows directly from the stiffness term.

The magnitude is where careless advice appears. A rule of thumb circulates that pressure should be adjusted by about five millimetres of mercury for every hundred micrometres of deviation from average thickness. Measured dependence is generally smaller and not linear, and the published correction formulas disagree with each other because they were derived in different populations with different assumptions. No correction table has been validated as a substitute for the measurement itself.

What the reading is good for is comparison and trend. The same eye measured on the same instrument over time is a meaningful series. A single number compared against a table of correction factors is not.

Why a thin cornea is worse than a bad reading

Central corneal thickness matters twice over, and the second reason is the one that changes management. It biases the measurement, and it is also an independent risk factor. In the Ocular Hypertension Treatment Study, thinner central corneas predicted which participants went on to develop glaucoma, over and above their pressure readings. So a thin cornea produces a pressure that reads falsely low while simultaneously marking an eye at higher risk — the two effects compound rather than cancel, which is the opposite of what a patient would intuitively expect.

This is also why a pressure reading is never a diagnosis on its own. Glaucoma is an optic neuropathy with a pressure-related risk factor, not a pressure disease. The nerve appearance, the visual field, the angle and the corneal thickness all contribute, and a single tonometer value in the teens does not exclude anything.

Other variables that move the number

  • Diurnal rhythm. Pressure is typically higher in the early morning; a single afternoon reading is a sample, not a level.
  • Posture. Lying down raises the reading; so does a tight collar or breath-holding during the measurement.
  • Corneal curvature and astigmatism. A significantly toric cornea distorts the applanated circle, which is why the prism is rotated to align with the flattest meridian.
  • Fluorescein quantity. The mires — the two fluorescent semicircles seen through the prism — should overlap by about a tenth of their width. Too much dye thickens the tear meniscus and shifts the endpoint.
  • Instrument contact. Too much pressure on the lid, or a patient holding the eye open awkwardly, changes the reading before the prism touches the cornea.

Common misconceptions, corrected

A reading of 18 means the eyes are fine. A single normal reading is one data point from a variable measurement, in an eye whose risk also depends on thickness, nerve appearance and family history. It is reassuring; it is not a clearance.

The air-puff machine is the accurate one because it is newer. Non-contact tonometry is convenient and needs no anaesthetic, but it is more variable and is generally judged against applanation as the reference. A borderline non-contact reading is usually confirmed by another method rather than acted on directly.

You can calculate the true pressure from a correction formula. Formulas exist and are used in research. None is accurate enough to replace the measurement, and applying one arithmetic correction to one reading creates a false sense of precision.

If the pressure is normal, glaucoma is excluded. A substantial proportion of glaucoma occurs at pressures inside the statistically normal range. That is precisely why the optic nerve is examined and why visual fields are tested.

Pressure is the disease. It is the main modifiable risk factor. The disease is damage to the optic nerve, and its presence is established by structure and function, not by the tonometer alone.

Questions worth asking at the appointment

If you have a family history of glaucoma, a thin cornea, a reading that has changed quickly, or two eyes that differ markedly, ask what the corneal thickness is and whether it was measured, how the reading compares with previous visits rather than with a population range, and what the optic nerve looks like. Those three questions turn a number into information.

Applanation tonometry is a good instrument used inside a well-understood approximation. It is accurate enough to guide care and not accurate enough to be read in isolation — and the corneal thickness sitting between the prism and the pressure is the reason.
