Why Your Prescription Keeps Changing: The Lens Inside Your Eye Is an Optical Element Too

November 14, 2026 · 9 min read

Your glasses correct a system, not an eyeball

The refractive power of an eye is the sum of two lenses and one length. The cornea supplies roughly two thirds of the total, around +43 dioptres of the approximately +60 dioptres in a typical relaxed eye, and the crystalline lens inside supplies most of the rest, near +19 dioptres when unaccommodated. The distance from the corneal surface to the retina, the axial length, is about 24 mm. A useful rule of thumb is that 1 mm of axial length is worth roughly 2.50 to 3.00 dioptres of refractive error, which is why a millimetre is a large quantity in this business and why the growth of a child's eye moves a prescription so quickly.

Because the prescription describes the whole system, it can change whenever any one element changes. Three of them do, routinely, and each produces a recognisably different pattern. Knowing which one is moving is the difference between ordering glasses and investigating a problem.

Reason one: the lens inside your eye changes its own refractive index

The crystalline lens is not a piece of glass with a fixed index of refraction. It is a layered structure of protein-rich fibres, and with age those fibres compact and the nucleus yellows and hardens. That process is nuclear sclerosis, and it does something optically specific: compaction raises the refractive index of the nucleus, which raises the plus power of the lens, which pushes the eye toward myopia. This is the mechanism behind the myopic shift that population studies have documented in people developing nuclear cataract, and behind the old observation of second sight, where a hyperopic patient in their sixties suddenly reads without glasses and is delighted, until the same process begins to cost contrast, colour saturation and glare tolerance.

A drift toward minus is therefore not always a sign that the eye grew longer or that the previous glasses were wrong. In an older adult, a myopic change of half a dioptre or more over a year or two, combined with complaints about oncoming headlights or a general dulling of vision that a new prescription does not fix, is a reason to have the lens examined rather than simply re-ordered. The same logic applies to a hyperopic shift with no other explanation: the lens is a physical object and it changes.

Reason two: blood sugar moves the lens by osmosis

The crystalline lens has no blood supply of its own. It is nourished by the aqueous and it maintains its water content through osmotic balance, which is why it is vulnerable to changes in the chemistry around it. When blood glucose runs high, glucose enters the lens and is converted to sorbitol, which draws water in. The lens swells and steepens, its power increases, and the refraction shifts toward myopia. When glucose falls, the reverse happens and the shift is hyperopic. Because the change is driven by hydration rather than by structure, it is transient and it can move in either direction within days.

This makes newly diagnosed or newly treated diabetes a specific hazard for anyone ordering glasses. A person in that window can have a refraction that is genuinely unstable, and a pair made from one measurement may be wrong a month later. The practical rule in eyecare is to wait until glucose control has been stable for a period measured in weeks before finalising a prescription when the change is suspected to be metabolic, and to refract again rather than to adjust the old pair.

Reason three: the cornea changes shape, and small differences are not always real

Corneal curvature sets most of the power of the eye, so anything that alters it alters the prescription. Orthokeratology is the deliberate version: an overnight lens flattens the central cornea by microns and the effect is designed to be temporary, which is why ortho-k wearers are refracted at a defined time of day. Keratoconus is the pathological version, producing increasing irregular astigmatism that spectacles cannot fully neutralise. Pregnancy, eyelid surgery and chronic eye rubbing can all move the corneal surface as well.

At the small end, tear film quality and the repeatability of the measurement itself matter. Prescriptions are written in steps of 0.25 dioptres, and a single step is at the edge of what two careful refractions of the same eye will agree on. One 0.25 dioptre difference is not evidence of deterioration, and treating it as such leads people to replace glasses that were doing their job.

What people get wrong about a changing prescription

The most common belief is that glasses weaken the eyes. They do not. Axial length, corneal shape and the crystalline lens are not responding to the presence of a lens in front of them. The myopic shift of childhood is growth; the shift of later life is the lens. Wearing glasses accelerates neither and prevents neither. The second belief is that any change means the previous prescription was wrong. A prescription is a measurement of a moving system at one moment, and the useful question is whether the change is large enough to affect comfort or safety, not whether the numbers match. The third is that an adult prescription should be stable forever. It is often stable in axial terms for decades, but presbyopia arrives regardless and lenticular change is normal in later life, so a change after forty is expected rather than suspicious.

There is also a mirror-image error: assuming a big change must be pathological. A one-dioptre myopic shift over twenty years in a sixty-year-old is unremarkable; a one-dioptre shift in six months in one eye is not, and the difference is in the rate and the symmetry, not the number itself.

When a change needs a professional look rather than a new pair

See an eyecare professional promptly if the change appears suddenly, if it affects one eye only, if it comes with flashes, new floaters or a curtain across part of the vision, or if glare and halos are the main complaint rather than blur. Bring the old prescription if you have it, because the size and direction of the change is diagnostic information in its own right. If you have diabetes, say so before the refraction rather than after, because the timing of the test matters as much as the technique.
