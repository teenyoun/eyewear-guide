How Photochromic Lenses Work: Why They Darken in UV and Stay Clear in a Car

October 04, 2026 · 9 min read

Why a photochromic lens is not a brightness sensor

Most people assume photochromic lenses darken because it is bright outside. They do not. They darken because ultraviolet radiation reaches them. The trigger is a specific band of the spectrum, not the visible brightness you perceive, and that single fact explains almost everything people find strange about these lenses.

The mechanism is a reversible chemical change inside the lens. In the organic photochromic lenses that dominate the market, the active ingredients are dye molecules - naphthopyrans and related compounds - dispersed through the lens material. At rest these molecules are transparent to visible light. When ultraviolet photons arrive they supply enough energy to rearrange bonds within the molecule, producing a form that absorbs visible light strongly and therefore looks grey or brown. When the ultraviolet goes away the molecule relaxes back to its original shape and the lens clears.

Two terms matter. Photochromism is the name for a light-induced, reversible colour change. Activation is the darkening step and fading (or bleaching) is the return to clear. Both are chemistry, not electronics, and neither is under your control.

Two technologies, one behaviour

Photochromic lenses come in two chemistries. In mineral glass, tiny silver halide crystals are distributed through the material; ultraviolet drives a reversible silver reaction that darkens the glass - a mechanism related to the chemistry of old photographic film. In plastic lenses, now the norm, the same effect has to be produced with organic dye molecules, because the silver chemistry depends on the glass matrix.

The practical consequences differ. Glass photochromic lenses tend to fade more slowly and to hold their darkness better as temperature rises, because the inorganic reaction is less heat-sensitive. Plastic lenses are lighter, safer and available in higher indices, but rely on the organic chemistry with its temperature dependence. Neither is strictly better; they are differently balanced.

Why they go clear inside a car

This is the most common complaint and it has a precise cause: car glass is formulated to block ultraviolet. Windscreen glass in particular is specified to filter the large majority of UV, with figures around 99% commonly quoted for the laminated glass used in windscreens. No ultraviolet, no activation.

So a photochromic lens worn while driving stays close to clear even in strong sun, and this is not a fault - it is the design working as specified. If you need a genuinely dark lens while driving, you have two options: a lens whose photochromic system also responds to visible light (newer generations do, and cost considerably more), or a fixed tint. Why they never get as dark as the shop suggested

Temperature. The fade reaction competes with the activation reaction, and heat pushes the balance back towards the clear state. A photochromic lens therefore darkens less on a hot day than a cold one, under identical ultraviolet. In summer heat you may get a mid-grey rather than the dark tint the sample showed you under a UV lamp in an air-conditioned shop.

This is also why the same lens can look impressively dark on a cold ski slope and disappointing at the beach. Nothing has changed except the temperature.

Activation and fade are not instantaneous

Both steps take time, and the asymmetry between them is what most annoys wearers. Activation is relatively quick: the lens begins to darken within seconds of ultraviolet exposure and usually reaches most of its density in well under a minute. Fading is slower, because it is a thermal relaxation process - the molecule has to find its way back to the resting shape, and that happens faster when it is warm and slower when it is cold. On a cold day, walking from bright sun into a dim interior, you can be left wearing a lens that stays visibly tinted for several minutes.

Common misconceptions

  • "They darken in any bright light." They respond primarily to ultraviolet. A very bright but UV-poor source - much indoor lighting, a screen, or sunlight through a car windscreen - will barely activate them.
  • "Darker means more UV protection." The dangerous one. UV protection and tint depth are separate properties. A photochromic lens should be blocking UV even in its clear state; the darkness is a comfort feature, not the protection. Read the UV claim, not the tint.
  • "They last forever." Organic photochromic dyes degrade with cumulative UV exposure. Over years both the maximum darkness and the fading speed decline, and the lens may hold a faint residual tint when it should be clear. That is ageing, not a defect.
  • "They replace sunglasses." Behind glass they are not dark at all, and they cannot match a fixed dark tint across a full day of bright sun. They suit people who move constantly between indoors and outdoors.

When to ask a professional

Raise it with a dispensing optician before ordering if you drive long distances in daylight, work outdoors in high heat, or need a reliably dark lens for a specific activity - in each case the chemistry works against you and a fixed tint is usually the better answer. Mention it too if you have a condition that makes you light-sensitive: the depth of darkening a photochromic lens delivers is neither adjustable nor guaranteed, and a medical need is better met with something predictable. If a photochromic lens stops darkening well before you would expect, say so - the dye may simply have reached the end of its useful life.
