The Clock Dial: Where Your Astigmatism Axis Actually Comes From

November 16, 2026 · 8 min read

One question, asked in a strange format

At some point in a refraction the practitioner asks which line on a dial looks sharpest. It sounds like a trick question, and in a sense it is: the dial is not measuring how well you see. It is using the pattern of blur to locate a direction, and the direction it finds becomes the axis printed on your prescription.

The instrument is the astigmatic dial, also called the fan or the clock dial, and it has been in use since the nineteenth century. Understanding how it works explains why the axis can be measured to a degree, why the answer changes when the target is fogged, and why the number on the clock face is not yet your axis.

Two focal lines, not one blur circle

A spherical lens has the same power in every meridian, so it brings light to a single focus. An astigmatic eye does not. Its two principal meridians, ninety degrees apart, have different powers, so they focus at different distances. Optically the result is two focal lines separated by a gap, and the gap is called the interval of Sturm. Between the lines the blur is an ellipse; at one plane it becomes a circle, which is the circle of least confusion.

This is why astigmatic blur has a direction. Along one meridian the retinal image can be nearly in focus while the perpendicular meridian is badly out, so a line target oriented with the good meridian looks crisp and a line perpendicular to it looks soft. The dial simply offers twelve or more orientations at once and asks which one wins.

Why the target is deliberately fogged

The test is run with extra plus power in place, a technique called fogging, enough to push the eye toward myopia. Fogging converts almost any refractive state into compound myopic astigmatism, in which both focal lines sit in front of the retina. In that condition one meridian is always closer to the retina than the other, and therefore always clearer. Without fog, a slightly hyperopic eye can accommodate its way into a confusing mixture where both meridians look acceptable and the answer becomes meaningless.

Once fogged, the patient is asked for the sharpest, blackest or clearest line, described in clock terms because that vocabulary is easier than degrees. The meridian that appears clearest is the one nearest the retina, and that meridian identifies the axis of the correcting minus cylinder. The plus power is then reduced in 0.25 D steps until the lines equalise, which is also how the sphere endpoint is confirmed.

The thirty-degree rule

A clock face has twelve hours for 180 degrees of line orientation, because a line at two o'clock is the same line as one at eight o'clock. Each hour therefore represents 30 degrees, and the conversion is a multiplication:

  • Two o'clock is 2 x 30 = 60 degrees.
  • Six o'clock is 6 x 30 = 180 degrees.
  • Half past the hour is 15 degrees, so two-thirty is 75 degrees.

When two adjacent lines are equally dark, the answer lies between them and has to be expressed as a fraction of an hour: two o'clock and two-thirty equally dark gives 67.5 degrees, which is recorded as 67 or 68. When one line is darkest but one neighbour is darker than the other, the true orientation is interpolated toward that neighbour, which is how an axis finer than the 30-degree spacing gets recovered. Dials printed at 10 or 15 degrees of spacing make the interpolation shorter and the answer more precise, at the cost of a more confusing target.

What the dial does not do

The dial localises the axis. It does not measure the cylinder power by itself: power comes from adding minus cylinder at that axis until the two perpendicular sets of lines look equally clear, and then checking the result. A supplementary step, adding a small plus sphere and asking whether both sets blur together, is used to confirm that the cylinder is neither under- nor over-corrected. If one set stays clearer, astigmatism remains uncorrected.

The axis found this way is a starting value, and in modern practice the Jackson cross cylinder refines it. That ordering matters: the dial gives a rough axis and a rough power quickly, and the cross cylinder squeezes the last few degrees. Attempting to refine a badly wrong axis with a cross cylinder produces an endless loop, which is why the coarse step exists at all.

Three things people get wrong about the fan

The darkest line is my axis number. It is a clock position, and it needs multiplying by 30. Two o'clock is not axis 2.

The dial measures how much astigmatism I have. The orientation of the clearest line is an axis. Magnitude comes from the lens power that neutralises the difference between the two meridians.

If I can read the letters, I do not have astigmatism. Small cylinders blur edges without destroying acuity, which is exactly why the dial exists: it makes a directional defect visible when a letter chart looks almost normal.

Why this is not a home test

Fan images circulate online with instructions to stare at them and read off an axis. What they cannot reproduce is the fogging, the controlled distance, the calibration of the display, or the sequence in which sphere and cylinder are balanced against each other. A screen at 40 cm with an unfogged eye is a different optical situation from a projector at six metres behind a +1.00 fogging lens, and the same person can produce two different answers from the two setups.

Where the dial does earn its place outside a test room is in understanding a number you already have. If a prescription says axis 60, the dial explains why that figure moves in 30-degree steps when a rough estimate is made, why 67 and 68 both appear, and why a five-degree disagreement between two prescriptions is less alarming than it looks. Axis tolerance is not the same as power tolerance, and a small angular error costs far less than the same relative error in cylinder strength.

When an axis deserves a second look

Ask for a recheck rather than ordering online if your axis has moved by a large amount since the last test, if two recent prescriptions disagree by more than a few degrees, if vision is clear in one meridian and smeared in another at the same distance, or if new glasses produce a tilted or leaning world. Those patterns are about the measurement and the fit, not about the frame you chose.
