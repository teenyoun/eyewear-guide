Sturm's Conoid: The Two Focal Lines Inside an Astigmatic Eye

November 19, 2026 · 9 min read

An astigmatic eye never forms a point image

A spherical lens takes a bundle of parallel rays and brings it to a single focus. A toric surface, which is what every astigmatic correction contains, does not. It has two principal meridians about ninety degrees apart, and each meridian has a different curvature and therefore a different power. Rays crossing the steeper meridian are bent more strongly than rays crossing the flatter one, so they come to focus at a different distance. The result is two line foci instead of one point, with a lens-shaped volume of blur between them. That volume is Sturm's conoid, named after the mathematician Jacques Charles Francois Sturm, and it is the most useful mental model for understanding what an astigmatic prescription is actually doing.

The two principal meridians, and what their powers mean

In a prescription written in minus-cylinder form, the sphere gives the power along one meridian and the sphere plus the cylinder gives the power along the meridian at right angles to it. A lens marked -2.00 -1.50 x 180 therefore carries -2.00 D along the 180-degree meridian and -3.50 D along the 90-degree meridian.

The axis number is the part most people misread. The axis is not the direction in which the astigmatism runs; it is the meridian in which the cylinder is doing no work at all. On a -1.50 x 180 lens, the 180-degree meridian receives the sphere power and nothing else. The 90-degree meridian, at right angles to it, receives the full cylinder. Read the axis as the meridian that escapes the cylinder, and the notation stops being mysterious.

What the blur looks like on the retina

Follow one bundle of rays from a distant point. Before either focus the rays form an ellipse. At the first focal line they collapse into a straight line. Beyond it they open into an ellipse again, but rotated ninety degrees, until at the dioptric midpoint the patch becomes circular: the circle of least confusion, the smallest round blur spot the surface can produce. Then the ellipse narrows in the other direction, forms the second focal line, and diverges again.

Every object point is smeared into that same sequence of shapes, and because the two focal lines sit at right angles the smear has a direction. This is why astigmatic blur is described as streaking or ghosting rather than as general softness, and why some patients notice it far more at night, when a wide pupil admits rays from a larger area of the lens.

The interval of Sturm has a length you can calculate

If the two meridians have powers F1 and F2, their focal lengths in air are 1/F1 and 1/F2, and the distance between the two line foci is (F1 - F2) / (F1 x F2). Take a +3.00 D sphere with a +2.00 D cylinder. The meridians are +3.00 D and +5.00 D, so the focal lengths are 333 mm and 200 mm and the interval is 133 mm. Put 3.00 D of cylinder on the same sphere instead and the interval stretches to roughly 375 mm. The interval grows much faster than the cylinder number suggests, which is why 3.00 D of astigmatism is not simply twice as troublesome as 1.50 D.

Inside a real eye the same geometry is compressed into a few millimetres, because the whole eye is only about 24 mm long. The retina almost never lands exactly on either focal line. It usually sits near the circle of least confusion, and that position is precisely what the spherical equivalent describes: the sphere plus half the cylinder. It is a compromise, not a correction, and it is why a spherical-equivalent contact lens leaves an astigmat slightly smeared.

Why astigmatism smears instead of doubling

Patients often arrive saying they see two of everything, and the phrase gets repeated in articles about astigmatism. Strictly, it is the wrong description. True doubling needs two reasonably well-formed images, which is what happens with a double pupil, with a dense cataract that splits light, or with a ghost image from a badly positioned lens edge. Astigmatism produces no sharp image at any plane. One meridian is focused while the other is a blurred line, so the percept is a directional smear rather than two copies of the same object. Someone who genuinely sees two distinct, separately readable images should be examined for something other than a simple refractive error.

Myth: astigmatism means your eye is shaped like a rugby ball

The rugby-ball comparison describes the front of the cornea in the most common form of regular astigmatism, and it misleads in two ways. Astigmatism can come from the cornea, from the crystalline lens inside the eye, or from both, and the two can partly cancel each other. Second, not all astigmatism is regular. In keratoconus, and after corneal injury or surgery, the surface is irregular, meaning there is no single steep meridian and no axis that a cylinder can compensate. Irregular astigmatism is the reason a pair of glasses can look correct on paper and still disappoint in practice.

Myth: an axis of 90 means the astigmatism is vertical

Because 90 degrees is the vertical meridian on a protractor, people assume that axis 90 means the eye is steep vertically. The axis actually marks the meridian that receives only the sphere. On a -1.50 x 90 lens the vertical meridian gets the sphere alone and the horizontal meridian gets the extra -1.50 D. The steep direction is ninety degrees away from the axis number. Getting this backwards changes nothing about how the glasses are made, because the laboratory works from the written axis, but it matters when you are trying to follow what an optometrist is telling you about your own eye.

Where axis accuracy starts to matter

A small axis error leaves a small amount of unwanted cylinder behind. The useful rule of thumb is that the residual cylinder is about 2 x C x sin(theta), where C is the prescribed cylinder and theta the angular error. Five degrees of error on a 2.00 D cylinder leaves roughly 0.35 D of uncorrected cylinder, which most people tolerate. At 4.00 D of cylinder the same five degrees leaves about 0.70 D, which many do not.

When to book an eye examination

Astigmatism that is new in one eye only, that changes quickly, or that comes with distorted or ghosted vision should be assessed rather than corrected by guesswork. Children with significant astigmatism need it addressed early, because a persistently blurred meridian can leave that orientation of vision underdeveloped. Adults who have worn the same cylinder for years without symptoms are usually fine. Adults whose cylinder has shifted by more than a few degrees between tests, or whose vision no longer clears with an updated prescription, need an examination that looks at the cornea and not only at the numbers on the printout.

Sturm's conoid is not an abstraction. It is the shape of the blur you are wearing every day the cylinder is wrong, and the prescription is simply the instruction that collapses it back into a point.
