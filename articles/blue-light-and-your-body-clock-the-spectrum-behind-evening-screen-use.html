Blue Light and Your Body Clock: The Spectrum Behind Evening Screen Use

October 11, 2026 · 9 min read

"Blue light" is a band, not a product

Light is a continuous spectrum, and the phrase blue light simply names its short-wavelength visible portion — broadly the region from about 380 to 500 nanometres, sitting between ultraviolet, which we cannot see, and the greens. The sun emits it in abundance. So does every screen, every LED lamp, and every fluorescent tube. The interesting questions are therefore not "is there blue light" but "how much, and when".

Why the body responds to one particular wavelength

Classical vision science describes two photoreceptor systems: the rods, which handle dim light, and the cones, which handle colour and fine detail. Both are built for seeing. A third class of cell in the retina, the intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells, is built for something else entirely. It contains the pigment melanopsin, and its job is to report ambient light level to the body's master clock in the hypothalamus.

That system has its own sensitivity curve, peaking in the blue-cyan region at roughly 480 nanometres. It is important to see how unlike ordinary brightness this is: the eye's conventional luminous efficiency peaks much further along the spectrum, in the green around 555 nanometres. Light that looks dim to you and light that reads as "daytime" to your body clock are therefore not the same measurement, and a source can be strong on one scale and weak on the other.

What the melatonin signal does

When that pathway reports bright short-wavelength light, the clock suppresses melatonin release and pushes the timing of sleep later. Light in the evening, arriving at the wrong point in the cycle, tends to delay the clock; morning light advances it. This is well established as a mechanism. What is much harder to establish is how much of a typical evening's screen use actually matters, because the effect depends on intensity, duration and timing together.

The dose problem

Here the comparison is stark. Even a bright phone or monitor at normal indoor settings delivers light on the order of a small number of tens to low hundreds of lux at the eye. Ordinary overcast daylight is measured in thousands of lux, and direct sun in tens of thousands. A screen is therefore a weak light source next to a window, which is why the strongest defensible statement about evening screens is about timing and displacement — they keep you up and push the light later — rather than about a large photic dose delivered to the clock.

That framing matters, because it predicts what helps. Reducing screen brightness, moving the source further from the eye, and finishing earlier in the evening all act on the same variables. So does getting bright outdoor light in the morning, which strengthens the contrast between day and night rather than merely weakening the evening.

What a blue-light filter changes spectrally

Blue-light eyewear comes in two broad families, and they behave differently. One uses absorbing materials or dyes, which simply take a bite out of the short-wavelength end and often leave a perceptible warm or yellow cast. The other uses a reflective coating that selectively bounces part of the blue band away from the eye — the same thin-film approach used in anti-reflective coatings, aimed at a narrower band, and recognisable by the violet or blue tint it reflects back at an observer.

Either way, the filter reduces a proportion of light in a band; it does not remove a category. There is no equivalent of the five-category scale that sun filters use under EN ISO 12312-1, which is why "blue light blocking" figures are hard to compare between products — they are rarely measured over the same band with the same weighting.

Common misconceptions

  • "Blue light comes from screens." Screens are a minor source compared with daylight. What is distinctive about screens is proximity, duration and the hour at which they are used.
  • "Blue light damages the retina." This is the claim most often stated as settled fact. It is not: the exposure levels involved in ordinary screen use are far below those used in laboratory studies of retinal damage, and the eye's own lens filters a large share of short-wavelength light before it reaches the retina.
  • "A brighter screen means a stronger clock signal than daylight." Measured in lux at the eye, daylight exceeds a screen by orders of magnitude even on a dull day.
  • "A filter that blocks more blue is better for sleep." More absorption also means more colour distortion and a greater reduction in the light you need in the morning, which is the signal that anchors the cycle.
  • "Blue-light glasses are pointless." The honest position is narrower than either side of the argument. The mechanism is real; the typical evening screen dose is small; and any benefit is likely to be modest compared with timing and morning light.

When to seek a professional assessment

Persistent difficulty falling asleep, daytime sleepiness, or a sleep pattern that has shifted markedly is a sleep problem, and a lens filter is not the right first tool for it. Likewise, eye strain, dryness or headaches during screen work are more often related to viewing distance, blink rate and the accuracy of a prescription than to the colour of the light. If your last eye examination is out of date, that is the more useful appointment — and it will also tell you whether a tinted or filtered lens is doing anything for you at all.
