How Often Should You Get an Eye Exam? Age-Based Schedule 2026

August 30, 2026 · 6 min read

Most people only see an eye doctor when their vision gets blurry. That's a mistake — comprehensive eye exams catch conditions that have no early symptoms: glaucoma, macular degeneration, diabetic retinopathy, and even signs of cardiovascular disease. Here's the evidence-based schedule for every age.

The Official Schedule

AgeFrequencyWhy
6-12 monthsOnce (infant screening)Catch congenital issues early
3-5 yearsOnce before schoolAmblyopia (lazy eye) is treatable now, not later
6-18 yearsEvery 1-2 yearsSchool vision screening misses many issues
19-40 yearsEvery 2 years (annually if using contacts)Adult-onset myopia, contact lens health
40-60 yearsEvery 1-2 yearsPresbyopia onset; early glaucoma detection
60+ yearsAnnuallyCataracts, AMD, glaucoma risk rises sharply

Why Kids Need Exams (Even With 'Perfect' Vision)

School screenings catch maybe half of vision problems in children. Conditions like amblyopia (lazy eye) and strabismus (eye misalignment) are 100% treatable in early childhood but become permanent after ages 7-9. A child who can't see the board isn't 'distracted' — they literally can't see it. One exam before starting school is the minimum.

What a Comprehensive Exam Includes

  1. Refraction test: 'Which is better, 1 or 2?' — determines your prescription
  2. Cover test: Checks eye alignment and binocular vision
  3. Slit-lamp exam: Microscope check of cornea, iris, lens for disease
  4. Dilated fundus exam: Eye drops widen pupils to inspect the retina — catches glaucoma, AMD, diabetic damage
  5. Intraocular pressure: Air puff or contact test for glaucoma risk
  6. Visual field test: Peripheral vision screening (often from 40+)

Signs You Shouldn't Wait For the Schedule

  • Sudden vision change, flashes of light, or floaters that multiply — emergency
  • Eye pain, redness, or discharge lasting more than a day
  • Double vision at any age
  • Diabetes or family history of glaucoma/AMD — start annual exams early
  • Any vision change during pregnancy (hormones affect corneas)

Where to Get an Exam

Cost: $50-150 without insurance. Options:

  • Optometrist (OD): standard exams, prescriptions, contact lens fitting
  • Ophthalmologist (MD): medical eye care, surgery, disease management
  • Retail clinics (Costco, Walmart Vision): cheapest, quality varies
  • Online vision tests: prescription renewal only — never a substitute for comprehensive exams
