Best Glasses for Heart-Shaped Faces — Balance a Wide Forehead

August 20, 2026 · 5 min read

A heart-shaped face is widest across the forehead and temple line, holds that width through high cheekbones, then converges to a narrow chin. Everything that flatters it follows from one question: where does the frame put its visual mass? High mass exaggerates the top; low mass restores the balance.

Where a frame puts its weight

A frame is read as three things: a top rim, a bottom rim, and an outline. On a face that is already widest at the top, the top rim lands on the widest band while the bottom rim lands where the face has narrowed. A rimless mount deletes the top rim entirely; a browline doubles it. That structural difference, not the label on the frame, explains most of the advice below.

So the measurement that matters is not the frame's category but the horizontal position of its widest point. Set that span at or below the pupil line and the added width sits where the face is narrow. Set it above — a heavy browline, an upswept cat-eye — and the added width goes to the forehead. Before shopping, compare forehead width against jaw width; the wider the gap, the lower the frame's maximum width should sit.

What rimless actually changes

A three-piece mount — two lenses drilled and screwed to a bridge and two temples, with no rim — leaves the lens edge as the outline. Optically nothing changes; visually the added horizontal disappears, which is why rimless suits this face so well. The cost is exposure. A minus lens is thinnest at its optical centre and thickest at the edge, and edge thickness grows with both power and lens diameter, so a strong prescription in a large rimless lens shows a visible band of glass at the temple. Keep the A and B measurements small, or specify a rolled and polished edge.

Shapes that work

Rimless and semi-rimless

Semi-rimless with a nylon cord along the bottom keeps the top edge light while adding a line low, which is the useful direction.

Round and oval

Curve softens a pointed chin. Keep the rim thin so the lens carries the shape rather than the frame.

Bottom-weighted: teardrop and aviator

Both put their widest span below the pupil line, which is the correction this face needs.

Cat-eye, chosen carefully

The classic upswept corner adds width where the face already has it. A low-sweep version keeps the lift without the added top mass.

What to avoid

  • Heavy browline / Clubmaster: adds a thick horizontal at the brow, the one band that needs nothing added.
  • Oversized square or Wayfarer: broad and top-heavy, it repeats the forehead and squares off the chin.
  • Decorative temples: pattern or hardware on the arms pulls the eye outward along the forehead line.
  • Very deep lenses: the bottom rim drops onto the cheek and visually shortens the lower face.

Get the sizing numbers right

The size code inside the temple follows ISO 8624: 52 □ 18 − 145 means A = 52 mm lens width, DBL = 18 mm between lenses, 145 mm temple length. Frame PD, the centre-to-centre distance between lenses, is A + DBL — 70 mm here — and should sit close to your own pupillary distance; the difference is decentration you wear for hours. Total front width, roughly 2A + DBL plus end pieces, should match your temple width rather than exceed it.

To measure that width, hold a bank card beside your face in the plane of your eyes and photograph yourself straight on: the card's long edge is 85.6 mm per ISO/IEC 7810 ID-1, which gives the photo a scale.

Myths worth clearing up

  • "Heart faces should never wear cat-eye." The sweep angle matters more than the category. A low-sweep cat-eye with a strong lower rim often works; the high-sweep version is what misfires.
  • "Rimless is always the safe choice." It removes the added outline but exposes lens edge thickness, conspicuous in a high minus prescription.
  • "A big frame balances a big forehead." Width above the pupil line adds to the forehead. Balance comes from mass placed low, not from overall size.
  • "A wide forehead needs an extra-wide frame." Front width should match temple width. Exceeding it adds width at the temples and the frame slides.

Bottom line

Rimless or semi-rimless, round or oval, or a teardrop whose widest span sits below the pupil line — then check the numbers before the shape: frame PD close to your pupillary distance, front width equal to temple width, and a thin lower rim adding the line where you need it.
