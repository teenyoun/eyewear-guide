ISO 8980: What the Spectacle Lens Standards Actually Specify

October 15, 2026 · 8 min read

﻿

A finished lens is allowed to be slightly wrong

Ask for a lens of a given power and the laboratory will aim at that power. It will not hit it exactly, and it is not required to. Surfacing, polishing and the measurement of the finished surface all carry uncertainty, and the material itself moves the answer: optical power shifts with temperature, and a lens measured on a warm bench from a cold delivery is a different lens from one measured calmly at reference conditions.

The ISO 8980 series exists to make that gap explicit. It does not promise that a lens is exactly what the prescription says. It defines how far a finished lens may deviate, and how each property is to be verified, so that a lens which leaves specification is a documentable fact rather than an argument.

ISO 8980 is several documents, not one

Treating ISO 8980 as a single standard is the first thing to get straight, because each part answers a different question.

  • Part 1 — Uncut finished spectacle lenses: specifications for single-vision and multifocal lenses. The current edition is dated 2017.
  • Part 2 — the same specification for power-variation lenses, the category that includes progressives, where power changes gradually across the lens rather than sitting in distinct zones.
  • Part 3 — Transmittance specifications and test methods. This is the part about how much light of each wavelength the lens passes or blocks, including attenuation of solar radiation.
  • Part 6 — a part concerned with validating specific claims made for lens properties, which is the mechanism by which a manufacturer can back a particular performance statement rather than a general one.

The parts are referenced together in practice but they are not interchangeable, and a claim that a lens "meets ISO 8980" tells you almost nothing until you know which part is meant.

What Part 1 actually specifies

The optical requirements are a list, and reading it is the quickest way to see what a lens can be checked for. Part 1 addresses the back vertex power — the power measured from the surface nearest the eye, which is the quantity your prescription describes. It addresses the direction of the cylinder axis, the addition power of multifocal lenses, and the prismatic power along with the orientation of the prism base.

Alongside the optical requirements sit geometrical ones: dimensions and thickness, and, for multifocal lenses, the dimensions of the segment. The standard also deals with the orientation of polarising lenses, which matters because a polarising filter only performs as designed if its transmission axis is set to the intended direction relative to the frame.

Two further clauses are easy to overlook and worth knowing about. There is a reference temperature, because power is temperature-dependent, and there are marking requirements — certain single-vision lenses and polarising lenses are expected to carry identification so that their intended position or orientation can be recovered later. A lens that has lost that marking has lost part of what makes it verifiable.

Why a tolerance is not a concession

It is tempting to read a permitted deviation as a licence to be sloppy. The opposite is closer to the truth. A tolerance is what makes a specification enforceable: without a stated limit, every dispute about a lens becomes a matter of opinion, and there is no way to distinguish a lens that is slightly off from one that is wrong.

Tolerances also have to be physically achievable. Thickness interacts with power, index and frame size; a demand for exact power in every case would push manufacturers toward thicker, heavier lenses and, in the extremes, toward designs that simply cannot be cut into the frame the customer chose. The standard's job is to hold the useful properties — power, axis, addition, prism direction — within limits that preserve function, while accepting that some measured drift is normal.

Common misconceptions

  • "A lens either matches my prescription or it does not." Finished lenses are permitted to deviate within defined limits. A small measured difference is not automatically an error, and a large one is what the limits are for.
  • "ISO 8980 covers everything about a lens." It does not cover sunglasses as a solar-protection product, which is a different standard, and it does not cover impact protection, which is a different family again. Mixing them up is common in marketing copy.
  • "ISO 8980 means the coating is durable." The transmittance part is about light, not about abrasion, adhesion or how a coating survives cleaning.
  • "Within tolerance means I will not notice." A tolerance is a manufacturing boundary, not a psychological one. Whether a wearer notices a given deviation depends on their sensitivity and on how the two eyes compare.

Why any of this is useful to a wearer

Two practical consequences follow. The first is that a power check on a new pair is a reasonable request rather than an accusation: there is a defined set of properties to measure, in a defined way, at a defined temperature, and knowing that is what turns "these feel wrong" into something a laboratory can respond to.

The second is that it clarifies where to look when something is off. A lens can be within specification on power and axis and still be a poor outcome, because the source of the problem is elsewhere: the optical centre sits too far from the pupil, the frame has been adjusted so the vertex distance or pantoscopic tilt differs from the assumption the lens was designed around, or the two lenses differ from each other more than they differ from the prescription.

When to take it further

If a new pair produces strain, distortion or a sense that one eye is doing more work than the other, ask for the prescription and the lens specification in writing, and ask which standard the lenses were supplied against. Bring the frame, not a description of it. An optometrist or dispensing optician can verify the lens properties and check the fitting measurements at the same time, which is where most answers actually live.
