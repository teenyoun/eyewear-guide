EN 14139: What a Pair of Ready-Made Readers Is Actually Required to Meet

November 15, 2026 · 9 min read

Two products that look identical on the shelf

A pair of +2.00 readers from a pharmacy and a pair of +2.00 lenses from a prescription laboratory can look the same and are not the same product. One is a complete ready-to-wear spectacle, sold without a prescription and intended for occasional use. The other is a medical device made to an individual measurement. The difference is written down, and the document is EN 14139.

EN 14139:2010, Ophthalmic optics, Specifications for ready-to-wear spectacles, was approved by CEN on 13 May 2010, supersedes the 2002 edition, and carries the classification ICS 11.040.70. Its scope is narrow and worth stating plainly rather than paraphrasing: it specifies the minimum requirements for complete ready-to-wear spectacles, and it records that these are not intended for regular use without the approval of an eye-care professional. That sentence is the whole argument of this article. The standard is a floor for manufacture, not a substitute for an examination.

What a product standard can promise, and what it cannot

The logic of any product standard is substitution: instead of testing every pair against every wearer, the standard fixes properties of the object so that a buyer can rely on a description. Conformity means the spectacles match the written specification. It cannot mean the spectacles suit your eyes, because the standard has no knowledge of your eyes.

Three terms carry most of the weight here. The back vertex power is the power measured at the back surface of the lens, which is the surface that faces the eye and the value that matters optically for a finished pair. The optical centre is the point on a lens through which a ray passes undeviated, and it is the point where prism is zero. Ready-to-wear spectacles are complete, finished spectacles offered in fixed powers rather than made to a prescription, which is the category the standard governs.

The arithmetic that decides whether a reader works

Readers are sold in 0.25 dioptre steps with both lenses carrying the same power. A dioptre is the reciprocal of focal length in metres, so the marking on the reader is also a statement about distance. A +1.50 lens has a focal length of 0.67 m and is built for a working distance around 67 cm. A +2.00 is built for 50 cm. A +2.50 is built for 40 cm. A +3.00 is built for 33 cm.

That mapping is the entire basis of choosing a strength, and it is also the reason two people with the same prescription can need different readers. The power is not a measure of how bad the eyes are; it is a statement about where the page is expected to sit. Someone who reads at 60 cm and buys a +2.50 will find the page sharp only if their own focusing can relax by the difference, and a presbyopic eye is by definition the eye that cannot relax much further.

At 40 cm the demand on the eye is 2.50 dioptres. That figure is fixed by geometry, not by preference, which is why the standardisation of reading distance matters more than the standardisation of power. A reader chosen for the wrong distance produces the same complaint as the wrong power: blur, or a strain that builds over twenty minutes rather than appearing at once.

What a ready-made pair structurally cannot do

  • Astigmatism. A reader is spherical, with one power in every meridian. A cylinder, which is what astigmatism needs, has different powers on two meridians ninety degrees apart. No spherical lens can express that difference.
  • Unequal eyes. Both lenses carry the same power, so an anisometropic wearer, whose two eyes need different corrections, gets one eye right at most.
  • Centration. The optical centres sit where the frame puts them, not where your pupils are. Any offset between the two induces prism by Prentice's rule, roughly one prism dioptre for every 10 mm of decentration at 1.00 dioptre of power.
  • Working distance. Fixed by the power, as above, with no allowance for a screen, a violin, or a workbench.
  • Fit. Vertex distance and pantoscopic tilt are set by the frame on your face, and both change the effective power you actually experience.

Four claims worth correcting

The CE marking means these glasses suit my eyes. The marking indicates conformity with a product specification. Suitability depends on measurements the standard never sees.

Readers weaken your eyes. Presbyopia progresses whether or not a reader is worn, because it is driven by the changing lens inside the eye. A correct reader does not train the focusing system out of anything and does not accelerate the decline. What readers can do is mask a problem that needed attention, which is a different failure.

A reader in my prescription power is the same as my prescription. Same sphere, and that is where the similarity ends. Astigmatism, anisometropia, centration, prism and working distance are all absent from the reader.

Cheap readers are all the same. Standards exist precisely because they are not: optical power tolerance, surface quality, mechanical robustness and material safety are all properties a specification can constrain and a market can ignore.

Where a reader is the right answer

A reader is a reasonable tool for a straightforward presbyope: two eyes with similar needs, little or no astigmatism, a conventional reading distance, and no symptoms beyond wanting to read a menu. Used that way, a conforming pair is a legitimate, cheap and honest solution, and the standard is what makes the cheapness tolerable.

Stop and get measured instead when one eye is consistently blurrier than the other, when letters look ghosted or doubled rather than simply soft, when a headache arrives after twenty minutes of reading, when the screen and the book sit at different distances and both matter, or when the strength that clears the page no longer clears it after a few months. Those are the patterns that a stronger reader cannot fix and that a measurement can.

EN 14139 is the reason a cheap pair is at least a known quantity. It is not a reason to skip the part where somebody looks at your eyes.
