Why Lenses Fog and How Anti-Fog Coatings Actually Work

October 05, 2026 · 8 min read

Fog is condensation, and the droplets - not the water - are the problem

When you come in from the cold, or breathe out behind a face covering, the air next to your face is warm and carries a lot of water vapour. The lens surface is cooler. Air can only hold a certain amount of water vapour at a given temperature, and that limit falls as the temperature falls. When the lens drops below the dew point of the air around it, the excess vapour has to go somewhere, and it condenses onto the surface.

What you then see is not a layer of water in the ordinary sense. It is thousands of microscopic droplets, each one a tiny lens that scatters incoming light in every direction. That scattering is the white veil. The crucial insight for everything that follows is that water is not the problem. A continuous, uniform film of water is almost perfectly transparent. Discrete droplets are not.

Two ways to defeat fogging, and only one of them suits a spectacle lens

Surface behaviour with water is described by the contact angle - the angle a droplet makes with the surface where they meet. It is the standard measurable quantity in this field: a high contact angle means the droplet sits up and beads; a low one means the water spreads out flat. Every anti-fog strategy is really a decision about which way you want the water to behave.

  • Hydrophilic - water spreading. A water-attracting surface lowers the contact angle so that condensed water merges into a continuous sheet instead of standing in droplets. Because a sheet does not scatter light the way droplets do, the lens stays clear. This is the principle behind most anti-fog treatments and coatings for spectacle lenses, and it is the approach that directly answers the physics of the problem.
  • Hydrophobic to superhydrophobic - water shedding. A strongly water-repelling surface makes droplets bead and run off, which is excellent for rain on a windscreen or a jacket. For fog on a lens the mechanism is different and less direct: the aim is to make droplets coalesce and shed before they build into a veil. It is a real research area, but it is not the mechanism used by ordinary anti-fog spectacle coatings.

The distinction matters when you buy treatments, because a product that makes your lenses shed water beautifully is not necessarily one that stops them fogging, and the two are marketed with very similar words.

Why your anti-reflective lenses fog so readily

Anti-reflective coating stacks are normally finished with a hydrophobic and oleophobic top layer. That layer earns its place: it repels oil from fingerprints and water from rain, and it makes the lens easier to clean. But it is water-repellent by design, which means condensed water sits on it as droplets rather than spreading. Add the two other factors always present in spectacle wear - the lens is very close to a warm, moist source of air, and it is thin enough to cool quickly - and a treated lens can fog eagerly.

This is why anti-fog performance is often better on a plain lens than on a fully coated one, and why aftermarket anti-fog treatments work by overriding the hydrophobic top layer rather than cooperating with it.

How anti-fog performance is actually judged

Fogging resistance is not a matter of opinion in occupational eyewear. There is a defined test method, EN 168, which sets out how the fogging behaviour of personal eye protection is assessed, and EN 166, which states the performance requirements such products must meet. When a product is sold as fog-resistant for safety use, that is the framework it is being measured against. Consumer sunglasses and fashion eyewear are not generally held to it, which is one reason the same claim means very different things in different shops.

Common misconceptions

  • "Anti-fog coating lasts as long as the lens." Generally it does not. Built-in coatings are more durable than sprays and cloths, but liquid and wipe-on treatments must be reapplied, and every cleaning cycle removes some of the layer.
  • "Rubbing the lens clear solves it." It smears the water around and often makes the surface temporarily worse, because the moisture has not gone anywhere. The lens is usually clearest once the water has been allowed to spread or evaporate.
  • "If it fogs, it must be dirty." Not necessarily. Fogging is driven by temperature and humidity, and a spotless lens fogs perfectly well in a cold doorway.
  • "Anti-fog and anti-reflective are the same kind of coating." Different jobs: anti-reflective addresses reflection using thin-film interference, anti-fog addresses condensation using surface energy. One does not imply the other.
  • "Hot water is the safest way to clean and de-fog." Hot water is hard on coating stacks. Lukewarm water and a clean cloth are kinder to a lens you want to keep.

When to ask a professional

Raise it with an optician if fogging is constant, if it affects one eye far more than the other, or if it comes with pain, redness, discharge or a persistently gritty feeling. Fogging is usually a physics problem, but a tear film that is not working properly can make the surface of the eye itself behave badly, and that is for an eye care professional rather than a better cloth. If you wear a face covering or work in a setting where eye protection is required, mention it: the fitting of the covering and the specification of the eyewear both affect the outcome, and occupational eyewear is expected to be tested against the fogging standard rather than marketed with a slogan. And if you keep buying anti-fog treatments without success, describe exactly when the fogging happens - the fix tends to be specific to the situation, not universal.
