Hard Water, Salt and Sunscreen: What Actually Attacks an AR Coating

November 20, 2026 · 8 min read

A coating measured in micrometres, attacked by things in your bathroom

An anti-reflective coating is a stack of metal oxide layers a few micrometres thick, finished with a top layer measured in nanometres. It is durable enough to survive years of daily wear and chemically narrow enough to fail on things you use every day. Most coating damage that gets blamed on manufacturing is chemistry: minerals from tap water, salt from sweat and sea spray, solvents from cleaning products, and the residues left on fingers.

What the stack is made of

A typical stack alternates high-index and low-index oxides, commonly titanium dioxide or zirconium dioxide for the high-index layers and silicon dioxide for the low-index ones, built up in four to seven layers so that reflections from successive boundaries cancel each other by interference. The stack sits on a hard coat, a polysiloxane lacquer a few micrometres thick that provides abrasion resistance, and is topped by a hydrophobic and oleophobic silane a few nanometres thick that makes water bead and fingerprints lift easily. ISO 8980-4 is the standard that specifies requirements and test methods for anti-reflective coatings on spectacle lenses, which is a useful reminder that coating performance is a specified property rather than a marketing claim.

Attack one: hard water

Tap water carries dissolved calcium and magnesium bicarbonate. When a droplet dries on the lens, those salts come out of solution as carbonate and stay on the surface, and because the deposit is alkaline it bonds readily to the silica-rich top of the stack. A single dried droplet usually wipes off. Repeat it daily for a year and the deposit begins to sit in the microscopic pores of the coating, where wiping drags an abrasive mineral across a comparatively soft surface. Water is commonly described as hard above roughly 120 mg per litre of calcium carbonate, and much of the world's tap water exceeds that.

Attack two: salt

Sodium chloride behaves differently from hardness salts because it crystallises. Sweat and sea spray leave salt at the rim of the lens and along the bevel, where it grows into crystals that can lift the coating at the edge. This is one of the common routes to the peeling that starts as a thin bright line around the perimeter and creeps inward. Rinsing with fresh water after exposure, and drying with a clean cloth instead of letting the lens air-dry, removes salt before it can crystallise.

Attack three: solvents and the wrong soap

The hydrophobic top layer is a thin silane, and silanes are attacked by repeated solvent exposure. Occasional isopropyl alcohol, as found in most lens cleaners, is tolerable. Daily use of strong alcohol, acetone, ammonia-based glass cleaners or degreasers is not. High-pH detergents are a separate problem: silica dissolves in strongly alkaline solution, so a soap at pH 11 causes slow cumulative damage to the oxide layers even if the surface looks untouched. Neutral-pH soap, one drop, with water, remains the safest routine.

Attack four: sunscreen and insect repellent

Sunscreen contains UV filters and solvents designed to spread and penetrate, and insect repellents are built on solvents such as DEET. Both reach the lens from fingers and from the brow, and both attack the lacquer and the silane. Sunscreen on the hands, then a thumb and forefinger on the lens to take the glasses off, is a routine summer event and a routine cause of hazy patches that will not clean off. Wash your hands after applying sunscreen, and rinse the lenses if you know you have touched them.

Attack five: heat, which is a different mechanism

Thermal cycling deserves separating from chemistry. A car dashboard in summer reaches temperatures well above the glass transition of the lacquer, and the mismatch in expansion between hard coat and lens produces the fine network of cracks known as crazing. No cleaning routine prevents that; only keeping the glasses out of a hot car does.

Myth: vinegar dissolves hard-water spots on lenses

Vinegar does dissolve calcium carbonate, which is why it works on a kettle and on bathroom tiles. On a coated lens the acid also attacks the oxide layers and the lacquer, and the etched patch it can leave is permanent. A lens with mineral spotting is better handled by a professional ultrasonic clean than by an acid kept in the kitchen.

Myth: mild dish soap is always safe

Many dish liquids are neutral, and a dilute drop is the standard recommendation. Many others are alkaline, contain dyes and fragrances, or are built to strip grease aggressively. If the label boasts about cutting grease or lists a high pH, it is not the right product for a coated lens. Plain neutral soap, or a purpose-made lens cleaner, is the safe default.

The routine that actually protects the stack

Rinse the lens under lukewarm water before touching it, so grit leaves with the water rather than being ground in. Apply a drop of neutral soap and clean with fingertips, not with a cloth, then rinse and dry with a clean microfibre. Never dry-wipe a dusty lens, never use hot water, never use paper towels or clothing, and never leave the glasses on a dashboard. Store them in a case rather than a pocket, because a case exists to keep the surfaces away from everything else in your bag.

When to take the glasses back

Ask your optician to look at the lens if the coating is peeling at the edge, if cloudy patches survive a proper wash, if the surface looks crazed in raking light, or if the anti-reflective effect has visibly weakened in one area. Coating defects inside the warranty period are usually a manufacturing claim, and a photograph taken in oblique light against a dark background shows the pattern far better than a verbal description.
