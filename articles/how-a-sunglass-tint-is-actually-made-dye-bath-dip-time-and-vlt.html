How a Sunglass Tint Is Actually Made: Dye Bath, Dip Time and VLT

November 18, 2026 · 8 min read

Two completely different processes produce the same-looking lens

Hold two grey sunglasses side by side and they appear to be made the same way. In fact the colour in each arrived by one of two routes that share almost nothing: it was either mixed into the plastic before the lens was moulded, or diffused into the finished surface from a heated bath.

Mass tinting compounds pigment into the resin itself. The colour is present through the full thickness, it survives polishing and re-edging, and it is extremely repeatable — which is why it suits high-volume moulded lenses. Its limitation is that it cannot be varied per order: the colour is decided before the lens exists, so a laboratory cannot take a clear lens off the shelf and make it category 3 that afternoon.

Surface dyeing can. It is the process behind almost every prescription sunglass lens and every custom tint, and it works by getting dye molecules to diffuse a short distance into the polymer.

What happens inside a dye bath

A dye bath is an aqueous dispersion of disperse dyes, held hot, with surfactants to help the liquor wet the surface evenly. The lens is mounted in a fixture and lowered in. Over minutes, dye diffuses into the outer layer of the material, typically to a depth measured in tens of microns — deep enough to be permanent against normal cleaning, shallow enough that the process does not disturb the optical surface.

Three variables set the result. Time in the bath sets the depth and therefore the density. Temperature sets how fast the dye moves; different substrates tolerate different temperatures, and polycarbonate in particular has to be dyed below the point at which it would begin to distort or release moulded-in stress. Concentration sets the ceiling. All three interact, which is why a laboratory's recipe book is a table of times and temperatures rather than a single number.

Substrate matters as much as recipe. The same bath, the same dwell time, will not produce the same density in polycarbonate, in CR-39 and in a nylon lens, because each polymer absorbs dye at a different rate. A lab that dyes one material routinely has to re-qualify its times when it starts working with another.

Gradients are a withdrawal, not a print

A gradient tint is made by moving the lens through the bath rather than dropping it in. The lens is lowered slowly and partially, or withdrawn at a controlled rate, so the top of the lens spends less time in the dye than the bottom. The transition is a genuine diffusion gradient inside the material — there is no printed film and no layer to lift.

That is also why two gradients from different suppliers can differ in character even at the same density. The steepness of the fade is set by the withdrawal speed and the dye's diffusion behaviour, not by a choice of "soft" or "hard" as a menu option.

The mirror is a coating; the tint is not

This is the distinction most often lost in marketing copy. A mirror finish is a thin vacuum-deposited layer of metal or metal oxide on the front surface. It reflects a proportion of the light before it ever enters the lens, which is why a mirrored lens looks darker from outside than it behaves from inside. Being a deposited layer, it can craze, scratch and — in bad cases — delaminate at the edge.

A tint, by contrast, is in the polymer. It does not peel, because there is nothing to peel.

What the process is aiming at

The target is not "dark". It is a luminous transmittance that lands inside a defined category. Under ISO 12312-1 the filter categories are bands of visible light transmission: category 0 at 80 to 100 percent, category 1 at 43 to 80, category 2 at 18 to 43, category 3 at 8 to 18, and category 4 at 3 to 8. A laboratory dyes to a measured transmission value and then verifies it, because the band boundaries are what the label and the driving rules refer to.

Notice that none of those numbers describes ultraviolet protection. In most sunglass lenses the UV absorber is a separate chemistry compounded into the material, doing its work in a wavelength range the eye cannot see and the dye does not touch. A lens can be dyed to category 3 and still transmit ultraviolet, or be almost colourless and block it completely.

Common misconceptions

"The tint is a coating that wears off." Surface dye sits inside the polymer, not on it. What does fade is dye attacked by solvents it was never formulated for, and what delaminates is the mirror layer or the anti-reflective stack on the back surface.

"Darker means more UV protection." Darkness is visible light transmission. UV blocking is a separate absorber, and the two are specified separately. A very dark lens with no UV absorber is worse than a clear lens with one, because the pupil opens behind it.

"Grey is grey." Two lenses can match under one light source and differ under another, because "grey" is a blend of dyes chosen to flatten the spectrum, and different blends flatten it differently. A neutral-looking lens can still shift colours slightly at the edges of the visible range.

"Any lens can be re-dyed." Coated lenses generally cannot: a hard coat or an anti-reflective stack blocks the dye from reaching the polymer, and stripping the coating to dye underneath is not a service anyone offers economically. Polycarbonate also constrains the process temperature, so a recipe developed for CR-39 does not transfer unchanged.

When a tint is a reason to ask questions

Have a pair checked if the colour has visibly lightened in a patch rather than evenly, if the mirror shows a milky edge, or if you can see a boundary line where a gradient should be smooth — those indicate process faults rather than wear. Ask for the category before buying a lens for driving, since category 4 filters are not legal for driving in most of Europe and are intended for high-altitude and water sports.

And if a tinted lens makes the world look flat, or makes reds and greens harder to separate than they used to be, that is worth an eye examination rather than a different tint. Colour perception is a property of the eye as much as of the lens in front of it.
