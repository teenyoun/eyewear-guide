What Is Actually on the Front of Your Lens: Hard Coats and Scratch Resistance

October 04, 2026 · 8 min read

Plastic lenses are soft, and that is why hard coats exist

Most spectacle lenses are made from polymers. CR-39 and the higher-index plastics used for thin lenses are all relatively soft at the surface - softer than glass, and softer than the dust and grit that ends up on a cleaning cloth. A surface that soft collects fine scratches through ordinary handling, and once the surface is scratched, the light it scatters cannot be polished away without removing the layer that provides the correction.

The answer is a hard coat - also called a scratch-resistant or abrasion-resistant coating. It is a separate, very thin, transparent layer bonded to the lens surface after the lens has been surfaced. Its job is to take the wear so that the lens underneath does not.

What the coating is made of

Hard coats on plastic lenses are typically siloxane or polysiloxane based - a cured silica resin built from silicon chemistry rather than an organic plastic. That choice follows from the job: a silica network is harder than the polymer beneath it, can be made optically clear, and bonds well enough to survive years of cleaning.

Two families are in use. Lacquer coatings are applied wet; vacuum coatings are deposited in a vacuum chamber. On the wet side the common industrial method is dip coating: lenses are submerged in the coating solution and withdrawn slowly enough to leave a uniform film, then cured in a thermal oven for several hours to develop adhesion and abrasion resistance. Other wet routes spin the coating onto the surface or form it in the mould.

How thick is it? Far thinner than most people assume - hard coats on spectacle lenses are usually on the order of 0.5 to 10 microns. For scale, a human hair is roughly 70 microns across. The entire protective layer is a fraction of the thickness of a hair, which is precisely why it protects against handling wear rather than against impact.

Why it is deliberately thin

The coating cannot simply be made thick until it becomes indestructible. It has to stay thin to avoid two failure modes:

  • Crazing and cracking. Coating and lens are different materials with different flexibility. A thick, stiff film on a more flexible substrate develops a network of fine cracks - crazing - which scatters light and looks like a defect even though the lens itself is intact.
  • Stress and loss of adhesion. Thicker films carry more internal stress, and stress is what makes a coating haze, lift or peel at the edges.

Coating a curved surface adds a further difficulty. Drawing a curved lens out of a dip tank tends to leave a slightly uneven film - thinner at the top, thicker at the bottom - because gravity and surface tension act on the draining liquid. Coating lines manage this with controlled withdrawal speeds and by tuning the flow behaviour of the coating liquid itself.

What a hard coat cannot do

A hard coat is an abrasion-resistance layer, not an impact layer and not an anti-reflective layer. Those are separate coatings, and a lens is normally built as a stack: the hard coat sits directly on the substrate and provides the base the other layers bond to, while anti-reflective and hydrophobic or oleophobic layers go on top. Coating requirements for uncut finished spectacle lenses are standardised in Europe through the ISO 8980 series - ISO 8980-4, for instance, specifies the optical and non-optical requirements and the test methods for anti-reflective coatings, durability included.

Common misconceptions

  • "Scratch-resistant means scratch-proof." It does not. Abrasion resistance is graded relative to other lenses and measured with standardised abrasion tests such as the Bayer test, which produces a comparative ratio rather than a real-life pass or fail. A hard-coated lens still scratches; what it resists is the fine haze that ordinary handling creates.
  • "Cleaning cloths wear the coating away." Cleaning does not dissolve the coating. It is scratched by particles trapped in the cloth or left on the lens. That is why rinsing before wiping matters far more than which cloth you use - a dry wipe over dusty lenses is the single most effective way to damage a hard coat, and microfibre cloths only help if the grit is already off.
  • "A scratched lens can be repolished." Not in practice. Polishing removes surface material - on a coated lens that means removing the coating, and on a finished prescription lens it means removing the surface that shapes the focus.
  • "Glass lenses do not need a hard coat." Glass is inherently harder than the plastics, which is why the coating is associated with plastic lenses in the first place. Glass is not immune to damage, and it is considerably heavier.

When to ask instead of cleaning harder

Speak to your optician if the coating on your lenses appears to be crazing - a fine network of cracks rather than isolated scratches - or if it is hazing, lifting or separating at the edges. Those are coating failures rather than wear, and depending on the lens's age and how it was supplied they may be worth raising with the supplier instead of simply replacing. If you work around abrasive dust, metal particles or solvents, say so when you order: the right answer may be a different lens material or purpose-made safety eyewear rather than a better coating on a standard lens. And if your vision is reduced by a haze you cannot clean off, have the lenses examined before concluding that the coating is the whole story.
