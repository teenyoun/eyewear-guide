Laser Safety Glasses: What an Optical Density Number Actually Promises

November 08, 2026 · 9 min read

Optical density is a logarithm, and that changes everything

Laser eyewear is specified by optical density, usually written OD with a number after it. The number is not a rating out of ten and not a percentage. It is a logarithm of attenuation, defined as OD = log₁₀(1/T), where T is the fraction of the beam that gets through. OD 1 passes one tenth of the light. OD 2 passes one hundredth. OD 3 passes one thousandth, which is 0.1 percent. Every single step of one OD is another factor of ten, so the difference between OD 4 and OD 6 is not fifty percent more protection — it is a hundred times less light.

That is what makes the number useful and what makes it easy to misread. A 1 W continuous beam attenuated by OD 3 arrives at 1 mW. The same beam through OD 6 arrives at 1 µW. Both filters look like a pair of tinted safety glasses on the bench; only the printed specification separates them, and only for the wavelength the specification names.

The number is meaningless without a wavelength

Absorption is a property of a material at a wavelength, not a property of a pair of glasses in general. A filter with excellent OD at 1064 nm — the fundamental line of a neodymium YAG laser — may be nearly transparent at 532 nm, the second harmonic of the same laser, and completely unhelpful at 10.6 µm from a carbon dioxide source. Good eyewear therefore carries a table, not a single figure: a wavelength or waveband, the OD at that waveband, and often the laser class or power density the filter was verified against. A pair of goggles printed only "OD 5" with no wavelength is not a specification; it is marketing.

This is also why the marking on certified eyewear looks cryptic. Under EN 207, the European standard for laser protective eyewear, the marking combines a wavelength with a protection scale — the D scale for continuous-wave operation, and other letters for pulsed and giant-pulse modes — plus the optical density. Under EN 208, alignment eyewear is specified instead: it is intended for the visible beam during optical alignment, where the wearer has to see the beam to do the job, and it offers a much more limited level of protection than EN 207 eyewear. Treating an alignment filter as general-purpose protection is a category error, not a matter of degree.

What the optical density does not cover

Even a correctly specified filter only protects the path through the filter. It does not close the gap between the lens and the temple, it does not stop a beam arriving from below or behind, and it does not compensate for a frame that sits too far from the face. Nor does it cover a second wavelength present in the same beam — a frequency-doubled system emits both lines at once, and a filter specified for one of them may leave the other almost unimpeded.

There is also a time dimension that the OD figure hides. The exposure limits that justify a given OD are computed for a defined exposure duration and for a defined beam geometry, which is why the calculation belongs to a laser safety officer working from measured power, beam diameter, divergence, and the nominal hazard zone — not to a buyer choosing the darkest pair on a website. Diffuse reflections from a matte surface are usually far less dangerous than the specular reflection from a shiny one, and the difference can be several orders of magnitude. No single OD number survives that variation intact.

The misconception: buy the highest OD available

The intuitive move — take the biggest number — fails for a practical reason. Visible-light transmittance falls with rising OD, and at high optical densities in the visible band the wearer is looking through a very dark filter. People respond by lifting the glasses, peering over the top, or removing them "just for a second", which is exactly the behaviour the eyewear was meant to prevent. Invisible wavelengths make this worse: at 1064 nm the beam is invisible, so a filter that is comfortable in the visible may be doing nothing at all where the energy actually is.

A related error is assuming that laser goggles are interchangeable with welding filters or sunglass lenses because all three are "dark". They are specified against different quantities: welding filters against a shade scale for visible light with UV and infrared limits, sunglasses against a category scale for traffic use, laser filters against optical density at a named wavelength. The categories do not convert.

Why the frame is part of the product

Laser injury reports rarely involve a beam arriving perfectly perpendicular through the centre of a lens. They involve reflections, off-axis paths, and moments when the eyewear was not being worn. That is why laser eyewear is also tested as a frame — impact resistance, side coverage, and fit — and why the EN 166 base requirements sit underneath the laser-specific standard. A filter with a perfect OD curve mounted in a frame that leaves a two-centimetre gap at the temple is a filter, not eye protection.

After an exposure, the glasses are not the question

Symptoms after a laser exposure — a persistent afterimage, a blind spot, blurred or distorted vision, pain, or a sudden loss of colour perception — are not something to monitor at home while searching for a better specification. Retinal injury from a visible or near-infrared beam is typically painless at the moment it happens, because the retina has no pain receptors. The assessment needs a dilated fundus examination, and the sooner the better. Equally, no article and no product page can tell you which eyewear your particular laser requires: that answer depends on the source parameters and the optical layout, and it comes from a laser safety assessment, not from a shopping filter.
