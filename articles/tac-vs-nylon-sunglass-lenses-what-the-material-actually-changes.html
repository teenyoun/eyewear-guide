TAC vs Nylon Sunglass Lenses: What the Material Actually Changes

October 30, 2026 · 9 min read

Two materials, two manufacturing routes

Almost every polarized sunglass lens on a rack is sold by naming its substrate, and the substrate is doing less work than the marketing implies. TAC, triacetate cellulose, is not primarily a lens material at all: it is the carrier sheet that holds the polarizing layer. Nylon, a polyamide, is a genuine lens substrate, cast or moulded into a curved shell. The two end up looking identical in the frame and behave differently in four respects that matter: optical quality, impact, heat and price.

How a polarizing lens is actually built

Polarization is not a property of plastic. It comes from a film of polyvinyl alcohol that has been stretched in one direction until its polymer chains align, then dyed with iodine or a dichroic dye. The aligned chains absorb light vibrating along one axis and pass the perpendicular axis. That film is fragile, only tens of micrometres thick, and it has to be protected: in a TAC lens it is laminated between two triacetate sheets, which supply the surfaces, the hard coat and the mechanical support.

A TAC polarized lens is therefore a sandwich, and the polarizing efficiency lives entirely in the middle layer. The number quoted on the label compares transmission through the two axes: efficiency equals the difference between parallel and crossed transmission divided by their sum. A film that passes 35 percent along its axis and 0.35 percent across it is 99 percent efficient. That figure describes the film, not the substrate, and it says nothing about how the lens will behave when it is bent into a frame.

What nylon changes

A nylon lens is injection moulded or thermoformed from polyamide, which gives it two advantages. It is inherently tougher than a laminated sheet, and its optical surface can be produced with lower internal stress. Lower stress matters because stress in a transparent polymer creates birefringence, which appears as faint rainbow banding or as a small loss of contrast toward the edge of a wrap. A moulded shell also takes a wrap curve more gracefully than a flat laminate that has to be forced into one.

Nylon can be polarized, but not by being made of polarized material: a polarizing film is laminated to it or insert-moulded with it. The result is a polarized nylon lens, and it is the usual choice for sport wraps where impact and curvature matter more than unit cost.

The numbers that actually differ

  • Refractive index. Triacetate sits at about 1.48 and polyamide at about 1.53. Both are low, which is good for optical quality and largely irrelevant for thickness at the low powers used in sunglasses.
  • Thickness. A laminated TAC lens is often under a millimetre. A moulded nylon shell in a wrap is typically 1.5 mm or more, because it has to hold its curve with little help from the frame.
  • Impact. A laminated film stack fails by delamination and by crack propagation along the layer boundaries. A moulded polyamide shell deforms and recovers. For cycling, sailing and ball sports this is the deciding difference, and it is why nylon dominates those categories.
  • Heat. Triacetate softens at a lower temperature than polyamide. A closed car in summer reaches temperatures that a TAC lens survives but does not enjoy, and repeated cycles are where edge delamination begins.

Three misconceptions worth retiring

TAC is the cheap material, so it must be optically poor. TAC lenses are thin, flat and usually made to good tolerance. Their optical weakness is not the substrate but the laminate: a flat sheet bent into a wrap develops stress, and that is where contrast is lost.

Nylon cannot be polarized. It can. The polarizing film is added to it. What nylon cannot do is become a polarizer by itself, which is true of every lens material.

Thicker means safer. Thickness helps impact only when the material behind it is tough. A two-millimetre lens in a brittle material is worse than a one-millimetre lens in a tough one, and the safety standards test the finished assembly rather than the caliper reading.

Choosing between them

For a fashion frame with a flat or gently curved front, a TAC polarized lens is lighter, thinner and cheaper, and none of that is a compromise. For a wrap that will be worn on a bicycle, in a boat or on a slope, the moulded polyamide shell is the right answer because the failure modes differ: a TAC laminate that delaminates at the edge is a lost lens, while a nylon shell that takes a knock usually keeps working.

The interesting case is the middle: a moderately wrapped lifestyle frame. There the deciding question is not the material label but the base curve and the face-form angle. A flat laminate forced into a six-base wrap will lose more contrast than a nylon shell gains, and no amount of substrate marketing changes that geometry.

When to ask someone who can measure it

The optical difference between the two is subtle at low power and obvious at the edge of a wrap. If a lens shows rainbow banding under indoor light, if contrast drops as you turn your head, or if the tint looks uneven at the rim, that is stress birefringence and it is measurable rather than a matter of taste. Any optician can put the glasses between crossed polarizers and settle it in a minute, which is a better use of ten minutes than reading another specification sheet.
