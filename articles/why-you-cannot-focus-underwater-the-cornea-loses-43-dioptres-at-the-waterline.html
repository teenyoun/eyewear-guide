Why You Cannot Focus Underwater: The Cornea Loses 43 Dioptres at the Waterline

November 19, 2026 · 9 min read

Forty-three dioptres disappear at the waterline

Standing at the edge of a pool, your eyes focus normally in air and hopelessly in water. The change is not subtle and it is not a matter of getting used to it: roughly 43 dioptres of the eye's focusing power vanish the moment water touches the cornea, more than any eye can compensate for by accommodation. This is the clearest demonstration in ophthalmic optics that the cornea, not the lens inside the eye, is the eye's main refracting surface.

Why the cornea works in air

Refraction happens at an interface between two media with different refractive indices, and the power of a curved interface scales with the difference between them. Air has an index of 1.000 and the cornea about 1.376, a difference of 0.376, and the cornea's front radius of roughly 7.8 mm turns that difference into about 43 D. The crystalline lens inside the eye adds only about 19 D, so the eye's total power of roughly 60 D is mostly borrowed from the air-cornea boundary. That boundary is the optical element you never think about until it is taken away.

What water does to that boundary

Water has an index of about 1.333. Against a cornea of 1.376 the difference collapses to 0.043, roughly a ninth of the air value, and the cornea's contribution falls from about 43 D to about 5 D. The eye still has its internal lens, so total power drops to something in the high teens. An eye that was perfectly focused in air is now hyperopic by around 40 D, while the accommodative range of a young adult is perhaps 10 to 14 D and falls with age. There is no version of this that resolves: the image plane sits far behind the retina at every accommodation state.

Why your own glasses do not rescue you

The second surprise is that a prescription lens worn underwater loses power too, for exactly the reason the cornea did. A lens works only because of the index difference between its material and its surroundings. A CR-39 lens of index 1.498 has a power proportional to 0.498 in air; immersed in water at 1.333 that difference drops to 0.165, about a third of the labelled value. A -6.00 D lens behaves like roughly -2.00 D, and the eye behind it is still missing its corneal power. Ordinary spectacles in a pool therefore produce a blurred, distorted, magnified view that is worse than useless for swimming safely.

What a mask or goggle actually restores

A mask or a pair of swimming goggles does one thing optically: it traps a layer of air in front of the eye and restores the air-cornea interface. Corneal power comes back in full, and a normal eye sees normally again. Everything else the mask does is a side effect of the flat glass or polycarbonate plate between the water and that air space.

Because light bends as it leaves the water and enters the plate, the flat port acts as a weak magnifier. Objects appear about 33 percent larger and about 25 percent closer than they are, and the effect is slightly stronger in salt water than in fresh. The same geometry introduces pincushion distortion toward the edges and visible lateral colour fringing, which is why divers learn to turn the head rather than the eyes when they want to inspect something at the edge of the field.

Prescription goggles: what they can and cannot fix

Prescription swim goggles follow the same principle as ordinary glasses, with the eye side in air, so a distance prescription works at full strength. Availability is the limit. Most ready-made options are spherical only, in quarter or half dioptre steps, which means an astigmat needing 1.50 D of cylinder receives the spherical equivalent and a residual blur that is worst on high-contrast edges. Strong prescriptions are also hard to keep thin in a goggle frame, and the vertex distance in a mask is usually larger than in spectacles, so a very strong prescription can feel slightly different from the pair worn on land.

Myth: a tighter strap fixes a blurry mask

If the seal leaks, water enters the air space and corneal power disappears again, so a leaking mask really does cause sudden blur. But a mask that is sealed and still blurry is not suffering from a loose strap. It is either the wrong prescription, a spherical-only compromise in an astigmatic eye, or simply the expected 33 percent magnification that the wearer has not yet adapted to. Over-tightening a strap makes the seal worse rather than better.

Myth: contact lenses in the pool are the simple answer

Contacts do work underwater in the optical sense, because they sit on the eye and move the correction inside the water interface. The risk is not optical. Soft lenses absorb water, and water in pools, hot tubs, lakes and the sea can carry organisms the cornea does not normally have to defend against. If you swim in lenses, daily disposables worn once and discarded under a well-sealed goggle are the lower-risk arrangement, and lenses should never be rinsed or stored in tap water. Anyone who develops a painful, light-sensitive eye after swimming in lenses should be seen promptly rather than waiting it out.

When to ask a professional

Ask an optician about custom prescription goggles if your prescription includes significant cylinder, if you need a bifocal for reading a dive computer, or if you have had refractive surgery and your refraction is unusual. Ask an optometrist if your vision underwater differs between the two eyes, if one eye stays blurry with the mask sealed, or if you notice a new distortion that persists after you surface.

Underwater vision is a lesson in where refraction really happens. The eye is a water-filled instrument tuned to work in air, and every pair of glasses you own is a device for exploiting that accident.
