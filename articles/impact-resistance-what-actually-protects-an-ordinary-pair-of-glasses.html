Impact Resistance: What Actually Protects an Ordinary Pair of Glasses

October 14, 2026 · 8 min read

﻿

Impact resistance is not an optional extra

Most wearers do not know that impact resistance is not a premium feature on ordinary glasses. In the United States, prescription lenses are required to be impact resistant by regulation rather than by marketing choice. The rule dates back to the 1970s and applies to everyday dress eyewear, not only to safety glasses. It is the reason a lens cannot simply be a shaped piece of the cheapest clear plastic on the shelf.

The test that defines compliance is a drop ball test. A steel ball five eighths of an inch across, weighing about 0.56 ounces, is dropped from a height of 50 inches onto the lens, and the lens must survive it. In metric terms that is a steel sphere roughly 16 mm in diameter falling about 127 cm onto the centre of the lens. Compliance is normally demonstrated on a statistically representative sample of lenses rather than on each individual lens, which is worth remembering: the statement on the packet describes a design, not a certificate issued for the pair in your hand.

Brittle and ductile: why material choice decides the outcome

Impact resistance is not a single property, and two lenses with similar hardness can behave completely differently. What matters is how a material fails. CR-39, the conventional plastic lens material, is comparatively brittle. It is hard, clear and takes coatings well, but when it is overloaded it tends to crack rather than yield. Polycarbonate behaves the other way. It is ductile, meaning it deforms and absorbs energy before failing, which is why it became the default for children's eyewear, sports and safety applications. Trivex sits between the two, offering good impact behaviour with better optical quality than polycarbonate.

There is a tension built into lens design here. A surface that resists scratching is usually a hard surface, and hard surfaces are the ones that crack under a sudden load. A material that absorbs impact is usually softer and marks more easily. This is why a lens surface is normally a stack rather than a single substance: a relatively tough substrate carrying a thin hard coat, and above that an anti-reflective stack and a topcoat. Each layer is optimised for a different threat, and the impact performance comes mainly from the substrate underneath.

The floor is not the ceiling: dress lenses versus safety lenses

The regulatory requirement for everyday lenses sets a minimum, not a maximum. Occupational safety standards ask materially more. ANSI Z87.1 in the United States and EN 166 in Europe require lenses to survive high-mass and high-velocity impact tests, and they also cover the frame, the mount, and how much of the eye is covered from the side. A lens can meet the requirement for ordinary glasses and still not qualify as a safety lens, and the reverse is also true in comfort terms: a safety lens is typically thicker, heavier and mounted in a frame designed to stay put during an impact, which is not always what you want to wear for eight hours at a desk.

Thickness enters the picture in a way that is easy to underestimate. A lens is machined to achieve the required power, and at high minus powers the centre becomes thin while the edge thickens, whereas a high plus lens is the opposite. Because impact performance depends on the material and the thickness present at the point of contact, two lenses in the same material and the same prescription can differ simply because one was surfaced with a larger minimum centre thickness. That is a legitimate reason for a lens to be slightly heavier than expected.

Common misconceptions

  • "Shatterproof." No lens is shatterproof. Impact-resistant materials are specified to deform rather than crack under a defined test, and the word on a shop sign has no defined technical meaning.
  • "Any plastic lens is safe for a child." The material matters more than the word plastic. A thin lens in a brittle material can fail where a ductile material of the same power survives.
  • "Safety glasses are just thicker ordinary glasses." The standards cover the frame, the side coverage and the mounting as well as the lens, which is why an ordinary frame cannot be converted into a safety frame by changing lenses.
  • "Passing the drop ball test means the lens cannot be damaged." The test is a single defined strike against a new lens. Edge chips, scratches and long exposure to ultraviolet light all change how a lens behaves afterwards, and the test says nothing about any of that.

When to ask for more than the minimum

  • Children and teenagers, and anyone who wears glasses during sport or rough play.
  • Work involving flying particles, chemicals, or any hazard identified in a risk assessment. Insist on marked occupational eyewear rather than ordinary lenses.
  • A lens that has chipped at the edge, or a frame that repeatedly lets a lens pop out.
  • High minus prescriptions, where the centre of the lens is already thin.
  • Any situation where you want the maker to state the standard the lens actually meets, rather than relying on a general claim of toughness.

Mounting matters as much as the lens does. A lens that survives the drop ball test on a laboratory holder can still fail in a frame if it is held under stress, if the groove is cut too tight, or if it sits loose enough to move on impact. That is why the same material in a rimless mounting, where the lens is drilled and screwed, is a different proposition from the same material held in a full rim. None of this changes the material test, but all of it changes what happens on a face.
