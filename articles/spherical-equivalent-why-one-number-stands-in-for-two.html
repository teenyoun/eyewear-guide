Spherical Equivalent: Why One Number Stands In for Two

November 13, 2026 · 9 min read

Two powers inside one lens

A lens that corrects astigmatism has two focal lengths, not one. Its surface curvature differs between two meridians at right angles — the principal meridians — and light arriving along each meridian is focused at a different distance. In a sphero-cylindrical prescription those two powers are written as sphere and cylinder, and the axis says which meridian the cylinder occupies. Between the two focal lines sits a blur disc rather than a point, and its narrowest cross-section is called the circle of least confusion.

The dioptric distance between the two focal lines is the astigmatic interval. That interval is the reason a single summary number is possible at all: the circle of least confusion sits at approximately its midpoint, so a plain sphere of the right power can put that blur circle on the retina even though neither meridian is in focus.

The formula, and where the half comes from

That midpoint is the spherical equivalent: SE = sphere + ½ cylinder. The half is not a convention or a fudge. It is the arithmetic statement that the two meridians are equally spaced either side of a centre, so the centre lies halfway between them in dioptres. A prescription of −2.00 with a −1.00 cylinder has meridians at −2.00 and −3.00, and its spherical equivalent is −2.50. A prescription of +1.00 with −2.00 cylinder has meridians at +1.00 and −1.00, and its spherical equivalent is 0.00 — a mixed astigmat with one meridian long-sighted and one short-sighted, summarised as plano.

That second example is the one to keep in mind, because it shows exactly what the number hides. An SE of 0.00 sounds like no correction needed. The eye in question has two dioptres of astigmatism and will not see well without a cylinder.

Where the number is used honestly

Defining refractive error for research and screening. The standard definition of myopia used in the current consensus literature is a spherical equivalent of −0.50 D or more myopic, measured with accommodation relaxed. Studies need a single number per eye, and SE is that number.

Comparing two prescriptions. Because SE is invariant to transposition, two prescriptions written in different forms can be compared at a glance. −2.00 −1.00 × 180 and −3.00 +1.00 × 090 are the same lens, and their shared SE of −2.50 makes that obvious in a way the raw numbers do not.

Starting a contact lens trial. When a soft lens is selected for a wearer with low astigmatism, the spherical equivalent is a legitimate starting point if the residual astigmatism is small enough to be tolerated. It is a starting point, not the prescription: the trial is what decides.

Judging anisometropia. Two eyes with very different refractive errors are compared on SE, because that is the number that predicts how differently the two retinal images will be scaled — the reason aniseikonia becomes a practical problem in high unilateral prescriptions.

Where it fails

The failure mode is always the same: astigmatism is thrown away, and with it the reason the lens is not a simple sphere. A spherical equivalent lens corrects the defocus component of a refractive error and leaves the astigmatic component uncorrected. At half a dioptre of cylinder that is usually acceptable. At two dioptres it is not, whatever the SE says.

The second failure is directional. Astigmatism at an oblique axis produces a blur pattern whose principal directions are diagonal, and the visual system tolerates oblique astigmatism less comfortably than with-the-rule astigmatism of the same power. Two prescriptions with the same SE and the same cylinder but different axes are not equivalent in experience, and the arithmetic cannot see the difference.

The third is that SE says nothing about higher-order aberration, pupil size or the quality of the tear film, which is why two people with identical SE prescriptions can describe their vision in completely different terms.

The claims that need correcting

My spherical equivalent is my prescription. The prescription has three numbers precisely because one is not enough. SE is a summary of the defocus, not a description of the correction.

A plano spherical equivalent means I do not need glasses. Plano SE with cylinder means the two meridians straddle zero. That eye has astigmatism and needs a cylinder, even though its average power is nil.

Two prescriptions with the same SE are interchangeable. They may differ in cylinder power and axis, which is exactly the part the SE discards. Interchangeability is a claim about the lens, and the lens is defined by all three numbers.

Over-the-counter readers work because the SE is close enough. Readers are spheres with identical power in both eyes and no cylinder and no axis provision. For a wearer with meaningful astigmatism, matching the SE leaves the astigmatism uncorrected — a small amount is often tolerable, a large amount is not, and the reader cannot tell the difference.

When to take the question to an optometrist

If you have found your way to a spherical equivalent and are wondering whether a lens made to that number would suit you, the honest answer is that the number was never designed to answer that question. It is a research and comparison tool. What decides whether a correction suits your eyes is a refraction, a trial frame or a trial lens, and a conversation about how you use your vision.

Two situations are worth raising directly. The first is a large difference between the two eyes, where aniseikonia and prismatic imbalance can both follow from the prescription itself. The second is any persistent mismatch between what a prescription predicts and what you experience — blurred vision at one distance, distortion that does not settle, or strain after close work. Both are clinical questions, and neither is answered by averaging two dioptres into one.
