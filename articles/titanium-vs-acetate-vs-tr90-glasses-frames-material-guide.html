Titanium vs Acetate vs TR90 Glasses Frames — Material Guide

July 31, 2026 · 7 min read

Frame material decides three things you feel every day: how heavy the glasses are, how much abuse they survive, and whether an optician can adjust them at all. It also decides something you notice later — how they age. Here is how titanium, acetate and TR90 actually differ, and where each one stops being the right answer.

The three properties that decide everything

Every frame question reduces to three material properties. Density sets weight: multiply it by the volume of the frame and you have the grams sitting on your nose. Elastic modulus sets stiffness — how far a given section deflects under load. Glass transition temperature, written Tg, is the point where a polymer stops behaving like a rigid solid and starts behaving like a soft rubber; it sets whether a frame can be reshaped with heat at all.

Stiffness does not scale with thickness, it scales with the cube of it. For a rectangular section of width b and thickness h, the second moment of area is I = b·h³/12, so bending stiffness is proportional to E × h³. Halve a temple's thickness and one eighth of its stiffness remains. That relationship is the whole titanium argument: its modulus is around 105 GPa against roughly 200 GPa for stainless steel, so a titanium part must be slightly thicker to match a steel one — but its density is 4.5 g/cm³ against 7.9, so at equal stiffness it is still far lighter. Stiffness divided by density is specific stiffness, and it explains how a frame feels.

Titanium: light, corrosion-proof, and often mislabelled

Commercially pure titanium is soft, corrosion-resistant and weldable; beta titanium alloys add vanadium, chromium and aluminium to become springy and heat-treatable; temples are often beta alloy while the front stays pure. Neither rusts, because titanium forms a passive oxide layer a few nanometres thick that rebuilds itself when scratched — the same mechanism behind its biocompatibility.

The label worth checking is “memory titanium”. True shape memory comes from nickel-titanium alloy, nitinol, which is roughly half nickel by weight. Nitinol springs back from being folded, and it contains nickel: EN 16128 is the reference test method for nickel release from spectacle frames and sunglasses, applying the 0.5 µg/cm² per week limit used for items in prolonged skin contact. A frame that passes is compliant, not nickel-free, and the distinction matters if you react to nickel.

What titanium does not do is survive neglect: its weak points are the parts that are not titanium — acetate tips, silicone pads, and screws that loosen in a softer insert.

Acetate: the only common frame material you can actually adjust

Cellulose acetate is plant-derived: cotton linters and wood pulp, acetylated and blended with plasticiser. Frames are not moulded from it. Blocks are sliced into sheets, the front is cut and often built up in layers so colour runs through the material, and the edges are polished by tumbling with wooden chips. That is why acetate holds a translucent depth no coating reproduces.

Its Tg sits near 100°C, low enough that hot water or a frame heater makes it pliable. That single number is why acetate can be widened, re-tensioned, re-angled and repaired for years — the most useful property a frame can have if your face does not match the standard headform. The cost is mass and toughness: density is only about 1.3 g/cm³, but acetate needs thick sections for strength, so a front typically runs 25–40 g, and under impact it does not deform, it cracks.

TR90: a nylon-family polyamide, not a moulded toy

TR90 is a trade name for a transparent amorphous polyamide from the nylon family, chosen for the combination of low density and high elongation before break. It is injection moulded, so it leaves the tool close to finished shape — which makes it the cheapest of the three to produce. Its advantage is elastic: a TR90 frame bends far before it fails, then returns.

The same property is the limitation. Low modulus plus high recovery means the material resists permanent adjustment: bend a TR90 temple and it creeps back toward the shape of the mould. Repairs are usually replacement. Because nothing is cut or polished, colour lives in the compound and the palette stays flatter than acetate's.

Choosing by use, not by prestige

  • All-day office wear: titanium. Mass is the variable you notice most over ten hours, and lighter frames press the pads less.
  • A fit that needs correcting: acetate. If your bridge is low, your head narrow, or one ear sits higher, you want a material an optician can move; titanium and TR90 cannot.
  • Sport, children, clumsy adults: TR90. Flexibility plus low replacement cost beats every other argument.
  • High prescription: titanium, because light frames matter more when the lenses themselves are heavy. A strong minus lens adds front mass, which is exactly what makes a frame rotate forward.
  • Nickel sensitivity: acetate, or titanium with a verified nickel-release test. Avoid assuming that “titanium” on the box means no nickel anywhere in the frame.

Myths worth dropping

  • “Titanium is unbreakable.” Thin titanium temples bend and kink like any metal, and work-hardening at the fold makes them harder to straighten than acetate.
  • “TR90 is indestructible.” It survives being sat on, not abrasion, and it cannot be re-tightened.
  • “Acetate is just cheap plastic.” Injection-moulded frames and cut-and-polished acetate are different objects; the giveaway is colour depth and whether heat moves it.
  • “Heavier means better made.” Mass is a cost, not a feature. Quality shows in hinge construction, temple alignment and how the frame holds adjustment after a month.
  • “Memory metal never needs adjusting.” Nitinol returns to its set shape, but that shape is not your head; it still needs a fitting.

Care follows the material

Acetate dislikes heat and solvent: a summer dashboard or a splash of alcohol can craze the surface or distort the front permanently, and that damage does not polish out. Titanium tolerates both but shows fingerprints and wears its pads and screws first, so check screws monthly. TR90 is chemically forgiving yet scratches easily, and since it cannot be polished or reshaped, prevention is the only maintenance. Whatever the material, ISO 12870 is the frame standard covering mechanical requirements and test methods — the reference to cite when a retailer makes durability claims.

What to check before buying

Read the temple: material, lens width, bridge width, temple length. Confirm the seller can adjust the frame. Ask whether spare screws and pads exist — a titanium frame with unobtainable pads fails long before the titanium does. The best material is the one that still fits in three years, not the one with the longest specification sheet.
