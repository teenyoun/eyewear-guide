Acanthamoeba Keratitis: Why Water Is the One Thing Contact Lenses Cannot Tolerate

November 26, 2026 · 9 min read

A rare infection with an unusually specific cause

Acanthamoeba keratitis is an infection of the cornea by a free-living amoeba found in soil, tap water, swimming pools, hot tubs and household plumbing. It is uncommon: the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention estimates roughly one to two new cases per million contact lens wearers per year. It is also one of the few eye conditions in which a single everyday habit is a well-established risk factor, and in which the habit is entirely avoidable.

The principle: a cyst built to survive a swimming pool

Acanthamoeba exists in two forms. The trophozoite is the active, feeding form. Under stress — drying, temperature change, starvation, disinfectant — it converts into a cyst: a double-walled, dormant structure that is markedly resistant to chemical and physical insult, including the chlorine concentrations maintained in swimming pools and hot tubs.

That is the crux of the problem. Pool chlorine is dosed to control bacteria and to protect swimmers from waterborne gastrointestinal illness. It is not a sterilant for amoebic cysts, and it was never designed to be. Water can be legally and hygienically acceptable for swimming and still contain viable Acanthamoeba.

Now add the lens. A soft contact lens is not an inert window: its surface adsorbs organisms and organic material, and a lens worn under a closed lid holds a thin layer of water and debris against the corneal epithelium for hours, in a microenvironment warmer and less oxygenated than the open eye. Rinsing a lens in tap water, or wearing one into a shower, pool or hot tub, delivers the organism directly to the surface it needs. The eyelid supplies the incubation step, which is why the exposure that matters is water contact while wearing the lens, not merely water in the same room.

The same logic explains why the disease is so strongly associated with lens wear rather than with swimming alone. Most reported cases occur in contact lens wearers, and it is rare in people who do not wear lenses at all.

What the mechanism means in a bathroom

  • No tap water on lenses or cases. Not to rinse, not to wet them, not to top up solution. This is the highest-value change available and it costs nothing.
  • No lenses in showers, pools, hot tubs, lakes or the sea. If you swim in lenses, use daily disposables and discard them straight afterwards, with sealed swimming goggles over the top — and understand that this reduces exposure rather than eliminating it.
  • The case is part of the system. Rub it, rinse it with fresh solution rather than water, and let it air-dry open. A case closed wet, or topped up with old solution, concentrates exactly what the disinfectant is supposed to remove. Cases are commonly recommended for replacement every few months; they are cheap and their failure mode is not.
  • Care products have a defined performance bar, not a guarantee. Contact lens care products are tested to ISO 14729, which sets log-reduction requirements against specified bacteria and fungi for products and regimens. Passing that test is a real, regulated standard of performance. It is not a sterilisation certificate for every organism in the environment, and no solution compensates for water entering the system in the first place.
  • Daily disposables lower risk but do not change the rules. No case and no reuse removes the storage pathway. It does not make a lens safe to wear into a hot tub.

Beliefs that increase risk

  • "Chlorinated water is sterile." It is disinfected, which is a different claim. Cysts survive routine pool chlorination.
  • "Salt water is natural, so it is clean." Acanthamoeba is found in fresh and coastal water alike; salinity is not a barrier.
  • "I only rinse with tap water, I do not store the lens in it." The rinse is the exposure. The organism does not need minutes; it needs contact and a closed lid.
  • "If something were wrong, it would hurt immediately." Early Acanthamoeba keratitis is easily mistaken for other forms of keratitis, and the pain is often described as out of proportion to what is visible — a reason for urgent referral, not reassurance. The textbook ring-shaped infiltrate is a later finding, not an early one.
  • "Antibiotic drops will sort it out." It is not a bacterium. Treatment is a prolonged specialist regimen using specific agents, and the outcome depends heavily on how early it is recognised.

When to take the lens out and be seen the same day

Remove the lens and keep it, along with the case, if you have: pain that seems disproportionate to how the eye looks, marked light sensitivity, a foreign-body sensation that does not settle, redness that worsens, blurred vision, or watering that continues after the lens is out. Same-day ophthalmology is the right level of urgency for that combination, and faster if the eye is painful and the vision is dropping.

Do not restart lens wear while waiting, do not use leftover drops from a previous episode, and do not assume lubricants are treatment. Bringing the lens and the case to the appointment is useful, because the organism can sometimes be recovered from them when corneal samples are equivocal.

The wider point is less dramatic than the disease. Almost every case traces back to a small, ordinary decision: a rinse under the tap, a lens left in for a shower, a case that was never dried. None of this is a diagnosis and none of it substitutes for an examination. It is simply the mechanism, which happens to be unusually clear.
