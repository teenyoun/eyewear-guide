Monovision: One Eye for Distance, One for Near, and What the Brain Pays for It

November 14, 2026 · 9 min read

One lens cannot hold two focal lengths

Every pair of single vision glasses makes a decision. The lens has one power, and that power puts one distance in focus on the retina; everything else is blurred by an amount that depends on how far it sits from that distance and how small the pupil is. Reading at 40 cm needs about 2.50 dioptres of accommodation, and the ability to supply that declines through the forties until the near point has moved past the distance the person actually works at. Presbyopia is not a disease and it does not stop: the add needed at 40 cm climbs from nothing to around one dioptre in the mid-forties and often past two dioptres by the late fifties.

Spectacle solutions add a second zone, whether bifocal, trifocal or progressive. Contact lenses and refractive surgery have one extra option that spectacles do not, and it is the one this article is about. Rather than putting two powers into one lens, monovision puts one power into each eye.

What monovision actually sets up

In conventional monovision the dominant eye is corrected for distance and the non-dominant eye is left slightly myopic, or given a plus add, for near. In contact lens practice the near eye typically carries an add in the region of +1.50 to +2.00 dioptres. In refractive surgery the target is usually a residual myopia of about -1.50 dioptres in the non-dominant eye. Either way the optical result is identical: a deliberate, induced anisometropia, meaning the two eyes now carry different refractive errors, and each eye is defocused at one of the two distances the person uses all day.

Mini-monovision is the conservative version, with a smaller difference, often under one dioptre, bought at the price of less near help. Modified monovision mixes the idea with a multifocal design in one eye. The vocabulary matters when a practitioner offers you an option, because two clinics can both say monovision and mean quite different amounts of anisometropia.

The mechanism that makes it tolerable, and what it costs

Two eyes receiving different image quality should produce confusion, and in a laboratory they do. What makes monovision workable in ordinary life is interocular blur suppression: the visual system learns to attend to the eye that is in focus at that moment and to discard the defocused image from the other. It is not the same as the rivalry produced by two incompatible pictures. It is a learned attentional filter, and establishing it takes days to weeks rather than minutes. Sensory dominance, the ordinary preference for one eye's input, is what the fitter is assessing when deciding which eye to leave near, and it is also why a proportion of wearers never settle: their suppression is not strong enough, or not stable enough under fatigue and low light.

The cost is binocularity. Stereopsis, the depth sense built from the small horizontal differences between the two eyes, depends on both retinal images being comparably sharp and comparably sized. Give one eye two dioptres of blur and the disparity signal degrades. Reported measurements show distance stereopsis falling as the add increases, with adds around +2.25 dioptres and above producing a marked reduction, while +1.50 dioptres is often described as the compromise point where near function improves without destroying depth judgement. Binocular summation, the small gain in contrast sensitivity that comes from having two clear eyes, is also given up. None of this is visible on a Snellen chart, which is the reason a monovision fit is judged by tasks rather than by acuity alone.

Why the trial matters more than the theory

Suppression ability varies enormously between people and cannot be predicted from a prescription or from a dominant-eye test alone. That is why monovision is trialled with contact lenses for a week or two before anyone commits to a surgical version: the trial is the test. A useful trial includes the awkward cases rather than the easy ones, which means driving at night, reading in dim light, walking down stairs, and whatever the person does for a living. Some wearers adapt and stop thinking about it. Others find the compromise constant: a slight sense of looking through the wrong part of a window, worse in low light when the pupil dilates and depth of field shrinks, and worse again when tired.

Occupations that depend on fine stereopsis are a poor match, and the list is worth stating plainly: pilots, surgeons and dentists, crane and excavator operators, precision assembly and inspection work, and anyone whose sport involves catching or hitting a fast ball. Alternating monovision, where the two eyes swap roles according to task, exists but demands more of the wearer and is fitted deliberately rather than casually.

Common misconceptions

The most persistent is that monovision is another name for multifocal lenses. It is not. A multifocal or extended-depth-of-focus design distributes two or more powers across the pupil of each eye, so both eyes retain some binocularity and the brain is not asked to abandon one image wholesale. Monovision is cruder and, for a well-selected patient, more robust, but it is a different trade. The second misconception is that being able to read and to drive means adaptation has succeeded. Both tasks can be done with one eye suppressed at a time; stereopsis is a separate question and it is tested separately. The third is that monovision harms the eye. No lens power changes the health of an eye, but there is a real reason not to use the approach in children and teenagers: imposed unequal blur during visual development is a route to amblyopia, and monovision is an adult strategy for an adult problem.

When to ask for something else

Persistent headache, a pulling sensation at the desk, double vision, or discomfort driving after dark are all reasons to revisit the fitting rather than to persevere. If a trial leaves you avoiding stairs or unable to judge where the kerb is, the difference is too large or the approach is wrong for you. Bring the specific tasks that failed, because the fix may be a smaller difference, a different near eye, or plain spectacles for the demanding part of the day. Monovision is a trade, and a good eyecare professional will tell you exactly what you are trading before you accept it.
