Photokeratitis: Why Snow Blindness Is a Sunburn on the Front of the Eye

November 08, 2026 · 8 min read

The damage lands in the outermost cells

Photokeratitis is an ultraviolet burn of the cornea — the clear front window of the eye. It is the same injury welders call arc eye and skiers call snow blindness, and the mechanism is the same in both cases. The corneal epithelium, the thin outermost layer, absorbs strongly in the ultraviolet B band, roughly 280 to 315 nm, and a good deal of ultraviolet A as well. Those wavelengths do not pass through to the retina; they are stopped in the first few cell layers, which is exactly where the dose is deposited. The cells are damaged, and the surface begins to break up into tiny punctate defects.

The crucial detail is timing. Like a sunburn, the injury is not felt while it is happening. Symptoms typically begin several hours after the exposure — commonly somewhere between six and twelve — which is why people come home from a bright day on the water feeling fine and wake in the night with eyes that feel full of sand. By the time it hurts, the exposure is long over and the only thing left to do is support the surface while it regenerates.

Why snow, water and altitude raise the dose

The eye receives ultraviolet from two directions: directly from the sun, and reflected from whatever is underneath. Fresh snow is one of the most reflective natural surfaces there is — the large majority of incident ultraviolet bounces back upward, straight into a face that is tilted down toward the slope. Water and pale sand do the same thing less dramatically, and pale concrete in a city does it enough to matter over a long afternoon. This reflected component is why a hat brim alone is not sufficient: it shades the direct path and leaves the upward path wide open, which is the argument for wrap-around coverage and side panels rather than a narrow lens.

Altitude compounds it. There is less atmosphere above to absorb ultraviolet, so the intensity at the surface rises as you climb, and the snow line usually coincides with the thinner air. Ultraviolet also passes through thin cloud far more readily than intuition suggests, which is why the classic snow-blindness scenario is an overcast spring day on a glacier rather than a bluebird one.

Two terms used precisely

  • Photokeratitis. Inflammation of the cornea caused by ultraviolet or short-wavelength visible radiation. The injury is epithelial, which is why it usually heals without scarring as the surface layer turns over.
  • Punctate epithelial keratopathy. The clinical appearance of that injury under a slit lamp: many small, scattered defects in the surface cells rather than one discrete abrasion.

The symptoms follow from the surface damage: a gritty or foreign-body sensation, profuse tearing, marked light sensitivity, a burning ache, and sometimes enough surface irregularity to blur vision. Both eyes are usually affected at once, and there is typically nothing visible on the outside of the eye to explain how much it hurts.

The misconception: a dark lens is a protective lens

This is the one that actively makes things worse. A dark tint reduces visible light, so the pupil opens wider. If the lens does not absorb ultraviolet, more ultraviolet reaches the cornea and the crystalline lens through a larger pupil, and the wearer feels comfortable while receiving a higher dose. Tint and ultraviolet absorption are separate properties, and only one of them protects the eye.

The concrete version of this is the UV400 claim. It means the lens blocks radiation up to 400 nm, which covers the whole ultraviolet range plus the violet end of the visible spectrum. A lens labelled only "dark", "polarised", or "Category 3" is telling you about visible light transmission and glare, not about ultraviolet. Under EN ISO 12312-1, sunglass lenses are required to limit ultraviolet transmittance regardless of their category, which is what makes a compliant cheap pair safer for the mountains than an expensive non-compliant one. Polarisation, by contrast, is a glare filter — it does nothing for ultraviolet.

A second misconception is that the injury only happens in extreme environments. Long days of reflection off water, a full day of sailing, a bright afternoon of spring skiing in a T-shirt, or an hour of arc welding with the helmet lifted will all do it. The dose is cumulative across the day, not a single dramatic event.

What to do, and what not to do

Do not rub. The surface is already disrupted, and rubbing can convert a diffuse epithelial injury into a frank abrasion. Contact lenses should come out, and they should stay out until the surface has settled. Driving yourself anywhere is a poor idea while your eyes are streaming and light-sensitive. Most episodes of uncomplicated photokeratitis settle over one to three days as the epithelium regenerates, but "most" is not "all", and the symptoms overlap with things that need active treatment — a retained foreign body, a corneal infection, a contact-lens-related problem, or a chemical exposure that the patient did not connect to the eye.

Seek professional assessment the same day if the pain is severe, if vision has dropped rather than merely blurred, if you wear contact lenses, if only one eye is affected, or if nothing has improved after about two days. A corneal burn is a clinical finding, and the treatment decision — lubrication, a therapeutic bandage lens, antibiotic cover, or simply time — depends on what the surface actually looks like under magnification.

Prevention is a coverage problem

The practical rule for snow, water and altitude is a lens that meets the ultraviolet requirement, a category dark enough for the brightness, and a shape that blocks light arriving from below and from the side. For welding and for bright artificial sources, the filter has to be the specified one for the process rather than whatever is to hand. Wrap-style frames, a brim, and sunglasses worn consistently — including on overcast days — do more than any single expensive feature, because the exposure that causes photokeratitis is the one you did not notice accumulating.
