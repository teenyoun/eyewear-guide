Edge Thickness: Why Your Lens Is Thickest Where the Frame Is Widest

November 19, 2026 · 8 min read

The rim of a lens is arithmetic

When a pair of glasses looks bulky at the edge, the cause is almost never lens quality. It is the sagitta of the surfaces, the distance a curved surface drops between its centre and its rim, and sagitta grows with the square of the distance from the optical centre. Move the rim outward by a fifth and the edge thickens by nearly half. Nothing in the marketing of a lens changes that arithmetic; it only changes the numbers you put into it.

Sagitta: the number behind every edge

A spherical surface of radius R falls by s = y squared divided by 2R over a distance y from its vertex, for the small apertures used in spectacles. The radius follows from the power and the refractive index: R = (n - 1) / F, in metres. A -5.00 D lens in ordinary 1.50 plastic therefore has a surface radius of 0.5 / 5 = 0.100 m, or 100 mm. At 30 mm from the optical centre that surface has fallen 900 / 200 = 4.5 mm. At 35 mm it has fallen 1225 / 200 = 6.13 mm. Those two numbers are the difference between a lens that looks neat in a 50 mm frame and one that looks chunky in a 56 mm frame, on an identical prescription.

Worked example: same prescription, two frames

Take -5.00 D in 1.50 index with a 1.5 mm centre thickness. In a frame where the furthest rim point sits 28 mm from the optical centre, the added sag is 784 / 200 = 3.92 mm. In a larger frame that puts the rim at 36 mm, the sag becomes 1296 / 200 = 6.48 mm, about 2.5 mm more material on the same edge. This is a single-surface approximation, since a real lens is a meniscus with a front base curve that subtracts a roughly constant offset, but the way thickness scales with rim distance is captured correctly: frame size is an optical decision, not only a styling one.

Why the optical centre is not the middle of the lens

Lenses are decentred. The optical centre is moved toward the frame so that the wearer's pupil sits behind it, which is what keeps prism out of the line of sight. On a minus lens, decentration pulls the thinnest part of the lens toward the pupil and pushes more material to the temporal edge. That is why one side of the rim can be visibly thicker than the other, and why a lens that looks acceptable when held up loose can look worse once it is edged into an asymmetric frame. Minimum blank size, decentration and frame shape all feed the same calculation.

Index changes the radius, not the ambition

A higher-index material bends light more per unit of curvature, so the same power needs a flatter surface and therefore less sag. At 1.67, a -5.00 D surface has a radius of 0.67 / 5 = 134 mm, and the sag at 35 mm falls from 6.13 mm to 1225 / 268 = 4.57 mm: about a quarter less, and clearly slimmer in a frame. The gain is real but not free. Higher index usually means a lower Abbe number, so colour fringing away from the centre increases, and several high-index materials are denser, so a thinner lens is not automatically a lighter one.

The plus lens is the mirror image

Everything reverses for plus powers. The edge is thin, the centre is thick, and centre thickness is driven by the same sagitta: a +6.00 D lens needs a centre thick enough to leave a workable edge all the way round the blank. This is why strong plus lenses get heavy in the middle, why they are sometimes lenticularised, and why high plus prescriptions often look better in a smaller, rounder frame that cuts the rim distance down. The geometry is symmetrical; only the direction of the problem changes.

Myth: a higher index always means a thinner lens

It usually means a thinner edge at the same power and frame size, but always is wrong for three reasons. Minimum centre thickness rules exist for impact safety, particularly in polycarbonate and for plus powers, and they set a floor that no index can go below. Density varies, so 1.74 can be thinner and no lighter. And at low powers the difference between 1.50 and 1.67 is a fraction of a millimetre, invisible in the finished frame and not worth the dispersion penalty.

Myth: the thick part is where the laboratory ground it unevenly

An uneven rim is normally geometry, not a defect. A minus lens is thickest at the point furthest from its optical centre, so if that centre sits 4 mm nasal of the geometric centre, the temporal rim is a couple of millimetres thicker than the nasal rim at -5.00 D. Asymmetric frames, a wide bridge and strong decentration all exaggerate it. A rim that is uneven in a way that does not follow the optical centre, or a single thick point on an otherwise even edge, is worth showing to your optician.

Using this before you order

If you want to know how a prescription will look rather than how it sounded in the shop, run the numbers: the lens thickness calculator takes power, index, frame width and decentration and returns an edge estimate. Above roughly -4.00 D or +4.00 D, the frame dimensions matter more than the lens brand, and a 3 mm smaller eyesize often beats an upgrade in index. If a pair arrives noticeably bulkier than expected, ask whether the optical centre was placed as intended before assuming the lens was made wrong.

When to ask for a second opinion

Ask your optician when the prescription is strong, the frame is large, or the two eyes differ substantially, because those are the cases where a smaller frame or a different index genuinely changes the outcome. Ask again if a lens looks thick on one side only when the frame is symmetric, if the edge shows a visible step at the bevel, or if the glasses feel heavy at the nose despite thin lenses, since weight depends on density and centre of mass rather than on rim profile alone.

Edge thickness is one of the few things in eyewear that can be predicted with a pencil before anything is ordered. Treat it as design, and most of the disappointment disappears.
