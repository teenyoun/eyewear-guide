Nose Pad Contact Mechanics: Why Marks Appear and What Actually Relieves Them

November 06, 2026 · 9 min read

A pair of glasses weighing twenty-eight grams can leave an indentation that lasts an hour. The frame is not heavy by any ordinary standard, and yet the skin on the sides of the nose registers it continuously. The explanation is not weight but concentration: a small mass delivered through two very small contact patches produces a pressure that the tissue underneath notices, and a pad that has lost its shape delivers the same mass through an even smaller patch.

Pressure is force divided by area

This is the whole argument in five words, and it explains why two frames of identical weight can feel completely different. A spectacle front sits on the nose and the ears; for a well-fitted frame the nose carries a substantial share of the load. Take a front assembly of about thirty grams resting mostly on two pads. Thirty grams is a force of roughly 0.29 newtons. If each pad touches the skin over about fifteen square millimetres, the total contact area is thirty square millimetres, or three times ten to the minus five square metres. Dividing gives a pressure of roughly ten kilopascals.

Now halve the contact area, which is what happens when a pad tilts onto its edge or hardens and stops conforming. The pressure doubles to about twenty kilopascals without a single gram being added. This is why the intuitive fix, buying a lighter frame, often fails: the new frame may have smaller pads, a wider bridge or a steeper pad angle, and the pressure at the skin can be higher than before.

Geometry decides the contact patch

A nose pad is not a cushion. It is a small rigid or semi-rigid plate on an adjustable arm, and the area that actually touches the nose is determined by three angles: how the pad is splayed relative to the frame, how it is tilted fore and aft, and how much of its width is presented to the slope of the nose. Get those right and the pad lies flat against the tissue with its full face in contact. Get them wrong and it rocks onto a corner.

Pad shape matters for the same reason. Symmetrical pads suit a narrow bridge with steep sides; asymmetric pads, with a longer edge on the nose side, follow a flatter, wider bridge where a symmetrical pad would touch only along one edge. A pad of the wrong shape cannot be adjusted into full contact.

Material, and why hardness beats brand names

Pads are sold as silicone, as PVC, as "medical grade" or as titanium. What the skin responds to is the effective stiffness of the assembly, which is the pad material plus the metal arm behind it. A soft pad on a stiff arm still concentrates load, because the arm does not deflect. A slightly firmer pad on a well-shaped arm that follows the nose can distribute load better than a very soft pad on a flat one.

Material also changes friction. Silicone grips, which limits sliding but transmits shear to the skin when the frame moves. Harder plastics slide, so the frame creeps down and the wearer pushes it back up, adding a transient load far above the resting one.

The chain that produces a mark

Marks and soreness rarely have a single cause. The usual progression looks like this:

  • Contact area shrinks because a pad has tilted, hardened or been bent away from the nose. Pressure rises immediately.
  • The frame is wider than the face, so the pads are pushed inward by the temples and press harder on the sides of the bridge rather than resting on it.
  • The front is heavy or the lever is long. A strong minus lens with a thick edge, or a large eye size, increases the torque about the pad line.
  • The pad surface glazes or oils, friction falls, the frame slides, and the wearer pushes it up dozens of times a day. Each push is a load spike.
  • The skin reacts, either mechanically from pressure or chemically from what the pad and its plating release.

Only the last of these is a materials problem. The first four are fitting problems, and they are the common ones.

The nickel question, and what the number means

Metal pads, pad arms and temple tips are the parts of a frame in prolonged contact with skin, and nickel is the classic sensitizer. European regulation addresses this through REACH Annex XVII, entry 27, which sets a nickel release limit of 0.5 micrograms per square centimetre per week for articles intended for prolonged skin contact, with a stricter 0.2 microgram limit for items inserted into pierced skin. Compliance is demonstrated by test, using EN 1811 as the reference release method, with EN 12472 used to simulate wear and corrosion before testing.

In practice, "nickel free" and "nickel release compliant" are different claims. A component can contain nickel and still comply, because the regulation limits what escapes onto the skin, not what is in the alloy. Conversely, plating that wears through can expose a nickel-containing layer that previously passed, which is why a frame comfortable for a year can suddenly cause a rash.

The claims worth correcting

  • "Tighter pads stop the glasses sliding." Usually the opposite. Compression squeezes oil onto the surface, friction drops, and the frame slides more until it settles into a sore spot.
  • "Silicone is hypoallergenic." Silicone itself is chemically inert, but pads contain fillers, pigments and sometimes an adhesive backing, and the metal arm sits underneath. Contact dermatitis traced to a pad is not automatically a silicone allergy.
  • "Marks mean the glasses are too heavy." Marks mean pressure, and pressure is area as much as mass. A twenty-gram frame with two pads on edge will mark more than a thirty-gram frame with well-seated pads.
  • "I can just bend the pads back into place." A pad arm is a fatigue component. Repeated bending at the same point work-hardens the metal and eventually cracks it, usually where the arm joins the pad box.

What actually helps, in order

First, check contact. In a mirror, see whether each pad sits flat against the nose or rests on an edge; a bright wear line along one side means it is not seated. Second, check width: if the temples press into the sides of the head, the frame is too narrow and the pads are being levered inward. Third, reduce the load: a thinner lens in a smaller eye size changes the torque more than a different pad compound will. Fourth, if the skin is red, raised or itchy rather than simply indented, treat it as a possible contact reaction and have the frame checked.

When to see an optician

Book an adjustment if an indentation lasts more than half an hour after removal, if the frame leaves asymmetric marks, if one pad does not touch at all, or if there is any redness, scaling or itching that persists. Those are fitting and, in the last case, dermatological questions. Nose pad adjustment is a two-minute job with the right pliers and a genuinely difficult one with the wrong tool, which is the practical reason not to attempt it at the kitchen table.
