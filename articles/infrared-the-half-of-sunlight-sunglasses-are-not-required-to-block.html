Infrared: The Half of Sunlight Sunglasses Are Not Required to Block

November 15, 2026 · 9 min read

The part of the spectrum nobody prints on the tag

Sunglass labels talk about two things: UV, expressed as UV400 or a percentage, and visible light, expressed as a filter category from 0 to 4. Both are worth understanding. Neither of them describes the third band of sunlight that reaches the eye, which is infrared, and which no general sunglass standard requires a manufacturer to limit.

That omission is not a conspiracy. It reflects how the standards were built and what they were built to control. It does, however, leave a gap that matters in specific places: high altitude, snow, water, and any workplace with a hot source in front of it.

How the eye divides up infrared

Infrared runs from about 780 nm, where visible red ends, out to roughly 1 mm. The band is usually split into IR-A from 780 to 1400 nm, IR-B from 1400 to 3000 nm, and IR-C beyond that. The divisions matter because the eye is not equally transparent across them.

Sunlight's infrared load is dominated by IR-A. A substantial share of that band, up to roughly 1400 nm, passes through the cornea, the lens and the vitreous and lands on the retina and choroid, where it is absorbed as heat. Wavelengths beyond about 1400 nm are largely stopped by the lens and vitreous, and beyond about 2000 nm the anterior chamber absorbs almost everything. The practical result is a two-part problem: the retina takes the near-infrared heat, and the front of the eye takes the far-infrared heat.

For solar exposure the band worth thinking about is roughly 780 to 2500 nm, which is where the eye's own filters stop being a complete answer.

What a tinted lens does and does not change

Spectral transmittance is the fraction of light at each wavelength that a filter passes. A grey tint reduces the visible band fairly evenly and, depending on the substrate and any added absorber, may do very little in the near-infrared. Grey is a colour, not a specification.

Luminous transmittance, written as tau-V, is the visible-light figure that the filter categories are built on. Category 3 sits between 8% and 18%, category 4 between 3% and 8%. Those ranges say something about brightness and nothing at all about heat.

Here the physics turns slightly awkward. A dark lens reduces visible light, so the pupil opens to compensate. A wider pupil admits more of everything that is not filtered, including near-infrared, and it also changes the geometry of where that light lands. This is the same argument that has been made for decades about UV: a dark lens without UV filtering can leave the eye worse off than no lens at all, because the pupil is dilated behind a filter that does not cover the band in question. The same reasoning applies to infrared, which is why an absorbing layer in the near-infrared is a meaningful addition to a sun lens rather than a marketing line.

The occupational side of the same problem

Industrial heat sources are a different exposure from the sun: much higher radiance, much closer, and often with a strong far-infrared component. That is the territory of EN 171, the European standard for infrared filters, which sits inside the EN 166 family of eye and face protection and appears as a marking on the product rather than as a claim on a web page. Furnaces, molten metal, glassblowing and welding all have specific filter requirements, and fashion sunglasses are not a substitute for any of them.

The historical association between infrared and lens damage is real and old: the classical description of the glassblower's or furnace worker's cataract dates back well over a century. What is less settled is the mechanism. Chronic heating of the lens is the plausible route, but separating that from age, UV, and every other exposure in a working life is difficult, and the causal chain is still described in the literature as likely rather than proven. That uncertainty cuts both ways: it is not a reason to be alarmed by a sunny afternoon, and it is a reason to take occupational exposure seriously.

Three claims that do not hold up

A darker lens means more protection. Darkness is a visible-light property. A category 4 lens is darker than a category 2 lens for the same reason a curtain is darker than a blind, and it tells you nothing about what happens at 1000 nm.

IR is what makes your face feel hot, so a tint blocks it. The warmth you feel on your skin is largely absorbed in the first millimetres of tissue, which is why it feels like heat rather than light. A tint that is transparent in the near-infrared transmits it whether or not it looks dark.

UV400 covers everything. UV400 is a statement about wavelengths below 400 nm. Infrared begins at 780 nm. The two numbers are not adjacent, and the gap between them is the entire visible spectrum.

What actually helps

  • Read the standard, not the adjective. For sun lenses, ISO 12312-1 sets the UV and visible requirements; for road use it adds a check that signal lights remain distinguishable, and it rules out lenses under 75% luminous transmittance for twilight or night driving.
  • Look for an explicit near-infrared absorber if you spend hours on snow, water or at altitude, where the infrared load is high and the visible load is not the whole story.
  • Use EN 171 filters for hot work. Not a fashion pair, and not a pair bought for the beach.
  • Keep the sides covered. Light arrives around a lens as well as through it, and a wrapped shape with a close fit reduces the total dose at every wavelength at once.

When heat exposure needs a professional, not a product

See an eyecare professional if you have pain, watering or a gritty sensation after intense light exposure, if after-images persist far longer than they should, if glare and halos have become a night-time problem, or if vision in one eye has changed in a way that glasses do not fix. Occupational exposure should be assessed by whoever owns the risk assessment, with the correct standard in hand; no article, and no lens tag, replaces that.

The honest summary is narrow. Infrared is a real part of sunlight, the eye's own optics deliver part of it to the retina, general sunglass standards do not require a manufacturer to block it, and a darker lens is not a cooler eye. That is enough to change how you read a label, which is all this article claims to do.
