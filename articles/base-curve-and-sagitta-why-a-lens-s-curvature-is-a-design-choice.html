Base Curve and Sagitta: Why a Lens's Curvature Is a Design Choice

October 10, 2026 · 9 min read

The number your prescription never mentions

A prescription specifies power. It says nothing about how curved the lens surfaces are, and yet every lens has two, each with a specific curvature. The front one is called the base curve, and it is chosen by the laboratory or fixed by the lens design rather than written on your prescription. Two lenses carrying exactly the same power can be built on different base curves, and they will not behave identically.

The reason is that the power of a lens is the difference between its two surfaces. Make the front steeper and the back must be adjusted to keep the same net power, so the base curve changes the physical shape of the lens even when the prescription does not change at all. That is why base curve is a design decision with consequences, not an accounting detail.

Sagitta: how deep the curve goes

The quantity that connects curvature to thickness is the sagitta: the depth of the curved surface, measured from the edge to the centre over a given diameter. For a circular lens of diameter d cut from a sphere of radius R, the sagitta is approximately:

sagitta ≈ (d ÷ 2)² ÷ (2 × R)

And the radius follows from the power and the refractive index: for a single surface in air, F = (n − 1) ÷ R, so a higher index produces a larger radius for the same power — a flatter curve. Put the two together and the practical result appears: for a given power, a higher-index material is flatter, so its sagitta is smaller, which is a large part of why high-index lenses come out thinner.

A worked example makes it concrete. Take a +6.00 D curve across a 50 mm lens. At an index of 1.50 the radius is about 83 mm and the sagitta is roughly 3.8 mm. Switch to an index of 1.67 and the radius grows to about 112 mm, bringing the sagitta down to about 2.8 mm — a full millimetre less depth from the same power. That difference is thickness you can see, and it is the mechanism the site's lens thickness calculator lets you vary directly.

Why the laboratory cares about base curve at all

If any base curve could carry any prescription, the choice would not matter. It matters for four practical reasons.

  • Frame compatibility. A wrapped or strongly curved sports frame needs a front surface that follows the frame. Too flat a base curve leaves the lens edges proud of the rim, or pulls the lens into a shape the frame cannot hold cleanly.
  • Peripheral optics. A steeper base curve keeps the lens surface closer to perpendicular to off-axis light, which reduces oblique astigmatism — the blur and distortion in the outer part of the lens. This is the same trade-off that made aspheric designs common.
  • Cosmetics and magnification. Steeper curves on plus powers mean thicker centres and a more magnified appearance; flatter curves thin the lens but push the optical work harder at the edges. There is no direction that is free.
  • Manufacturing constraints. Only certain combinations of base curve and power can be produced within the thickness and quality the lab guarantees, which is why a very high plus prescription in a very wrapped frame can be impossible rather than merely expensive.

Base curve also interacts with lens form: the split of the total power between the front and back surfaces. The same power can be arranged as a steep front with a flat back, or the reverse. For most everyday prescriptions the difference is small; at high powers, or in wrapped frames, it becomes visible in the periphery and in how the lens sits.

Common misconceptions

  • "My prescription includes the base curve." It usually does not. Prescriptions carry sphere, cylinder, axis and sometimes prism and reading addition — curvature is a design choice made downstream.
  • "Thinner always means a better base curve." Thickness and optical quality are separate axes. A lens can be made thinner by flattening the curve in ways that cost peripheral sharpness, which is a trade rather than a gain.
  • "Higher index makes the lens stronger." Index changes how much the material bends light per unit of curvature. It says nothing about impact resistance, which depends on the material type and its treatment.
  • "The same prescription will look the same in any frame." Change the frame's curvature and the lab may have to change the form, which can alter edge thickness and peripheral behaviour even though the numbers on the prescription are untouched.
  • "A flat lens is a thin lens." Sagitta and thickness are related but not identical, because edge thickness also depends on minimum blank requirements and how the lens is decentred into the frame.

When to ask an optician or optometrist

Ask specifically about base curve and form if you are choosing a wrapped or strongly curved frame, if your prescription is above roughly plus or minus four dioptres, if you have been told a particular frame "cannot be glazed" and want to know why, or if you notice distortion in the outer part of a new pair while the centre is perfectly clear. Those are design questions, and a laboratory or optician can answer them with the actual numbers for your frame. If a new pair is uncomfortable rather than merely different, get the fit and the lens form checked before assuming you need to adapt.
