Rainbow Patterns in Your Lens: Stress Birefringence Explained

October 23, 2026 · 7 min read

The odd thing you notice on a bright day

Tilt a pair of glasses against a bright sky or a phone screen while wearing polarised sunglasses, and the lens may light up with coloured bands, a dark cross, or a rainbow ring that follows the rim. Nothing has been spilled on it. The pattern is inside the material, and it is evidence of a physical property that most optical polymers are supposed to avoid: stress birefringence.

Why stress changes the refractive index

Plastics used in lenses, polycarbonate and the various high-index resins among them, are amorphous polymers: long molecular chains tangled without crystalline order, which is precisely why they are transparent. When such a material is strained, the chains straighten and align slightly in the direction of the strain. Aligned chains present a different electron density along their axis than across it, and the refractive index becomes direction-dependent. The material has become birefringent.

The consequence for light is retardation. A ray entering the strained region splits into two components polarised at right angles, travelling at slightly different speeds. On leaving, one is delayed relative to the other by an amount that depends on the magnitude of the index difference and the thickness it passed through. Where that retardation corresponds to part of a wavelength, the two components recombine into a different colour or a different intensity than they entered with.

You cannot see any of this in ordinary light, because the eye does not analyse polarisation. Put a polarising filter in front and an analyser behind, and the hidden pattern becomes visible. That is exactly the arrangement in a photoelastic bench, and it is also what happens when you look at a lens through polarised sunglasses while the light behind it is already polarised.

Where the stress comes from

  • Frame fit. A lens edged a fraction oversized and forced into a rim is compressed at its perimeter. The stress is highest near the rim and decays toward the centre, which is why the pattern usually starts at the edge.
  • Screws and mounts. Rimless lenses are drilled and clamped; over-tightened screws create local stress concentrations around the holes.
  • Injection moulding. Polycarbonate lenses are moulded, and the flow of melt plus uneven cooling leaves frozen-in orientation. Good moulding minimises it; cheap moulding leaves streaks.
  • Surfacing. A lens surfaced too aggressively, or held under pressure while being blocked, can retain strain.

Does it actually harm the vision?

Usually not much, and the honest answer is that the severity matters more than the presence. A mild pattern confined to a few millimetres at the rim sits outside the part of the lens you look through. Two things make it worth attention.

The first is magnitude. Enough stress produces enough retardation to blur, particularly with high-power lenses where the material is thin and the stress per unit area is higher. The second is location: stress that reaches the optical centre, which happens when a lens is badly distorted by an overtight frame or a drilled mount, degrades the image where it matters most.

There is a real-world check a wearer can do in ten seconds. Hold the lens in front of a white screen, look through a polarising filter, and rotate the filter. If the pattern is a faint rim artefact, the lens is behaving normally. If a pronounced cross or dense fringes reach the middle, the mounting is suspect and a remount in a correctly sized frame is cheaper than a remade lens.

Why the same effect is used on purpose

Photoelasticity, the technique of reading stress from coloured fringe patterns, is a standard engineering method rather than an optical curiosity. A polariscope shows where a part will crack before it does so, and the fringe count can be calibrated to give a number rather than an impression. Lens workshops use the same principle at inspection: a lens that shows a dense, asymmetrical pattern is a lens that has been strained, and it is cheaper to catch that before edging than after the customer complains.

That is the useful way to think about the fringes on your own glasses. They are a stress map you can read without instruments, and the reading is about the frame and the mounting as much as about the plastic.

Misconceptions

  • "Rainbow lines mean the coating is failing." Coating defects look like crazing, peeling or a milky haze, not ordered fringes. Coloured bands that rotate with a polariser are stress, not delamination.
  • "Polycarbonate is immune because it is tough." Toughness and stress birefringence are unrelated properties. Polycarbonate is in fact strongly stress-optical, which is why it shows patterns readily.
  • "Any pattern means a defective lens." Almost every plastic lens shows something at the rim under polarised light. The question is extent and location, not existence.
  • "Polarised sunglasses will show this on everyone." Only when the light entering the lens is already polarised in a compatible orientation; ordinary daylight through a window will not produce the display.

When to take it to an optician

Bring it up if vision is noticeably worse with the lenses in the frame than with the same lenses held loose, if the distortion is one-sided, if you see colour fringing around high-contrast objects in normal light, or if a lens has been re-mounted or re-edged shortly before the problem appeared. Frames that are slightly too small, screws tightened without a torque limit, and lenses forced into rims are the usual culprits, and all of them are correctable without replacing the optics.
