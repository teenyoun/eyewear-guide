EN ISO 12312-1: What the European Sunglass Standard Actually Tests

November 03, 2026 · 8 min read

A standard is a test list, not a quality badge

EN ISO 12312-1 carries the title "Eye and face protection — Sunglasses and related eyewear — Part 1: Sunglasses for general use". It was written to replace the older European standard EN 1836, and it governs the ordinary pair of sunglasses you buy for walking, driving and sitting outside. What matters for a buyer is the shape of the document rather than its reputation. It is a set of requirements, each one tied to a measurement method defined in a companion standard, EN ISO 12311. It is not a grading system. It does not rank one pair above another, it does not say a lens is durable, and it does not cover every situation a sunglass might be used in. Filters for looking directly at the sun, filters for use behind the wheel at night, and occupational eye protectors are governed elsewhere or not at all.

That distinction matters because almost every marketing claim in the category borrows the authority of the standard without doing any of the work the standard describes. If you know which quantities are actually constrained, you can tell a real specification from a decorative one.

The categories constrain exactly one number

The five filter categories, 0 through 4, are defined by luminous transmittance, written τV: the proportion of visible light the filter lets through, weighted by the photopic luminosity function V(λ), which describes the eye's sensitivity in daylight, and measured under a specified geometry. The bands are fixed and narrow enough to be useful:

  • Category 0: above 80% — essentially a clear or very pale lens
  • Category 1: 43% to 80% — light tint, overcast conditions
  • Category 2: 18% to 43% — the general-purpose middle band
  • Category 3: 8% to 18% — strong sun, the usual summer sunglass
  • Category 4: 3% to 8% — very dark, for extreme brightness

Here is the part that is almost never explained. Because τV is a weighted average across the visible spectrum rather than a flat percentage, two lenses can sit in the same category and behave nothing alike. A grey lens attenuates all visible wavelengths roughly equally, so colours stay recognisable and the scene simply gets darker. A brown or amber lens attenuates the short-wavelength end more heavily, which raises the apparent contrast of a scene at the cost of shifting colour. Same category, same number, different experience. The category tells you how much light survives, not which light.

Why the ultraviolet requirement is not the tint

The single most stubborn misconception in sunglasses is that darkness and ultraviolet protection are the same property, or at least that they travel together. They are produced by different mechanisms.

Visible attenuation comes from dye or pigment dispersed in the lens. Ultraviolet attenuation comes from the base material and from ultraviolet absorbers compounded into it during manufacture. The two can be engineered independently, which is precisely why the standard addresses them in separate clauses instead of deriving one from the other. A pale category 1 lens made from a material with good intrinsic ultraviolet absorption can measure lower in the ultraviolet than a very dark lens that was simply dyed heavily. When a product says UV400, the claim is a boundary: transmittance is held down out to 400 nm, the upper edge of the UVA band. It is a wavelength statement. It is not a statement that the optical tolerances, the signal-light requirement or anything else in the standard was met, and a printed logo is not evidence that a test was performed at all.

What else gets a number attached

Sunglasses are nominally afocal: a plano lens should have no optical power, because power is a prescription and a prescription given by accident is a problem. The standard therefore sets tolerances on the spherical power and the prism of a finished filter. This is not pedantry. A moulded lens with a degree of unintended power behaves like a mild prescription in front of an eye that did not ask for one, and the usual result is blurred vision at distance, a pulling sensation, or a headache that appears an hour into a drive and disappears when the glasses come off.

The other requirement with real-world teeth concerns signal lights. A tint that is selective enough can make red, amber and green hard to tell apart, so the standard requires that a driver can still recognise them. This is why heavily blue-blocking "night driving" tints sit awkwardly against road-use requirements, and why a lens can be perfectly protective against ultraviolet while being a poor choice for the road.

Claims that do not survive contact with the standard

  • "Category 4 is the strongest, so it is the best." Category 4 transmits only 3% to 8% of visible light and must not be worn for driving in most European jurisdictions. It exists for glaciers, high altitude and open water, where the problem is overwhelming brightness rather than ordinary sun.
  • "Darker means more UV protection." Different mechanisms, as above. A dark lens with no ultraviolet absorber is a worse choice than a pale lens with one.
  • "UV400 is a protection rating." It is a wavelength boundary at 400 nm. Useful, but partial.
  • "Polarised is a protection level." Polarisation removes light reflected from horizontal surfaces such as water and road. It says nothing about ultraviolet.
  • "The CE mark means an independent laboratory tested this pair." Sunglasses placed on the market as personal protective equipment fall into the lowest risk category, where the manufacturer's own declaration supports the mark. The mark records a claim of conformity, not a third-party certificate.

Where the standard is silent

Nothing in EN ISO 12312-1 predicts how long a coating will last, how a frame will behave after two summers in a hot car, or whether a lens will survive a ball. Mechanical requirements in a general-use sunglass standard are basic by design; a sport with impact risk needs a sport standard with an impact test, which is a different document answering a different question. Buying a "certified" sunglass for squash because it is certified is a category error, not a safety measure.

When the label stops being the useful question

If a sunglass lens makes a straight kerb look curved, gives you double vision in one eye, or produces a headache that reliably appears within the first hour, the cause is more likely an optical or fitting problem than a tint problem, and no category number will explain it. The same is true if one lens feels different from the other, if you need a prescription in the sun and are wearing a plano lens over it, or if you drive at night and find the world too bright or too dim. Those are reasons to have your eyes and your current correction measured rather than to read another specification. A standard can tell you what a lens was tested for. It cannot tell you whether that lens is right for your eyes.
