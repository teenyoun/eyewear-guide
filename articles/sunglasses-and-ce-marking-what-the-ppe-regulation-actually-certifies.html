Sunglasses and CE Marking: What the PPE Regulation Actually Certifies

November 26, 2026 · 8 min read

A tinted lens is regulated as protective equipment

Ask most shoppers what the CE mark on a pair of sunglasses means and the answer is something like "it passed European safety tests". The accurate answer is narrower, more specific, and more useful once you know it: the mark is a declaration by the company placing the product on the market that it meets the essential health and safety requirements of a particular regulation, and for sunglasses that regulation is the PPE Regulation rather than a general consumer-goods directive.

The principle: the hazard is defined first, the product is classified second

Regulation (EU) 2016/425 replaced Directive 89/686/EEC and has applied since 21 April 2018. It defines personal protective equipment as equipment designed and manufactured to be worn or held by a person for protection against one or more health or safety hazards, and it sorts that equipment into three risk categories in Annex I.

Category I, the lowest, is a closed list of minimal risks. Item (d) on that list reads: damage to the eyes due to exposure to sunlight, other than during observation of the sun. That single line is the legal hinge for the whole sunglasses market. A tinted lens sold for general use is an eye protector against solar radiation, therefore it is PPE, therefore it is governed by 2016/425.

Two details follow directly from the wording. First, the carve-out for observation of the sun is why eclipse viewers are treated separately: they are not Category I sunglasses, which is why they are assessed against ISO 12312-2 instead. Second, classification follows the intended hazard, not the price, the brand or the lens material. A pair from a beach rack and a pair from a designer boutique sit in the same legal category and carry the same obligations.

What Category I actually asks of the manufacturer

Category I products are assessed by internal production control, Module A in Annex IV of the regulation. In plain terms: the manufacturer builds to a technical file, self-declares conformity, signs an EU declaration of conformity, and affixes the CE marking. No EU type-examination is required and no Notified Body is involved, because the regulation treats the risk as minimal.

The technical route to the presumption of conformity is a harmonised standard, EN ISO 12312-1 for sunglasses and related eye wear for general use. That standard is where the engineering lives: transmittance limits in the ultraviolet, visible and infrared, requirements on optical quality and power tolerance, robustness tests, and the filter category scheme.

So the correct reading of the mark is that the product has been declared conformant to 2016/425, with EN ISO 12312-1 cited as the route. It is a declaration backed by a technical file, and it is enforceable, because market surveillance authorities can demand the file and the declaration. What it is not is a per-pair laboratory certificate.

The number that decides whether you can wear them driving

The most consequential marking on a pair of sunglasses is not the CE mark but the filter category, 0 to 4, defined by visible light transmittance bands: category 0 at 80–100% transmittance, category 1 at 43–80%, category 2 at 18–43%, category 3 at 8–18%, and category 4 at 3–8%.

Categories 0 to 3 are suitable for road use and driving. Category 4 is not, and by the standard it must be marked as unsuitable for driving and road use. That is a statement about the quantity of light reaching the eye, not about quality. It also explains why the darkest lens on the shelf is not the best lens on the shelf: past category 3 you are buying a filter for glacier and high-altitude snow, and it will turn a shaded road at dusk into a hazard.

UV protection is a separate requirement, and the popular shorthand "UV400" describes a cut-off wavelength rather than a certification scheme. The requirement in the standard is that ultraviolet transmittance below 400 nm be limited to a small fraction, and a lens either meets that or does not, whether or not anyone prints the number. A UV400 label is a claim about spectral cut-off; a CE mark is a claim about conformity. They are different statements, and neither is a measurement performed on the pair in your hand.

Claims that do not survive contact with the regulation

  • "CE means an EU laboratory tested these." For Category I sunglasses, no. Conformity is self-declared. Testing may be in-house or subcontracted, and the obligation is to hold the evidence, not to submit samples to an authority.
  • "Category 4 is the maximum, so it is the best protection." It is the maximum tint, and it is explicitly unsuitable for driving. For road use, categories 2 and 3 are the useful range.
  • "Polarised means UV-protected." The properties are independent. Polarisation attenuates light according to its plane of vibration, which is what removes glare from wet roads and water. UV attenuation comes from the lens material and its absorbers. A well-made polarised lens will normally block UV as well, but neither property implies the other.
  • "A dark lens protects my eyes because less light gets in." Tint density and UV cut-off are separate specifications, and a dark lens without adequate UV absorption is the worst combination, because the pupil opens behind it and admits more of the ultraviolet the lens failed to stop. That is a mechanism, not a scare story, and it is why the UV requirement exists independently of the tint.
  • "The CE mark covers the frame too." Frames have their own standard, EN ISO 12870, covering dimensions, mechanical strength and material behaviour. A conformant lens in a frame that cracks at the hinge is a conformant lens in a defective product.
  • "CE and UKCA are interchangeable." They are separate marking regimes, and the recognition arrangements between them have been revised more than once. For the Great Britain market, check the current position rather than assuming either mark is accepted.

When this stops being a shopping question

Photokeratitis, the corneal sunburn behind the phrase snow blindness, has a characteristic delay of several hours between exposure and symptoms: a gritty burning sensation, marked light sensitivity, watering, and sometimes a temporary drop in vision. It is not solved by choosing a darker pair next time, and it is not a product-selection problem at all. The same applies to light sensitivity that outlasts the exposure by more than a day, to eye pain after time on water or snow or under a sunbed, and to any foreign-body sensation that does not settle. Those need an eye examination.

For everyone else the practical rule is unglamorous. Read the category number, check that a UV claim is present and specific, and treat the CE mark as what it is: a declaration of conformity to a standard that sets transmittance limits, not a promise about how the pair will fit or feel.
