Peroxide or Multipurpose: What Contact Lens Solutions Actually Do

November 09, 2026 · 8 min read

Two chemistries on the same shelf

A contact lens case holds a problem that has to be solved every night: a piece of polymer that has been in contact with tears, mucus, lipids, proteins and skin flora, sitting in a warm moist container, and expected to go back on the eye the next morning without carrying anything pathogenic with it. Two entirely different strategies are sold to solve that problem, and they are not variations on a theme.

Multipurpose solutions are preservative-based. They clean, rinse, disinfect and store, and they rely on antimicrobial agents that stay in the bottle at low concentration. Hydrogen peroxide systems are oxidant-based: they deliver a strong disinfectant and then destroy it before the lens touches the eye.

Peroxide: an oxidiser that has to be removed again

A 3 percent hydrogen peroxide solution is 30,000 parts per million of peroxide. That is not a typo and it is the reason the system is designed the way it is. At that concentration the solution is a genuinely effective biocide — it oxidises lipid membranes and protein, and it is active against organisms that preservative-based systems struggle with — but it is also corrosive to corneal epithelium and would be extremely painful if it reached the eye.

So the delivery system is the product, not the bottle. The lens goes into a special case with a catalase disc, or a platinum-coated element, built into the base or the basket. Catalase breaks peroxide down into water and oxygen. The lens sits in the case for a period the manufacturer specifies, commonly six hours or more, and by the end the peroxide has gone and the lens is sitting in saline.

Two consequences follow. First, the case is not interchangeable with a multipurpose case, and losing it effectively loses the system. Second, timing is the safety mechanism. Take the lens out early and you are putting residual peroxide, in an acidic solution, onto the cornea — which produces stinging, watering and redness that can be mistaken for an allergic reaction.

Older two-step systems required a separate neutralising tablet dropped in after disinfection. Those still exist, and they fail in a very specific way: if the tablet is forgotten, the lens in the case is still full-strength peroxide.

Multipurpose: preservatives at parts per million

A typical multipurpose solution contains polyhexamethylene biguanide (PHMB) at around one part per million, or polyquaternium-1 at roughly ten parts per million, together with a buffer, a chelating agent such as EDTA that weakens bacterial cell walls, and surfactants that lift deposits off the surface.

PHMB and polyquaternium-1 work by adsorbing onto cell membranes and disrupting them. They are effective at these concentrations because the target is a single-celled organism, and they are tolerable to the cornea for the same reason they are present in such small amounts. The margin between "kills Acanthamoeba" and "irritates the eye" is narrower than the four-order-of-magnitude gap between the two product families suggests.

There is also a mechanical component. Multipurpose solutions are formulated to be used with a rub-and-rinse step: a few seconds of gentle rubbing across the palm removes the biofilm that a surfactant alone will not shift. This matters more with silicone hydrogel lenses, whose surfaces adsorb both lipids and preservatives, and it matters most for the organisms that form organised communities rather than floating free.

The case is part of the product

  • Replace the case on the interval the solution manufacturer states, and sooner if it looks scratched or cloudy. A scratched case surface is a protected habitat.
  • Never top up. Adding fresh solution to old solution dilutes the preservative and leaves the organisms that survived the first night.
  • Air-dry the case open, face down on a clean tissue, between uses. A closed wet case is a culture dish.
  • Never use tap water at any stage, including rinsing the case and including rinsing a lens that has fallen. Tap water is the documented route by which Acanthamoeba reaches the cornea.
  • Saline is not a disinfectant. It is a rinse and a storage medium for lenses that are already disinfected.

Common misconceptions, corrected

"No-rub" means no cleaning. It means the manufacturer has validated a no-rub regimen for the lenses it was tested with. It does not mean deposits vanish, and the rub step remains the most effective single action available at home.

Peroxide is always the gentler choice. Fully neutralised peroxide leaves no preservative residue, which is a real advantage for some wearers. But an incompletely neutralised lens is a chemical burn, and peroxide systems are less forgiving of shortcuts than a preservative system.

Solutions are interchangeable between lens types. They are tested against specific lens materials. A solution validated for a conventional hydrogel is not automatically validated for a silicone hydrogel, and the difference shows up as comfort or as uptake of preservative.

A stinging lens means the solution has gone off. More often it means the lens went in too early, or the case was not the matching case, or the lens had residual cleaner on it. Persistent stinging after a correctly timed peroxide cycle is a reason to stop and ask, not to rinse and persist.

Rubbing the eye when something is under the lens helps. It is the fastest way to abrade the epithelium with a lens edge. Remove the lens, rinse with the correct solution, and if the sensation persists, leave the lens out.

When to stop and be seen

Stop wearing lenses and contact the practice the same day for eye pain, a red eye that does not settle, light sensitivity, discharge, a white spot on the cornea, or vision that remains blurred after the lens is out. Microbiological keratitis in lens wearers is rare but sight-threatening, it is frequently associated with overnight wear, contaminated cases and water exposure, and its outcome depends heavily on how quickly it is treated.

The choice between the two systems is genuinely individual: peroxide suits wearers who want no preservative residue and will follow a fixed routine, while a multipurpose solution suits wearers who need a portable single bottle and will actually rub and rinse. What is not individual is the chemistry. A 30,000 ppm oxidiser and a 1 ppm preservative demand different habits, and most solution-related problems come from applying the habits of one to the other.
