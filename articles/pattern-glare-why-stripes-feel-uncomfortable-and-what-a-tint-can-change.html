Pattern Glare: Why Stripes Feel Uncomfortable and What a Tint Can Change

November 27, 2026 · 9 min read

Why a striped shirt can be unpleasant to look at

Some people find high-contrast stripes actively uncomfortable. Escalator treads, venetian blinds, finely striped shirts, the grille of a radiator, the converging lines of a car park: the pattern is visible, nothing is wrong with the eyes, and yet looking at it produces a distinct sense of strain, sometimes a flicker at the edges, occasionally a headache that arrives an hour later. This has a name, pattern glare, and it is a spatial phenomenon rather than a brightness one.

The principle: the visual cortex responds to spatial frequency, not just to light

Vision science describes patterns by their spatial frequency: the number of cycles of light and dark per degree of visual angle. A grating of about three cycles per degree is close to the peak of the human contrast sensitivity function, which is the range of stripe widths the visual system detects most efficiently. That is also the range in which many people report the most discomfort when the contrast is high.

The standard way to demonstrate this uses three square-wave gratings at roughly 0.5, 3 and 12 cycles per degree. Most viewers rate the middle one as the most unpleasant, even though the fine and coarse patterns are equally visible. The excess discomfort at the intermediate frequency is what the term pattern glare refers to. It is a property of the observer's visual system, and it can be measured, which is why it is used as a clinical probe rather than a personality trait.

The usual explanation is cortical: the middle spatial frequency drives a larger population of neurons in the primary visual cortex, and in some people that response is less well damped. Studies of migraine have repeatedly reported differences in cortical excitability and in habituation to repetitive visual stimuli, which fits the observation that pattern glare scores tend to be higher in people who get migraine with aura. The mechanism is not fully settled, and it should not be presented as one.

What this explains, and what it does not

  • Why the trigger is geometric. Text is a stripe pattern. So is a slatted blind. The discomfort scales with contrast and with the width of the stripes, not with how bright the room is.
  • Why fluorescent flicker is a separate problem. Flicker is a temporal phenomenon; the 100 or 120 Hz modulation from mains-powered lighting is a different mechanism with a different measurement. Both can be present at once, which is why an office can be unpleasant in two independent ways.
  • Why "eye strain" is the wrong label. Pattern glare is not muscle fatigue from overuse. It is a response to a stimulus, and it can occur in a person who has been looking at the pattern for ten seconds.
  • Why the effect is not the same as photophobia. Photophobia is a response to light level; pattern glare is a response to spatial structure. A person can have one, both, or neither.

Where tinted lenses fit, and where they do not

A tint reduces the contrast of the retinal image, and reducing contrast reduces the drive to the mechanism. That is the entire optical rationale, and it is a reasonable one: the same grating at half the contrast is a weaker stimulus. Precision tinting systems go further, asking the wearer to adjust hue and saturation until a test pattern stops being aversive, on the argument that the optimal tint differs between individuals.

What the evidence supports is modest. Tinted lenses can reduce reported discomfort from patterned stimuli in some wearers, and individual responses vary enough that a generic tint is a guess. What the evidence does not support is the claim that a tint prevents migraine attacks, and it certainly does not support treating a tint as a substitute for medical management. For people who find screens or fluorescent-lit rooms aversive, a tint is a comfort measure; it is not prophylaxis.

There is a practical trap here. A tint that is too dark reduces contrast everywhere, including the contrast of the text you are trying to read, and the wearer compensates by turning up the brightness, which restores the original stimulus. The useful tint is usually a light one, chosen for the individual rather than for maximum darkness. For light-sensitivity complaints rather than pattern complaints, the logic is different again, and a much lighter filter is often enough.

Claims that do not hold up

  • "Blue light is what causes pattern discomfort." The stimulus is spatial contrast, not a narrow band of the spectrum. A grey-and-black grating with no blue content produces the effect.
  • "Any tint helps everyone equally." Responses are individual. A tint that one wearer finds calming another finds useless, which is the reason precision systems exist at all.
  • "Tinted lenses cure migraine." They do not. Reducing one visual trigger is not the same as altering the underlying disorder, and no lens changes the neurological condition.
  • "Discomfort from stripes means the prescription is wrong." It can coexist with a refractive error, but pattern glare occurs in fully corrected eyes. Refracting again will not remove a cortical response.
  • "Anti-reflective coating will fix it." AR coating removes reflections and improves contrast transmission. Better contrast is the opposite of what a pattern-glare sufferer needs from the pattern itself, though the coating genuinely helps with night lights and screen reflections.

When to see someone rather than buy a tint

A visual trigger that has just appeared, aura that is new or changing, visual loss, double vision, or a headache pattern that has altered — particularly after the age of fifty — needs assessment rather than a filter. Flashing lights, a spreading blind spot, or a curtain across the vision are reasons for urgent review, not for shopping.

For long-standing pattern discomfort with a stable eye examination, the sensible steps are ordinary: reduce the contrast of the stimulus where you can, sit further from the screen so the stripe pattern subtends a smaller angle, use matte rather than glossy surfaces, and if you want to try a tint, try a light one and judge it on a real task over a week. An optometrist can measure pattern glare and discuss whether a precision tint is worth pursuing; that conversation is a better use of an appointment than a guess about which colour is fashionable.
