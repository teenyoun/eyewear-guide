Simultaneous Vision: How a Multifocal Contact Lens Splits the Light

November 25, 2026 · 9 min read

Two images at once, and no way to choose

A progressive spectacle lens works by geometry: the eye looks through a different part of the lens for distance and for near, and the lens designer's job is to make the transition gradual. A multifocal contact lens has no such option. It sits on the cornea and moves with the eye, so every gaze passes through the same optical zone. Whatever the lens does, it does everywhere at once.

That single constraint explains almost everything wearers find surprising about multifocal contacts: why the letter chart can read 20/20 and driving at night still feels wrong, why the near vision seems to come and go with the light, and why the first week is a negotiation rather than an adaptation.

Principle: simultaneous vision

The design strategy is called simultaneous vision. Instead of moving the optical zone, the lens divides the pupil into regions of different power, so that light from a distant object and light from a near object are both brought to focus on the retina at the same time. Two families do this differently.

  • Concentric zone designs alternate rings of distance and near power, centre-distance or centre-near depending on the design. The eye receives a sharp image through the rings of the correct power and a defocused image through the others.
  • Aspheric designs change power continuously across the optic zone, typically with the centre carrying the distance correction and the periphery progressively adding plus, or the reverse. There are no discrete rings, so the defocus is distributed rather than segmented.

In both cases the retina receives a superposition: one in-focus image and one or more out-of-focus copies. The visual system has to suppress the unwanted image. That suppression is a learned, attention-dependent process, which is why performance improves over days and why it degrades when you are tired, in poor light, or doing something unfamiliar.

What the physics costs you

The unwanted image does not disappear; it becomes background. When a bright point source is superimposed on its own defocused copy, the result is a halo. The larger the add power, the further apart the two foci, and the more visible the halo. Add powers for presbyopic designs are typically offered from about +1.00 D to +2.50 D, and the difference between the low and high end of that range is mostly a difference in how much light is wasted on the wrong image.

Pupil size decides how much of the design you are actually using. A pupil of roughly 3 mm in bright light samples a small central disc of the optic zone; a pupil of 5 to 6 mm in dim light samples much more of it, including the outer rings. In a concentric design this changes the proportion of light going to near and distance as the light level changes — which is why some wearers report that near vision is good in a shop and poor at a dimly lit restaurant table, while others report the opposite depending on whether their design is centre-near or centre-distance.

Contrast sensitivity is the other casualty. Splitting light between two images reduces the contrast of the one you are trying to use, even when its focus is perfect. That is the mechanism behind the familiar complaint that everything is "technically clear but somehow flat", and it is worst exactly where contrast matters most: grey road markings at dusk, a face in shadow, small print on a matte label.

Why the prescription is only half the fit

A multifocal contact lens is fitted by over-refraction: the lens is placed, the residual error is measured on top of it, and the pair is adjusted. Three variables interact, and changing one changes the others.

  • Add power. Too little leaves near vision short; too much adds halo and reduces contrast at distance.
  • Centration. A concentric design assumes the rings sit around the pupil. A lens that rides high or decentres laterally puts the near zone off-axis, and the wearer learns to tilt the head to find it.
  • Binocular balance. Many practitioners deliberately bias the two eyes — a little more distance emphasis on the dominant eye, a little more near on the other — which is a controlled version of monovision. It works well when it is subtle and badly when it is not.

This is why a multifocal contact trial is a process with a review appointment rather than a single measurement. The lens that reads best on a chart in a bright room is not necessarily the lens that works at a desk at four in the afternoon.

Common misconceptions

"Multifocal contacts are progressive lenses in a different package." They are not. A progressive lens gives one sharp image and you point your eye at the zone you need. A multifocal contact gives two superimposed images and asks the brain to discard one. The adaptation is neural, not optical.

"If I can read the 20/20 line, the fit is right." High-contrast black letters on a white chart are the easiest target the visual system ever meets. They can be read through a design that is poor for driving, poor for stairs, and poor for reading a menu in a dark room.

"A stronger add will fix my near vision." Beyond the point where the near image is legible, extra add buys more halo and less contrast, and often reduces distance clarity enough that the wearer gives up on the lenses entirely.

"Multifocal contacts mean I never need glasses again." Most wearers keep a pair of spectacles for prolonged near work, for night driving, or for the days when their eyes are dry. That is a normal outcome, not a failure of the fit.

Where spectacles still win

For sustained reading, a pair of single-vision reading glasses or a well-fitted progressive gives a sharper, higher-contrast image than any simultaneous-vision lens, because no light is diverted to a second image. For night driving, the same argument applies with more force. Multifocal contacts are strongest for people who need to be free of spectacles during the day — sport, work, social settings — and who are willing to accept a small, permanent compromise in contrast in exchange.

When to stop and reassess

If distance vision is still fluctuating after two weeks of daily wear, if halos are worsening rather than settling, if the lenses are uncomfortable rather than merely optically odd, or if you have redness, pain or a change in vision that persists after removal, stop wearing them and be seen. Those are not adaptation issues. Discomfort and redness in particular are fit and physiology questions, and no amount of over-refraction resolves them.
