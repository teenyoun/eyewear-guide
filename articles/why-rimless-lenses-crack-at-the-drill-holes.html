Why Rimless Lenses Crack at the Drill Holes

October 23, 2026 · 8 min read

A hole is not a neutral feature

Rimless glasses look like the most honest design in optics: no frame between you and the world, just two lenses joined by a bridge and two temples. They also fail in a very specific place, and it is almost always the same place. The lens cracks from a drill hole outward, usually months after fitting, often without a fall or an impact to explain it.

The failure is not bad luck. A hole in a stiff, brittle material concentrates stress, and the geometry of a rimless mount puts that concentrated stress exactly where the lens is thinnest and most highly loaded. Understanding the mechanics explains why material choice, hole placement and screw torque matter more here than in any other frame type.

The mechanics: stress concentration

Take a flat plate under tension and cut a circular hole in it. The material next to the hole carries more load than the material further away, because the hole removes the material that would have shared it. For a circular hole in a wide plate, the classical stress concentration factor is about 3: the peak stress at the hole edge is roughly three times the nominal stress in the plate.

Three is an approximation that assumes an ideal plate, and real lenses are worse in several ways. The plate is not infinite, so the hole sits close to an edge where stress is already elevated. The load is not a clean tension: a rimless mount pulls at an angle, and every handling event adds a bending moment. And the material is viscoelastic polymer, so a load held for months produces creep around the hole rather than a clean elastic response.

Add a micro-crack at the drill edge, which is where drilling leaves its worst surface, and you have a slow crack-growth problem rather than a strength problem. That is why the crack appears later, and why it often starts on the nasal side where the lens is thinnest.

Where the choice is made for you

  • Material. Polycarbonate and Trivex resist crack growth far better than the brittle high-index materials, because toughness (resistance to crack propagation) is a different property from hardness. A high-index lens in a rimless mount is a legitimate choice for thickness, and it is the choice most likely to chip at the hole.
  • Edge thickness at the hole. The hole has to sit in material, not at the bevel. On a high minus lens the temporal edge is thick and the nasal edge thin, so the nasal hole has far less material around it.
  • Hole size and finish. A drilled hole is a machined surface with tool marks. Manufacturers who drill slowly, with a supported lens and a sharp bit, produce fewer initiation sites.
  • Mount design. A bushing or a sleeve spreads the screw's load over more material. A screw tightened directly into polymer threads cuts its own stress riser.

Why the nasal side usually goes first

On a minus lens the edge is thinnest nasally and thickest temporally, because the lens is decentred inward to place the optical centre over the pupil. The nasal drill hole therefore sits in the least material, where the same screw load is carried by a smaller cross-section. Add the fact that the nasal side is where the frame flexes most when the glasses are put on one-handed, and the pattern explains itself. On a plus lens the geometry reverses and the temporal hole becomes the vulnerable one, which is why the first question after a crack should be "which power, which side".

One more question is worth asking at the counter: whether the lens will be edged with a safety bevel around the drill point. A dressed edge removes the micro-cracks left by the bit, and it is the cheapest intervention available against a crack that starts six months later.

Misconceptions

  • "Rimless is the fragile option, so cracks are expected." Rimless is thinner and more exposed, but a well-made pair does not crack spontaneously. Spontaneous cracking points to a material or mounting decision, not to the category.
  • "Tightening the screw more will stop the lens moving." It usually stops the movement by starting a crack. The screw needs to be snug, with the bushing doing the work.
  • "Any lens can be drilled." Any lens can be drilled. Not every lens survives being drilled and then loaded for two years.
  • "A crack from the hole is a manufacturing defect and should be replaced free." Sometimes it is. Often it is a combination of material, thickness and mount that was chosen at the ordering stage, which is why the conversation is worth having before the lenses are cut rather than after.

What to ask for, and when to go back

If you are choosing rimless, ask which material is being used and why, and ask whether the lens is thick enough at the drill points for the frame you picked. If you already own a pair, check the holes periodically: a hairline running outward from a hole will not heal, and it will eventually relieve itself at the worst moment. Take the glasses back at the first sign rather than the second; a lens that is still intact can sometimes be re-edged or remounted, while a lens that has cracked through cannot.

Any crack that appears within days of a fitting, any crack on both lenses in the same position, or any crack without an impact should be shown to the optician who supplied them. That pattern is diagnostic, and it usually means a remake rather than a repair.
