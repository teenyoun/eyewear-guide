From Blank to Frame: How a Prescription Lens Is Actually Made

October 16, 2026 · 9 min read

Your prescription does not exist in a warehouse

Consider what a finished lens order actually specifies: a sphere power, a cylinder power, an axis angle, an addition, for two eyes, in a chosen material and cut to a particular frame shape and pupillary distance. The number of combinations runs into the millions before you even consider the frame. It is not possible to manufacture a moulded lens for each one and keep it on a shelf, so the industry does something else.

It makes semi-finished blanks. These are lens-shaped pieces of the chosen material with a fully finished front surface and a deliberate surplus of material on the back. The blank family fixes the front curve, which is why choosing a material also fixes a set of available base curves. Your prescription is then produced by removing material from the back surface until the combination of front curve and back curve gives the required power. That is the whole logic of prescription lens manufacturing: the front comes from a factory, the back is made for you.

Surfacing: three stages, each finer than the last

The back surface is created in sequence, and each stage exists because the one before it leaves marks it cannot remove itself.

  • Generating. A computer-controlled cutter with a diamond tool removes the bulk of the surplus material in one pass, producing a curve that is close to the required shape. The surface it leaves is visibly frosted, because the tooling marks scatter light.
  • Fining. Progressively finer abrasive suspensions are worked across the surface to remove the generating marks. The lens is still translucent rather than clear at this point.
  • Polishing. A soft pad with a polishing slurry takes the final fraction of material off until the surface is optically clear and specular.

The total depth of material removed is measured in fractions of a millimetre, but the tolerance on the finished curve is far tighter than that, because a small error in curvature is a measurable error in power. Free-form designs take the same sequence further, using a computer-controlled lathe to cut a back surface that is not a simple sphere, which is how a design can vary power across the lens rather than in a smooth fixed progression.

Coating happens in a vacuum, a fraction of a wavelength at a time

Anti-reflective coatings are not paints and they are not dips. They are built as a stack of extremely thin layers in a vacuum chamber, deposited by evaporation or sputtering onto a surface that has to be meticulously clean before it goes in. Each layer is only a fraction of a wavelength of visible light thick, and the layers work by interference: light reflected from the different interfaces cancels out, which is why the reflection disappears instead of merely being dimmed.

That interference is also why a coated lens gives away its presence with a faint colour. The residual reflection that survives the stack has a hue — typically a green sheen, sometimes purple or blue — and that hue is a direct readout of whether the layer thicknesses are on target. The other layers usually applied around it follow a fixed order: a hard coat to resist abrasion, then the anti-reflective stack, then a hydrophobic and oleophobic top layer so the finished surface repels water and skin oils.

Edging: cutting a round lens down to a frame

A surfaced lens is still an oversized round or nearly round disc. The last manufacturing step is to cut it to the shape of the frame and give it an edge that will stay in place.

A tracer records the inside of the frame rim, including the groove that many metal and acetate frames use. The edger then cuts the lens outline and grinds a bevel, an angled edge that seats into that groove, so the lens is held by its own geometry as well as by the frame. The edge profile depends on the frame type: a rimless frame takes a flat edge, with holes drilled for the mountings, and a nylon-cord frame takes a groove cut around the lens that the cord sits in.

Edging is also where a constraint becomes visible that the wearer never sees. Cutting the lens down moves its optical centre relative to the frame, and the lens has to be large enough that the centre can sit in front of your pupil in the finished position. That is what sets the minimum size of blank a given frame and prescription can be cut from, and it is a large part of why a frame shape that looks generous on a display stand can be unsuitable for a strong prescription, especially a high cylinder.

Verification, and what the marks on a new lens mean

A finished lens is expected to meet the standard for uncut finished spectacle lenses, ISO 8980-1, which specifies requirements for single-vision and multifocal lenses including back vertex power, cylinder axis direction, addition power, prismatic power and its base orientation, along with dimensional requirements. In practice the checking is done with a lensometer, which measures the finished power of the lens rather than trusting the manufacturing record.

If you look closely at a new progressive lens before it is cleaned, you may find faint marks: a small cross, a tiny number for the addition, and letters or symbols identifying the design. These are fitting marks used by whoever assembled the glasses, and cleaning removes them. What stays permanently on the lens is a set of very faint laser-engraved reference marks, invisible in normal wear, which identify the design for future verification.

Common misconceptions

  • "My lens is cut from a flat sheet of plastic to my prescription." It starts as a moulded semi-finished blank with a finished front curve. Only the back surface is generated, fined and polished to your prescription.
  • "Anti-reflective coating is sprayed or dipped onto the finished lens." It is deposited layer by layer in a vacuum, each layer thinner than a wavelength of light, which is precisely why it works at all. The faint green sheen on a good coat is not decoration; it is the interference pattern of the residual reflection.
  • "All labs make lenses to roughly the same quality." The sequence of steps is standard, but the precision of the finished surface, the number and control of the coating layers, and how thoroughly the result is checked are not. That is where the range in the market lives.
  • "The marks on a brand-new lens are a manufacturing defect." They are the fitting marks and design engravings described above. A visibly hazy lens, or a coating with patchy colour across it, would be a defect.

When it is worth asking for a check

If new glasses feel subtly wrong, it helps to separate three questions: is the power correct, is the fitting correct, and is the lens itself sound? A lensometer answers the first. The second is about optical centre position, fitting height and how the frame sits on your face. The third is a matter of the surface and the coating. Naming which of the three seems wrong makes the conversation far more productive than reporting that the glasses feel off.
