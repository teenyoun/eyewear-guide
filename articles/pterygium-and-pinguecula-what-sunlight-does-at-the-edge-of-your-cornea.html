Pterygium and Pinguecula: What Sunlight Does at the Edge of Your Cornea

November 22, 2026 · 9 min read

The cornea stays clear because of a border it cannot repair twice

The front of your eye is two different tissues meeting at a visible seam. The cornea is transparent and has no blood vessels in it, which is exactly why it can be an optical surface: light passes through without being scattered by vessels or pigment. Around it sits the conjunctiva, a thin vascular membrane over the white of the eye. Their junction is the limbus, and it is more than an anatomical line: it holds a reservoir of stem cells that continually replace the corneal surface. While that reservoir works, the cornea renews itself as clear tissue rather than scar.

Ultraviolet light attacks precisely that reservoir. UV-B, the band from roughly 280 to 315 nanometres, is absorbed in the front few cell layers rather than passing deeper, so the cornea, conjunctiva and limbus take the dose. What follows is a chronic wound-repair loop: DNA damage and oxidative stress in limbal cells, release of inflammatory signals and matrix metalloproteinases — enzymes that digest collagen — and a breakdown of the barrier that keeps conjunctival tissue off the cornea. Sunlight also produces solar elastosis, degeneration of elastic fibres in the conjunctival stroma that gives sun-damaged tissue its yellowish, thickened look.

Two named lesions come out of that loop, and they are stages of the same process rather than unrelated conditions.

Two lesions, one mechanism

A pinguecula is a yellowish, slightly raised patch in the conjunctiva next to the limbus, most often nasal. It does not cross onto the cornea. It is degenerative rather than neoplastic: altered collagen, elastosis, and often a dulled tear film over the top. Many people have one and never notice; others get intermittent irritation or a cosmetic concern.

A pterygium goes further. A wedge of fibrovascular tissue crosses the limbus and grows onto the corneal surface, usually from the nasal side, sometimes from both sides in advanced cases. Because it brings blood vessels and scar tissue onto a surface that was designed to be clear, it can do three optical things: drag on the cornea and induce irregular astigmatism, disrupt the tear film so the surface optics become uneven, and — if it reaches far enough — physically encroach on the visual axis. This is the lesion called surfer's eye — a nickname that tracks cumulative outdoor exposure.

Why the nasal side, of all places

The natural assumption is that the part of the eye facing the sun gets hit hardest. The geometry is worse than that. Light arriving from the temporal side at an oblique angle is refracted by the cornea's own curvature and converges on the nasal limbus — described in the ophthalmic literature as peripheral light focusing. The structure that gives you a clear corneal image also concentrates oblique sunlight onto one small patch of stem cells. Hence the nasal predominance, and a practical lesson: the light that matters most is not the light coming straight at you, but the light from the side, behind and above, which a flat-fronted lens does not intercept.

Reflected light raises the dose further. Water, sand, snow, pale concrete and car bonnets all bounce a meaningful fraction of incident UV upward and sideways into the eye. That is why a day on a boat can produce more ocular UV exposure than a day standing in a car park, at the same latitude and the same UV index.

What this means for choosing sunwear

Three properties matter, and only one of them is printed on the lens.

  • UV transmittance, across both bands. Protection means UV-A as well as UV-B, so the requirement is a transmittance limit measured out to 400 nanometres, not a claim about darkness. EN ISO 12312-1 sets the UV requirements in Europe; ANSI Z80.3 is the corresponding US sunglass standard.
  • Coverage and geometry. A lens with a large vertical depth, a wrapped or angled front, and a frame that reaches toward the temple intercepts oblique light before corneal refraction can redirect it. This is where most cheap sunglasses and most fashion sunglasses fail, regardless of their UV label.
  • Category, chosen for the conditions. The category number from 0 to 4 describes visible-light transmittance, not UV protection. A category 2 lens can be perfectly UV-blocking; a category 4 lens that is UV-transparent is still useless for eye health, and illegal to drive in besides.

The "UV400" label deserves care. It is an industry convention meaning transmission is held very low out to 400 nm, the upper edge of UV-A. It is not a term defined in EN ISO 12312-1, so a UV400 sticker states what the seller claims rather than which test was run. Buy where a standard number or test report is given.

The misconceptions that matter clinically

The most common one is darker means safer. Tint density and UV filtration are separate design decisions, and a dark lens with a weak UV filter is the worst combination available, because the pupil opens behind it and the retina is exposed to a wider beam of whatever UV the lens does pass. Photochromic and polarised lenses have their own characteristics, but neither property implies UV protection by itself. Polarisation removes reflected glare; it does not remove UV.

A second is "this only happens to farmers and surfers in the tropics". Latitude and occupation shift the odds, but the mechanism is cumulative dose, and dose includes altitude, reflective surfaces, time spent outdoors at midday, and years. Someone who works indoors but spends weekends on water with no side coverage is running the same arithmetic at a different rate.

A third is "removing it fixes it for good". Pterygium surgery is generally effective, but recurrence is well recognised and more likely in younger patients and in people who return to the same exposure unprotected. Surgery removes the tissue; it does not restore the limbal barrier or change the dose. That is why the boring advice about sunwear afterwards should be taken literally.

When to have it looked at

Most pingueculas are watched, not treated. A pterygium needs assessment when it is growing, when it is repeatedly inflamed, when it is inducing astigmatism, or when it approaches the visual axis. Sudden redness, pain, a rapid change in size or appearance, or any loss of vision are reasons to be seen promptly rather than to wait for a routine check — a pterygium is common benign tissue, but not everything on the surface of the eye is, and that distinction is a clinical one.

There is a refraction angle worth knowing too. If your astigmatism has changed on the same axis across two prescriptions and you have a lesion near the limbus, mention it to your optometrist; a corneal surface being pulled by scar tissue produces exactly that pattern, and a new pair of lenses will only partially compensate for it. If you are being fitted with contact lenses, say so as well — a raised pinguecula can interfere with how a soft lens sits and how comfortable it stays by the end of the day. In both cases the useful next step is a slit-lamp look, not a new pair of sunglasses.
