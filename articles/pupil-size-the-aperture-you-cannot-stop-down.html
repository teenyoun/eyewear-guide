Pupil Size: The Aperture You Cannot Stop Down

November 20, 2026 · 9 min read

The aperture you cannot adjust on purpose

Every camera lens has an iris, and so does your eye, but yours is not under voluntary control. Pupil diameter is set by the balance between the sphincter and dilator muscles of the iris, driven by light level, by accommodation and by arousal. That one diameter decides three things at once: how much light reaches the retina, how much depth of field the eye has, and how much of the cornea's and crystalline lens's optical error is exposed. The trade-off between those three explains a great deal of what people call night vision problems.

The numbers

In bright daylight a young adult pupil is roughly 2 to 4 mm across. In full dark it dilates to roughly 4 to 8 mm, with wide individual variation. Maximum pupil diameter falls steadily with age: the same dark-adapted eye that reaches 7 mm at twenty may reach only 5 mm at seventy, a change known as senile miosis. That narrowing is one reason older drivers complain less about glare from oncoming headlights than younger ones, and also one reason they see less well in the dark.

Aberration gets worse as the aperture opens

The eye is not a perfect optical system. Its surfaces carry spherical aberration, coma and oblique astigmatism that no spectacle prescription corrects, because a prescription corrects only defocus and regular cylinder. Higher-order aberrations grow steeply with pupil diameter, and their effect on the retinal image scales with the area of the pupil being used. At 2 mm the eye is close to diffraction-limited. At 6 or 7 mm the outer zones of the cornea and crystalline lens contribute light that lands in the wrong place, and the result is halos around lights, starbursts, and a loss of contrast that a wearer often describes as the prescription being wrong at night.

Diffraction gets better as the aperture opens

The opposite pressure is diffraction. A circular aperture of diameter D spreads a point of light into a disc whose angular radius is about 1.22 wavelengths divided by D. At a wavelength of 555 nanometres, a 3 mm pupil gives an angular radius of roughly 0.78 arcminutes, a 2 mm pupil about 1.17 arcminutes, and a 7 mm pupil about 0.33 arcminutes. Below about 2 mm the penalty becomes large, which is why looking through a pinhole sharpens an aberrated eye and blurs a perfect one.

Where the two curves cross

Diffraction blur falls as the pupil widens, aberration blur rises, and the sum has a minimum. For the human eye that minimum usually sits between about 2.5 and 3 mm, roughly the daytime pupil size in moderate indoor light. That is not a coincidence: the eye's optics are close to optimal at the aperture it uses most. It also explains a familiar observation, that a patient with a modest refractive error often reads better in a normally lit room than in a dim one, and that the same patient's complaints are worst at night.

Why a perfect prescription still leaves night vision imperfect

A spectacle lens corrects defocus and regular astigmatism at the plane of the pupil. It cannot remove the aberration of the eye's own surfaces, and it cannot stop the pupil from opening. A patient refracted to 20/20 in the consulting room can therefore still see halos at night, particularly after refractive surgery, where the treated optical zone may be smaller than a dark-adapted pupil. That combination, a wide pupil and a small optical zone, is the classic cause of night-time starbursts after laser surgery, and it is measured rather than guessed at.

Myth: bigger pupils mean better night vision

A larger pupil admits more light, and more light does help detection in the dark. But acuity and contrast discrimination depend on image quality as well as on light, and beyond about 4 mm the added light arrives with more aberration than the retina can use. This is why a 7 mm dark-adapted pupil does not simply outperform a 5 mm one, and why pupil-dilating drops do not improve night vision: they raise retinal illuminance while degrading the image.

Myth: 20/20 means your eyes work in all conditions

Visual acuity is measured under photopic conditions, with a pupil of roughly 2 to 4 mm and high-contrast letters. It says nothing about mesopic or scotopic performance, nothing about glare, and nothing about aberrations that appear only at a wide aperture. Two people with identical prescriptions can have very different night vision, and the difference lies mostly in their optics and their pupils rather than in their refraction.

Myth: drops that shrink the pupil are a vision correction

Miotic drops do reduce aberrations by narrowing the aperture, and they are used in specific clinical situations. They are not a correction. They also reduce retinal illuminance, narrow the field and blur the periphery, and they carry side effects. Anyone whose night vision is limited by a wide pupil should have that assessed properly rather than have the aperture changed as a workaround.

Practical consequences

If your night vision is poor despite a recent prescription, say so explicitly and ask whether your pupil size and higher-order aberration were considered. If you are choosing a frame for a strong prescription, remember that a wider lens exposes more peripheral aberration and more prism, so a smaller frame often performs better after dark. And if you notice halos together with headache, eye pain, or a pupil that is newly unequal, that is a medical question rather than an optical one: a pupil difference larger than about a millimetre, or one that has changed recently, should be examined.

The pupil is the one aperture in your visual system that adapts without asking you. Everything you notice about glare, halos and dim-light sharpness is a negotiation between that aperture and the imperfections behind it.
