ANSI Z80.3: What the US Sunglass Standard Actually Requires

November 01, 2026 · 9 min read

A standard is a set of limits, not a promise

A pair of sunglasses is a filter in a mechanical housing, and the only thing separating a useful filter from a harmful one is what it does at wavelengths you cannot see. That is the whole reason a sunglass standard exists. The failure mode it is written to prevent is not "not dark enough". It is the pupil-dilation trap: a dark lens makes the pupil open, and if that same lens lets ultraviolet through, the retina receives more UV than it would with no sunglasses at all. Every transmittance limit in ANSI Z80.3 exists to close that trap.

This matters because the label on the arm is usually the only information a buyer gets. A sticker that says a lens "meets ANSI Z80.3" is a claim about a specific set of measurements, taken under specific conditions, against a specific edition of the standard.

The three quantities the standard constrains

Z80.3 works with three spectral quantities, and they are not interchangeable:

  • Luminous transmittance, usually written τV, is the fraction of visible light (roughly 380–780 nm) a lens passes, weighted to the eye's photopic sensitivity curve. This is what people mean by "how dark" a lens is.
  • UVB, 280–315 nm, is the band most strongly associated with photokeratitis — the sunburned-cornea effect that shows up after a day on snow or water.
  • UVA, 315–380 nm, passes deeper into the eye and is the band that matters for cumulative lenticular exposure.

In the 2001 edition of Z80.3, mean UVB transmittance must not exceed 1 per cent, and mean UVA transmittance must not exceed 0.3 times the luminous transmittance of the same lens. Later editions have been revised, so the precise wording and the treatment of the 380–400 nm region should be checked against the edition a manufacturer cites rather than assumed.

Why UVA is a ratio and UVB is a flat number

This is the part of the standard that repays a second look, because it encodes an optical argument. Retinal UV dose is not set by the lens alone; it is set by the lens multiplied by pupil area, and pupil area follows τV. A very dark lens opens the pupil wide, so a fixed absolute UVA allowance would be far more dangerous in a category 4 lens than in a lightly tinted one. Expressing the UVA limit as a fraction of visible transmittance keeps the UV-to-visible ratio roughly constant, so the eye never receives proportionally more UV simply because the lens is darker.

The arithmetic consequence is worth spelling out. A lens transmitting 15 per cent of visible light may transmit at most 4.5 per cent UVA. A lens transmitting 60 per cent may transmit up to 18 per cent UVA and still be within the same limit. Both pass; the light lens is passing four times as much UVA in absolute terms. That is defensible for a fashion tint worn indoors, and it is the reason a light lens is not automatically the "safer" choice for a day of sailing.

Categories are use classes, not a darkness ranking

Z80.3 sorts lenses into use categories tied to τV — cosmetic, general purpose, and special purpose — rather than the numbered 0 to 4 filter scale used in Europe under ISO 12312-1. The two systems answer different questions. A US "general purpose" lens is a statement about intended use; a European "category 3" is a transmittance band with an explicit driving restriction attached. A pair can be perfectly legal in one market and mislabelled in the other, and neither label states the transmittance figure an optician would measure.

The standard also constrains how far a lens may shift the appearance of signal lights. A tint that meets the UV limits but mangles the red of a brake light or the green of a signal is within the letter of a transmittance table and outside the point of it. Deeply saturated tints — strong blue, strong green — are the usual offenders, and they are a poor choice for driving regardless of category.

What the sticker does not cover

Z80.3 is a transmittance and labelling standard. It says nothing useful about:

  • Impact. That is ANSI Z87.1 for occupational eyewear and ASTM F803 for racket sports. A fashion sunglass that meets Z80.3 has not been drop-tested.
  • Optical accuracy beyond basic tolerances. Power error, prism from poor decentration, and the axis of a prescription sunglass are separate questions, governed by lens standards such as ISO 8980.
  • Blue light. There is no Z80.3 test for "blocks 92 per cent of blue light". Such figures come from the manufacturer's own spectrophotometer and are usually quoted at a single wavelength rather than weighted to a hazard function.
  • Fit. Peripheral UV enters around the temporal edge of a lens. A small lens sitting far from the face can leave more of the eye exposed than its transmittance figure suggests.

Five claims that do not mean what they appear to mean

"Darker means more protection." Darkness is comfort. UV absorption is a separate property, and the two are only loosely correlated in cheap lenses.

"UV400 means it was tested to ANSI." UV400 describes a cut-off at 400 nm. The Z80.3 UVA window in the 2001 edition ends at 380 nm. A lens can honestly claim UV400 and still sit outside the standard's UVA definition; the two statements are not the same test.

"Polarised means UV-blocking." Polarisation removes light by orientation, not by wavelength. A polarising film can be UV-absorbing, but the polarising property itself does nothing for UV.

"Category 4 is the strongest, so it is the best." Category 4 transmits so little light that it is unsuitable for driving at any time, and it is the category most often sold to people who actually needed category 2 or 3.

"Expensive means certified." Price is not a test method. Independent testing has repeatedly found no reliable relationship between price and UV performance.

When the label is not enough

Take the sunglasses to an optician if any of the following applies: the lenses are prescription, because a tint magnifies every error in centration and axis; the two eyes need very different powers, because a tinted lens hides the thickness and magnification asymmetry that causes trouble; you have had cataract surgery or you are photophobic, because the appropriate transmittance is a clinical judgement rather than a shelf decision; you drive at night or in rain, where a category that feels comfortable by day is unsafe; or you have a colour vision deficiency, because a tint that separates red from green for most people can collapse the distinction for you.

For everyday sun exposure, polarized UV400 sunglasses at a modest price will usually meet the UV limits, because the UV absorber is a bulk additive in the lens material rather than a coating that can be skipped. What the price buys is optical quality, frame durability and consistency between pairs — not the transmittance table.
