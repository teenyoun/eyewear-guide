The Duochrome Test: Why Red and Green Reveal Your Focus

November 06, 2026 · 8 min read

Near the end of a routine refraction, the examiner often swaps a white chart for one printed with black letters on a red half and a green half, and asks a question that sounds trivial: on which side are the letters darker and clearer? The answer can move the final sphere by a quarter of a dioptre. What makes the question work is that the test does not rely on the patient's judgement of colour at all. It exploits a defect in the eye that cannot be turned off.

Chromatic aberration is not a lens fault, it is physics

Any refracting material bends short wavelengths more strongly than long ones. Glass and plastic spectacle lenses do this, which is why a high-power lens shows coloured fringes at the edge. The eye does the same thing with its own optics, and the amount is not small. The longitudinal chromatic aberration of the human eye is typically quoted as roughly one to two dioptres across the visible spectrum, which means the eye cannot bring all colours to a focus at the same plane.

Green light, around 535 nanometres, is bent more than red light, around 620 nanometres. So the green focus sits in front of the red focus, closer to the lens. For an eye looking at a white target, both ends of that interval are slightly out of focus and the retina sits somewhere between them. The interval is only a fraction of a dioptre wide for those two specific wavelengths, which is exactly what makes it useful as a fine adjustment rather than a coarse one.

What the test actually asks the eye to do

Put a target on a green background and another on a red background, matched for luminance, and the eye is being offered two images whose best focus planes are separated by that chromatic interval. Whichever target lands closer to the retina will look darker and sharper. The patient is not comparing colours; they are reporting which of two images is nearer to being in focus. That is why the test works even for people who describe the two halves as equally coloured.

Now the diagnostic logic, which is the part most people get backwards:

  • Green clearer means the green focus is nearer the retina. Since green already focuses in front of red, the retinal plane must be sitting forward of where it should be, which is the situation produced by too much minus. The correction is to reduce minus or add plus.
  • Red clearer means the red focus is nearer the retina, so the retinal plane has moved back. That is the signature of too little minus, and the correction is to add minus.

A neat way to remember it is that the test is not measuring the eye's colour vision. It is using the eye's own dispersion as a ruler with two marks a known distance apart.

Why it is a quarter-dioptre instrument

The chromatic interval between the two targets is small, so the test only becomes informative when the two images are otherwise nearly balanced. That is why it is used at the end of refraction, after sphere and cylinder have been settled by other means, and why each step is a quarter dioptre. It is a tie-breaker, not a measurement. Used early, or used to chase a large error, it produces confident answers about nothing.

The test also depends on the two halves being matched in luminance and on the room lighting being stable. A chart that is brighter on one side, or a display whose white point drifts, will bias the answer. This is a real limitation in practices that have moved from printed charts to screens, and it is one reason some examiners still keep the printed version.

Where patients and articles go wrong

  • "It tests for colour blindness." It tests spherical focus. A red-green chart looks like an Ishihara plate only in its colour scheme; the task and the interpretation are unrelated.
  • "If both sides look the same, my eyes are perfect." Equal clarity means the spherical endpoint is balanced to within roughly a quarter dioptre at that moment. It says nothing about cylinder, about binocular balance, or about how the eye behaves at a different pupil size.
  • "Green means I need stronger glasses." Green means less minus, or more plus. The intuition that "clearer" must mean "more correction" is the most common misreading of the test.
  • "The result is precise." It is a subjective comparison of two blurred images and it is sensitive to fatigue, to the patient's criterion for "clearer", and to how the question is phrased. Its value comes from being repeatable within a familiar routine, not from being absolute.

Why a strong tint changes the rules

Anything that filters the spectrum before it reaches the eye changes the balance the test depends on. A dense sunglasses tint, a strongly selective blue-blocking coating, or a coloured contact lens all narrow the spectral band reaching the retina, and a narrow band has less chromatic spread to exploit. Examiners generally refract without those filters in place for exactly this reason, and a patient who keeps sunglasses on during a refraction is not being careful, they are removing the signal.

Congenital colour vision deficiency is a separate issue and a more nuanced one. The duochrome depends on relative brightness and focus of two coloured patches, not on hue discrimination, so many people with red-green deficiency still give usable answers. It is not automatically invalid, but a clinician who knows the patient's colour vision status will weight the response accordingly.

What the test does not replace

Sphere is one number on a prescription with three or four. The duochrome does not refine the cylinder axis, does not check that the two eyes work together, and does not verify that the lens you eventually receive was made to that specification. Those come from cylinder refinement, binocular balance, and a lensmeter check of the finished pair respectively.

If a pair of glasses made from a duochrome-balanced prescription still feels wrong, the useful questions are about axis, about optical centre placement, about vertex distance if the frame sits differently from the trial frame, and about how long the wearer has persisted. Those are fabrication and fitting questions, and they are answerable by measurement rather than by another subjective comparison.

When to raise it with your optometrist

Mention it if you consistently preferred one side of the chart across several visits, if your new glasses are clear in the test room and strained everywhere else, or if the prescription changed by a quarter dioptre and the difference feels larger than that. Those are reasonable observations to bring to an examination. Interpreting them is a clinical task, and a single test performed at home with a printed chart and no control of luminance will not reproduce the conditions the test was designed for.
