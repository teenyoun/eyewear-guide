Contrast Sensitivity: Why You Can Pass 20/20 and Still Not See Well

October 07, 2026 · 9 min read

Two different things are being measured

A visual acuity chart measures one very specific ability: resolving high-contrast detail at a small size. Black letters on a white background, sitting at the threshold of being too small to read. When someone says "I have 20/20 vision," that is the only thing that number certifies.

Everyday seeing is almost never like that. Real scenes are full of low-contrast detail — a grey car against grey asphalt in the rain, a step edge in dim stairwells, a face across a dim restaurant, dark text on a dark screen, a curb at dusk. Perceiving those depends on a separate measurement called contrast sensitivity, and the two do not move together.

This is why "but I passed the test" and "but I still can't see properly" can both be true at once. They are answers to different questions.

The principle layer: contrast sensitivity is a curve, not a number

Contrast sensitivity is the ability to detect a pattern as its contrast is reduced. It is usually measured with gratings — alternating light and dark bars — or with letters that become progressively fainter.

The critical concept is spatial frequency, measured in cycles per degree of visual angle. Cycles per degree describes how tightly packed the stripes are within your field of view. Fine detail sits at high spatial frequency; broad, gradual changes sit at low spatial frequency.

Plot sensitivity against spatial frequency and you get the contrast sensitivity function, an inverted U. In normal young adults it peaks in the middle of the range — broadly around 3 to 6 cycles per degree — and falls away at both ends. Published reference intervals put average sensitivity at roughly 2.1 log CS near that peak, dropping towards the high-frequency end of the curve. In plain terms: we are tuned to see medium-sized detail, and we are worse at both the very fine and the very gradual.

Two consequences follow, and both are practical:

  • Acuity tests probe the right-hand tail of the curve. That is why a person can have intact 20/20 acuity and still have depressed contrast sensitivity.
  • Contrast sensitivity is usually measured under low contrast, and the measurement is expressed as log units — on the Pelli-Robson chart, for example. Lower log CS means more contrast is needed before a pattern becomes visible.

What actually degrades it

Contrast sensitivity is a system-level property. It depends on the optics in front of the retina, the retina itself, and the neural processing behind it, so it can be reduced by things that have nothing to do with refractive error:

  • Optical scatter in the eye media. Any haze in the cornea or crystalline lens spreads light sideways and flattens the image before it ever reaches the retina. This is one reason early lens changes can cost contrast sensitivity before they cost acuity.
  • Uncorrected or under-corrected astigmatism. Cylinder error smears the image in one meridian, which hits medium spatial frequencies hardest — the very region the eye is normally best at.
  • Light scatter at the lens surface. Surface reflections and a dirty, smeared or badly scratched lens add veiling glare. This is a large part of what an anti-reflective coating buys you.
  • Pupil size and light level. In dim light the pupil widens, which admits more aberrations, and the retina itself becomes less sensitive. Night vision is a genuinely lower-contrast regime.
  • Ageing. The neural and optical contributions both decline with age, and reduced contrast sensitivity is one of the more reliable age-related changes in visual function.
  • Dry eye and tear film instability. An irregular tear film is an irregular optical surface, and it degrades the image intermittently — which is why vision that fluctuates with blinking is a classic sign.

Common misconceptions

"20/20 means my vision is perfect." It means your high-contrast resolution is normal at that distance. It is a threshold measurement, not a description of visual quality.

"If contrast is poor, I need a stronger prescription." Often the prescription is already correct. Improving contrast perception usually means addressing the other contributors: correcting residual astigmatism, managing tear film, reducing surface reflections, or working in adequate light.

"A darker tint must make things clearer." Tint reduces the total light reaching the retina. In bright conditions that can improve comfort, but a dark lens in dim conditions lowers contrast — it works against you. Lens tint is a light-level solution, not a contrast solution.

"Scratches are only cosmetic." Deep scratches scatter light and produce localised loss of contrast in the affected area of the field. A lens can be optically correct and still perform poorly because of surface condition.

When to see an optometrist or ophthalmologist

Raise it if your acuity is reported as normal but you still struggle in low light, in rain or fog, on stairs, or when reading low-contrast text — and say so explicitly, because contrast sensitivity is not part of a routine refraction and is usually only tested when someone asks or when a specific condition is suspected.

Seek prompt assessment if reduced contrast sensitivity appears suddenly or asymmetrically between the two eyes, if it comes with glare or halos around lights, or if colours look washed out or different between eyes. Those patterns point towards causes that need examination rather than a lens change, and they should not be managed by ordering a new pair of glasses and hoping.

Practically, if you are choosing lenses and care about seeing well in poor conditions rather than only in good ones, ask what the lens does about scatter. Surface quality, coating choice and accurate correction of astigmatism usually matter more to real-world contrast than a change in index or a tint.
