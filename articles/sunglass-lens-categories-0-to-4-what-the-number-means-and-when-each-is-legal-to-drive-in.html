Sunglass Lens Categories 0 to 4: What the Number Means, and When Each Is Legal to Drive In

October 03, 2026 · 8 min read

The number on the temple arm is a light measurement, not a quality rating

Look inside the temple arm of a pair of sunglasses and you may find a small marking - "Cat. 3" or "filter category 3". It is not a review score, and it is not a UV rating. It is a statement of luminous transmittance: the proportion of visible light that actually passes through the lens. The classification comes from EN ISO 12312-1, the European standard covering afocal (zero-power) sunglasses and clip-ons for general use, and it sorts lenses into five bands.

  • Category 0 - 80% to 100% transmittance. Almost clear. Very limited glare reduction; used for fashion tints and some safety eyewear rather than for sun.
  • Category 1 - 43% to 80%. A light tint, suitable for low sun and overcast conditions.
  • Category 2 - 18% to 43%. A medium tint - the most broadly useful band for everyday wear in changeable light.
  • Category 3 - 8% to 18%. A dark tint for bright conditions: strong summer sun, beach, mountain. The band most often sold as general-purpose dark sunglasses.
  • Category 4 - 3% to 8%. Very dark, intended for extreme conditions such as high-altitude glacier travel and open water. Not permitted for road use.

Why category 4 is excluded from driving

Darkness cuts both ways on the road. A category 4 lens passes so little light that the wearer cannot reliably pick out dark objects - an unlit cyclist, a pedestrian in dark clothing, an unmarked kerb - and it also blunts the ability to read instruments and to see into shadow. European road-use rules therefore exclude category 4 from driving. The same logic generalises beyond regulation: a lens can be simultaneously very effective at suppressing glare and unsafe, because the two properties are not the same thing. Categories 0 through 3 are considered usable for driving; 4 is not.

Why a darker lens is not better protection

This is the misconception that matters most. Tint and UV protection are independent properties, and neither creates the other.

  • Tint controls how much visible light passes - that is the category number.
  • UV protection controls how much ultraviolet radiation passes, and depends on the lens material and its UV-absorbing treatment rather than on the depth of the dye.

A clear lens can block essentially all UV. A heavily tinted lens with no UV treatment can suppress visible light while letting ultraviolet through. That combination is worse than wearing nothing at all, because bright light normally triggers squinting and turning away; a dark tint removes that protective reflex while leaving the ultraviolet to reach the eye, and a dark environment also encourages the pupil to open wider behind the lens.

So the label worth looking for is not a tint depth but a UV claim. UV400 means the lens blocks radiation up to 400 nanometres, which spans the UVA and UVB ranges relevant to the eye. If a pair carries only a dark tint and no UV claim, the tint is doing nothing useful.

The category is only one of several things worth checking

Two pairs can both be marked category 3 and behave very differently, because the category states how much light passes and says nothing about how cleanly the lens is made. Four things are worth checking alongside it:

  • An explicit UV claim. Look for UV400 or an equivalent statement. Without it you are relying on the tint alone, and the tint is not what protects the eye.
  • Optical clarity. Hold the lens at arm's length, sight a straight edge through it, and move it slowly side to side. A well-made lens keeps that edge straight; a poor one makes it waver.
  • Coverage and wrap. Light reaches the eye around the frame as well as through the lens. Frames that sit far from the face or leave the sides open admit a meaningful amount of light past the lens, whatever the category says.
  • Fit stability. A lens that slides down the nose changes both the tint position and - for prescription wearers - the effective power, because the lens has moved relative to the eye.

Use case drives the right category more than fashion does. Water, snow and pale sand reflect a large share of the light that falls on them, so an afternoon on a boat or on a glacier is optically far more demanding than the same apparent brightness on grass or asphalt. That is precisely why category 4 exists for those settings - and why it is excluded from the road.

Common misconceptions

  • "Higher category means better sunglasses." Category describes tint density, not optical quality, UV protection, or how accurately the lens is made. A well-made category 2 lens will serve most daylight wearers better than a poor category 3 lens.
  • "Category 3 is the best choice for driving." It is legal, but category 2 is often more comfortable in changeable light, and category 3 can be too dark under overcast skies or in shade despite being permitted.
  • "Unmarked lenses are just unrated." An absent mark means the maker has not declared a category, not that the lens is harmless. Treat it as unknown and look for an explicit UV claim.
  • "Polarised means more UV protection." Polarisation reduces glare from light reflected off horizontal surfaces such as water and road. It is a separate function from UV blocking, and polarised lenses vary in UV performance. For most daylight wearers polarised UV400 sunglasses cover both jobs.

When to ask for advice instead of buying off the shelf

Talk to an eye care professional before choosing a category if you drive for a living, if you are selecting lenses for high altitude, snow or open water, if light causes genuine pain, or if you take a medication that increases photosensitivity. When buying for a child, check the UV claim rather than the tint depth: a strong dark tint on an unprotected lens is the least desirable combination for young eyes. And if bright light is consistently painful or if vision stays reduced in daylight, that is a symptom worth examining rather than something to shade.
