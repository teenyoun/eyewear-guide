Driving With Glasses: What the Vision Standard Actually Requires

November 18, 2026 · 9 min read

The standard is a floor, not a target

Driving vision rules are written to answer one question: is this person's eyesight bad enough to be a danger to other road users? That is a much lower bar than "can this person see well". The result is a legal minimum that a great many drivers clear comfortably while still seeing poorly in the conditions where crashes actually happen — rain at dusk, low sun, an unlit country road.

Understanding what the number means, and what it does not, is the difference between passing a test and driving well.

What the numbers actually say

In the European Union, the licence directive sets the group 1 (car and motorcycle) requirement at a binocular visual acuity of 0.5, with corrective lenses if they are needed to reach it, together with a horizontal visual field of at least 120 degrees. Decimal 0.5 is the same as 6/12 on a Snellen chart: what a person with standard vision sees at twelve metres, you must see at six.

The United Kingdom layers a practical test on top: read a standard number plate in good daylight at 20 metres, with characters 79 millimetres tall. That is broadly equivalent to 6/12, and it is deliberately a test anyone with reasonable correction can pass. In the United States the thresholds are set state by state, most commonly around 20/40 in one or both eyes, with wide variation in how field loss and monocular vision are handled.

Two details in that summary matter more than the headline number. The first is the phrase "with corrective lenses if needed": if you need glasses to meet the standard, you are required to wear them whenever you drive, and in most jurisdictions driving without them is an offence in itself, regardless of how well you happened to see that day. The second is that every one of these tests is conducted in good light, at high contrast, on a stationary target.

Acuity is one measurement out of several

Visual acuity is the ability to resolve fine detail under ideal contrast. It says almost nothing about the three functions that govern night driving.

  • Contrast sensitivity is the ability to see a target that is only slightly darker or lighter than its background — a pedestrian in a dark coat against wet asphalt. Two people can both read 6/12 and differ substantially here.
  • Glare recovery is how quickly vision returns after an oncoming headlight washes over the windscreen. This is a function of light scatter in the eye, and it degrades with age and with any opacity in the optical path, including a dirty lens.
  • Visual field is the width of the world you take in without moving your eyes. The 120-degree requirement exists because a field defect can hide a cyclist at a junction while central acuity is perfect.

None of those three is captured by a number plate at 20 metres.

Where glasses help and where they get in the way

A correct prescription sharpens the retinal image, and for most drivers that is the whole story. Two situations deserve more thought.

The first is the frame. A thick rim can cut into the upper field, which matters if you look up at traffic lights through the top of the lens rather than turning your head. A small eye size with a high fitting position gives more usable field than a large fashion frame with the eye sitting near its top edge.

The second is the lens surface. Anti-reflective coatings reduce the internal reflections that produce ghost images of headlights, and this is one of the few coating claims with a straightforward physical basis: less light reflected back toward the eye means fewer competing images. A scratched or hazed lens does the opposite, spreading light across the retina, and no prescription can recover contrast that was scattered before it reached the eye.

Common misconceptions

"If I pass the number plate test, my eyesight is fine for driving." It means you meet a statutory minimum in daylight. Night driving, rain and low sun are outside what the test measures, and they are where most vision-related crashes occur.

"I only need my glasses for distance, so I can drive without them." If the prescription is what brings you to the legal standard, wearing it while driving is a condition of the licence. The exception is a driver whose uncorrected acuity already clears the threshold, which is a question for an examiner rather than an assumption.

"A stronger prescription makes me a safer driver." Beyond the correct value, more minus blurs distance vision and can make the world look smaller. Overcorrection is not a safety margin; it is a defect.

"Tinted lenses are fine for night driving." Any lens that reduces light transmission reduces the already scarce light available at night, and category 4 filters are illegal for driving in most of Europe for exactly this reason. Yellow night-driving tints do not add photons.

Driving with one good eye

Monocular drivers can and do hold licences in most countries, usually subject to a field requirement and sometimes to a period of adaptation after the loss. The practical difference is not acuity but judgement of distance and the loss of a redundant field on one side, which is why mirror discipline and shoulder checks matter more rather than less.

When to book an examination rather than a new pair

See an optometrist if you find yourself read-ending number plates at a distance you used to manage, if oncoming headlights leave you with a blank patch for several seconds, if the edge of the road disappears in rain, or if you are turning your head to see what should be in peripheral view. Those are the symptoms that a legal minimum does not detect, and they are the ones worth acting on before the next renewal rather than after it.
