The Faint Marks on a Progressive Lens: What They Prove

October 23, 2026 · 7 min read

Tiny grey characters on a lens you thought was blank

Hold a progressive lens at an angle under a desk lamp and something appears near the edge: a pair of faint marks, sometimes a number, sometimes a letter and a number together. They were not printed on. They are laser engravings cut into the surface or just beneath a coating, and they exist because a progressive lens has no visible boundary to tell anyone what it is or where it is centred.

A single-vision lens has an optical centre you can find with a lensmeter. A bifocal has a segment edge. A progressive has a corridor, a fitting cross, and a power that changes continuously, and once the temporary ink markings are rubbed off by cleaning, nothing on the lens surface identifies which design it is. The engravings are the lens's birth certificate.

What is usually there

  • Two alignment marks. Small dots or dashes, set symmetrically on a horizontal line. They define the reference axis of the design, and the fitting cross sits at a fixed position relative to them. Their separation is specific to the manufacturer and the design, which is why an optician measures rather than assumes.
  • The addition. Engraved as the add value, sometimes without the decimal point: 200 for +2.00, 175 for +1.75. This is how a lens can be identified when two similar pairs are mixed up.
  • A material or design code. Two characters or so that identify the substrate index and, in some ranges, the design family. Manufacturers publish the code tables; they are not universal across brands.
  • Optional identifiers. Some labs engrave a customer or order reference.

Why they exist at the level of standards

Spectacle lens standards are written to make verification possible. ISO 8980-2 is the part of the ISO 8980 series that deals with power-varied lenses such as progressives, setting out optical requirements across the surface rather than a single central zone. Measuring a lens against those requirements depends on knowing where the corridor sits. The engravings supply exactly that reference: without a defined axis, a lens cannot be measured in the correct place, and a disagreement about whether it is within tolerance cannot be settled with evidence. In practice the trade also relies on them to identify an unlabelled pair.

How the marks are used in practice

Rebuilding the layout takes three steps. The two alignment marks are located and the distance between them recorded. A transparent overlay for that design, or the manufacturer's chart, is placed so its reference line joins the marks. The fitting cross, the prism reference point and the position of the addition then appear under the overlay, and a lensmeter can be placed at those points to read distance power, near power and prism.

This is how a lab checks a job before shipping, how an optician answers the question "are these the lenses I paid for", and how a replacement lens is made to match an existing pair whose ink marks are long gone. It is also why losing the pattern matters so little: a competent workshop can still find the reference axis, but it is faster when the marks survive.

Why they are so hard to see

The engraving has to satisfy two contradictory requirements. It must be durable enough to survive years of cleaning, and invisible enough that it never enters the wearer's field of view or scatters light. The compromise is a mark only a few micrometres deep, cut outside the corridor, and often placed under the coating stack rather than on top of it. Coating a lens after engraving fills the groove optically, which is why the marks look like a slight change in surface sheen rather than a cut.

That same geometry explains why they seem to disappear. Viewed straight on they are nearly invisible; they need oblique light and a dark background. Heavy cleaning with an abrasive cloth, or polishing that removes the coating at the edge, can genuinely erase them. When that happens the lens is not defective, but it is less identifiable.

What this means when ordering

If you are reordering a pair you like, the design name matters more than the numbers. Two lenses with identical power can have corridors of different lengths and different distributions of peripheral astigmatism, and the engravings are often the only way to establish which one you actually had. Photographing the marks before a lens is replaced costs nothing and settles the question later.

Misconceptions

  • "Those marks are scratches." They are engraved deliberately, in the same font and position on both lenses, usually outside the clear zone. A scratch does not repeat symmetrically.
  • "The marks show where to look." They do not sit at the optical centre, and looking through the corridor is not a matter of aligning the engravings. They are a manufacturing reference, not a user guide.
  • "Any lens can be identified by its engraving." The codes are manufacturer-specific. A number read off one brand's lens tells you nothing about another's.
  • "No marks means a fake lens." Some designs place engravings where they are hard to see, some are covered by thick coatings, and a few lenses are made without them. Absence is a prompt to measure, not a verdict.

When to ask about them

Raise it if a replacement lens does not match its twin in the corridor's position, if the fitting cross sits noticeably higher or lower than the pupil in one lens, or if a new pair behaves differently from the previous pair with the same prescription. Those are questions about whether the design and the layout match, and the engravings are how the answer is established. Distorted vision, double images or a corridor that cannot be found at all are reasons for a professional check rather than a guess.
