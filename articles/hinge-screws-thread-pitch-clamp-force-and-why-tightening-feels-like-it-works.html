Hinge Screws: Thread Pitch, Clamp Force and Why Tightening Feels Like It Works

October 31, 2026 · 8 min read

The smallest machine in your day

A pair of glasses contains two hinges, four screws and four screw threads, all of them smaller than a grain of rice. They are the parts that fail first, and they fail in a way that feels mysterious: a screw that was tight last week is loose today, you tighten it, it holds for an afternoon, and eventually the head turns without resistance. Understanding why requires nothing more than the thread pitch and a look at the materials on either side of the joint.

What one turn actually does

A screw advances by one pitch length per full revolution. Eyewear screws are commonly M1.4, a metric thread with a nominal diameter of 1.4 mm and a coarse pitch of 0.3 mm. One full turn therefore moves the screw 0.3 mm along its axis, and a typical hinge screw is three to four millimetres long, so the entire travel from engaged to seated is about ten to thirteen turns. That is worth knowing because it explains a common experience: a screw that has been loosened by vibration does not need a quarter turn, it needs to be re-seated through most of its travel.

The pitch also explains why the last half turn feels different. As the screw seats, the head contacts the barrel and further rotation converts torque into clamp force rather than into travel. The relationship is roughly linear: torque equals a factor times clamp force times diameter, where the factor depends on friction in the threads and under the head. For a small steel screw in a metal hinge, that factor is small, which is why a modest torque produces a large force and why the last half turn is where threads strip.

Where the failure actually happens

A hinge joint is a sandwich of two materials and one moving part. The screw threads into a threaded barrel, a tapped insert, or directly into the frame material. Each of those fails differently.

  • Direct threading into acetate. Acetate is soft. Every tightening and loosening cycle removes a little material, and the thread stops gripping after a handful of cycles. This is the most common cause of a screw that will not hold.
  • Tapped metal inserts. Titanium and stainless inserts hold far longer, but the screw itself can strip first, especially when a harder screw is forced into a softer insert.
  • Cross-threading. Starting the screw at an angle cuts a second, angled thread path. The joint feels tight and is permanently weakened; it is the single most destructive thing a home repair does.

Why it loosens at all

Threads are inclined planes, and an inclined plane under vibration will rotate. Walking, chewing and taking glasses on and off all load the hinge cyclically, and the load has a component along the thread helix. Once the clamp force drops below what friction needs to hold the joint, the screw walks out. A spring hinge makes it worse, because the spring stores energy that is released every time the temple is folded, and that release is a small impulse delivered directly to the thread.

Thread-locking compounds exist precisely for this: they fill the clearance between mating threads and raise the friction that resists rotation. A tiny amount on the last two or three threads is enough. A large amount migrates into the hinge barrel and gums the pivot, which is why the repair is easy to overdo.

Three misconceptions

Tighter is better. Beyond the point where the head seats, additional torque adds no clamp force and starts shearing the thread or crushing the acetate around it. The goal is seated, not tight.

Any small screw will do. Length matters more than diameter: a screw that is too long bottoms out in the barrel before the head seats, so it never clamps, and it can punch through the front of the hinge. A screw that is too short engages two threads and walks out immediately.

Adhesive is a permanent fix. Cyanoacrylate bonds the screw to the barrel and the barrel to the temple. The next repair removes the hinge with it. A thread-locker is reversible; superglue is not, and it is the reason many hinges arrive at a repair bench ruined.

What to do instead of tightening again

If a screw has needed tightening twice in a month, the thread is already damaged and the correct repair is replacement, not more torque. Ask for the screw to be replaced and, if the frame is acetate, for the hole to be re-tapped or filled and re-drilled. Both are routine jobs for an optical workshop and both cost less than a new frame.

If you are doing it yourself, use a screwdriver that fits the slot exactly, start the screw by hand until it turns freely for two full turns, and stop when resistance rises sharply rather than when it becomes hard to turn. Keep a spare pair of screws: they are the cheapest insurance in eyewear, and the only part of the frame designed to be replaced.

When the frame needs a workshop, not a screwdriver

Take it in if the hinge barrel is oval rather than round, if the temple swings without resistance after tightening, if the screw head sits below the barrel surface, or if a crack radiates from the screw hole. Those are structural, and forcing a screw into a damaged barrel converts a repair into a replacement.
