Convergence Insufficiency: When Close Work Feels Like Pulling Focus

November 03, 2026 · 9 min read

A focus problem and a pointing problem are not the same problem

When near vision becomes uncomfortable, most people assume the lens inside the eye is struggling. That is one possible cause, and past the age of about forty it is a common one. But reading requires two systems to act at once, and they can fail independently. Accommodation changes the optical power of the eye so that a near object comes into focus. Convergence rotates the eyes inward so that both point at the same object. The two are linked: focusing effort drives convergence, and convergence drives focusing effort. That linkage is what makes a convergence problem feel like a focusing problem, and it is why people spend years buying stronger reading glasses for something a reading glass cannot touch.

Convergence insufficiency is the specific case where the eyes cannot sustain inward rotation for near work. The eyes are healthy, the optics may be perfectly corrected, and the task is still exhausting, because the system that holds the two eyes pointed at one point keeps losing the argument.

The measurement that names the problem

The near point of convergence is the closest point at which a target can be held as single. It is measured by bringing a small target toward the bridge of the nose and recording where the person reports double vision (the break point) and where fusion is recovered when the target is moved away again (the recovery point). A break point receded beyond roughly 6 cm is one of the conventional signs, and the recovery point is often reported too, because a normal break with a poor recovery suggests the system gives up easily. The number is not a diagnosis on its own, but it is objective, repeatable, and easy to explain.

The second measurement is fusional reserve: how much prism the person can overcome while keeping a single image. Base-out prism demands convergence, so a reduced base-out break point at near — commonly cited around 15 prism dioptres or less — indicates that the convergence system has little in hand. The third is the near deviation itself: an exophoria at near that exceeds the distance phoria by about 4 prism dioptres or more describes eyes that drift outward when the near target removes the stimulus to converge.

Three findings, then, describe the same underlying shortfall from three directions: a receded near point, weak base-out reserves, and a near exophoria larger than the distance one. A diagnosis is built from the pattern, not from a single number.

Why the symptoms arrive late in the day

Fusional convergence is not a muscle that can be strengthened by simply using it more, but it does behave like a reserve in one respect: it is finite and it depletes with sustained demand. Sheard's criterion formalises this. It states that the compensating fusional reserve should be at least twice the demand placed on it for comfortable single vision. Where the reserve is barely above the demand, the system holds, but only just, and the margin disappears as fatigue accumulates.

That is the mechanism behind the characteristic history: fine at nine in the morning, strained by four in the afternoon, and a genuine headache by seven. It also explains why the same person can read a book for an hour without difficulty and find a spreadsheet unbearable — the two tasks differ in working distance, in the number of small targets, and in how long the eyes are asked to hold a single sustained posture.

Why ordinary reading glasses do not solve it

An over-the-counter reader adds plus power, which brings the print into focus at a chosen distance. It does not reduce the convergence demand, because the eyes still have to point at the same page. In some cases a reader makes the situation slightly worse: if the added plus lets accommodation relax, the accommodative convergence that came with it also relaxes, and the eyes drift further out at near. The person then reports that the glasses are sharp but the page still feels wrong, which is a very specific kind of complaint and a useful one to describe accurately.

A prescription that addresses the convergence problem works differently. Reducing the demand is the goal, and prism base-in is the direct way to do it: if the images are already displaced in the direction the eyes want to drift, less convergence is required to fuse them. Prism reading glasses are therefore a compensatory tool. They make the task comfortable without changing the underlying ability, which means the relief lasts exactly as long as the glasses are worn.

Vergence therapy, and what the evidence actually supports

Vergence and accommodative therapy is the other route, and it works by a different principle: rather than lowering the demand, it trains the response to a given demand. Office-based programmes using instruments, prism flippers and computer-guided targets have been studied more rigorously than the home exercises most people are told about. The largest controlled work in this area, the Convergence Insufficiency Treatment Trial, compared office-based vergence therapy with home-based pencil push-ups and with placebo, and found the office-based approach clearly more effective on both symptoms and clinical signs. Pencil push-ups alone were not meaningfully better than placebo in that comparison.

That result is worth stating plainly because pencil push-ups are the most commonly recommended home exercise in the world. They are not useless, but they are not equivalent to a structured programme, and the difference is dose and feedback rather than effort.

Claims to be sceptical about

  • "It is just screen strain, so take more breaks." Breaks help fatigue. They do not change a receded near point or weak reserves.
  • "Prism glasses cure it." Prism compensates. It does not train the response, and stopping the glasses returns the symptoms.
  • "It explains dyslexia." Convergence difficulties and reading difficulties can coexist, and treating the convergence problem may improve comfort. It does not explain the reading disorder, and claims that it does have repeatedly failed to hold up.
  • "Children grow out of it." Some do, some do not, and the ones who do not spend those years avoiding reading. The measurement is cheap and the alternative is guesswork.
  • "Stronger glasses will force the eyes to work properly." Over-plusing a young, accommodating eye can be actively unhelpful, which is why the refraction and the binocular assessment have to be read together.

When to have it measured rather than managed

Book an assessment if reading or screen work reliably produces headache or brow ache within twenty to thirty minutes, if print appears to swim or split, if you find yourself closing one eye to read, if you push a book further away to keep it single, or if a child is avoiding near work and reporting tiredness rather than blur. Sudden double vision, double vision that persists when either eye is covered, or double vision with a new headache and any neurological symptom needs prompt medical assessment, not a stronger pair of glasses. Nothing in this article is a diagnosis; the numbers only mean something when they are measured on your eyes.
