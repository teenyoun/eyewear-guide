Flicker You Cannot See: Temporal Light Modulation and Eye Strain

November 24, 2026 · 9 min read

The light in the room is not steady, even when it looks steady

Most people assume that a lamp either works or it flickers. In practice almost every artificial light source modulates — its output rises and falls many times a second, and the eye integrates the ripple into what looks like a constant glow. Whether that ripple is harmless depends on two numbers: how deep the modulation is, and how fast it repeats. Neither number is printed on the box, and neither is visible in a shop.

This is the part of screen-and-lighting comfort that gets almost no attention, because it cannot be sold as a lens. It is still worth understanding, for a practical reason: if your eye strain tracks the room or the monitor rather than the amount of reading you do, no pair of glasses will fix it, and you will keep buying lenses that do not address the cause.

Principle: fusion happens in the retina, not in the lamp

Temporal light modulation (TLM) is the technical name for any periodic change in light output over time. It is described by two quantities. Modulation depth, often reported as percent flicker, is the peak-to-trough swing expressed as a fraction of the average: a source that drops to zero and returns to full output is at 100 per cent, and a source that varies by one per cent is at one per cent. Frequency is how many times per second the cycle repeats.

The retina does not respond to the average; it responds to change. Below a certain rate the ripple is perceived as flicker. Above it, the visual system fuses successive cycles into continuous light — the critical flicker fusion frequency, which for a large, bright field sits in the region of 50 to 60 Hz and rises for small, high-contrast sources. Fusing the flicker, however, is not the same as being unaffected by it. Retinal pathways still carry the temporal signal, and the visual system still has to resolve a stimulus that is being chopped. The result is not "seeing flicker" but a slow cost in comfort, most obvious in people who are photosensitive or migraine-prone.

Where the modulation comes from

  • Mains ripple. Any lamp driven directly from AC power pulses at twice the supply frequency — 100 Hz on a 50 Hz grid, 120 Hz on a 60 Hz grid — because the waveform has two peaks per cycle. Older magnetic-ballast fluorescent tubes modulate deeply at this rate, which is why some offices feel worse than their measured illuminance suggests.
  • Pulse-width modulation. Most dimmable LEDs do not dim by reducing current; they switch fully on and fully off very fast, varying the proportion of on-time. At moderate to high frequencies this is comfortable; at low frequencies and deep modulation it is the worst case, because the light is genuinely going to zero.
  • Panel behaviour at low brightness. Many displays use PWM only below a certain brightness level, so a monitor can be calm at 80 per cent and restless at 15 per cent. This is why late-evening screen use at low brightness sometimes feels worse than the same screen at a comfortable level.

Rate alone does not settle the question. A 200 Hz source that swings from full to dark still produces a visible artefact when your eye moves: the phantom array effect, in which a saccade across a small bright object leaves a dotted trail of images instead of one. That artefact is a spatial signature of a temporal problem, and it is often what people describe as "this screen looks gritty when I look across it".

What the standards actually say

IEEE 1789-2015, a recommended practice rather than a law, relates permitted modulation depth to frequency instead of setting a single limit. Its low-risk guidance lands near 8 per cent modulation in the region of 100 Hz, with a stricter boundary — around 3 per cent — for the "no observable effect" category at similar rates, and much tighter limits at lower frequencies where the eye is more sensitive. The practical reading is simple: depth and rate trade against each other, and a marketing claim of "flicker-free" usually means "high frequency" rather than "zero modulation".

That distinction matters because a display can be flicker-free by one measure and uncomfortable by another. A 100 per cent modulation at 2 kHz is generally a non-issue. A 100 per cent modulation at 120 Hz is a very different stimulus, even though both are technically "high frequency" compared with a 50 Hz ripple.

Common misconceptions

"If I cannot see it, it does not affect me." Fusion describes perception of the flicker itself, not the absence of a physiological response. People differ widely, and the population that notices is not small.

"Flicker-free on the box means no modulation." It usually means the modulation has been pushed above a rate where it is easy to see. Ask for the rate and the depth, or measure them.

"Blue light glasses will settle it." A tint changes which wavelengths arrive; it does not change how the light is chopped in time. If the symptom follows the lamp or the panel, a filter is the wrong instrument. The useful interventions are a different light source, DC dimming or a higher PWM rate, and a screen at a brightness where the panel behaves.

"Brighter rooms are always better." Raising illuminance raises the fusion frequency the eye needs, so a marginal lamp can feel worse when the room is bright than when it is dim.

How to check your own environment

Two tests need nothing but a phone. Point the camera at the lamp or screen in slow-motion mode: banding or a rolling dark bar across the frame indicates modulation the eye is fusing. Second, wave a pencil quickly back and forth in front of the light — if you see a series of discrete pencil images rather than a blur, the source is pulsing at a rate your eye can resolve across a saccade. Neither test is a measurement, but both separate "this light is steady" from "this light is chopping".

When it is not a lighting problem

Flicker intolerance, glare sensitivity and headaches that appear specifically under certain lights can also be driven by an uncorrected refractive error, a binocular vision problem, dry eye, or a primary photosensitivity condition. A tinted lens, a different lamp and a new monitor all fail if the underlying issue is that the two eyes are not working together comfortably. If symptoms persist after you have changed the lighting and had your refraction checked, the next step is a full binocular vision assessment rather than another pair of glasses — and if light sensitivity is severe, sudden, or accompanied by pain, that is a medical assessment, not an optical one.
