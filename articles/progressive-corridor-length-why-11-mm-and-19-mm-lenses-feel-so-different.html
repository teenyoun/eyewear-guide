Progressive Corridor Length: Why 11 mm and 19 mm Lenses Feel So Different

November 27, 2026 · 9 min read

Two pairs, same prescription, completely different feel

Two people with identical prescriptions can order "progressive lenses" and receive lenses that behave nothing alike, because progressive is a category rather than a specification. One of the parameters that differs most, and is almost never printed on an order form, is corridor length: the vertical distance over which the power actually changes from distance to near.

The principle: the add has to be spent somewhere

A progressive lens has to increase power by the full reading addition between the distance zone and the near zone. That increase happens along a narrow vertical band called the corridor, and the rate at which power changes is set by how much vertical space the design spends on it.

Spread a 2.00 D addition over a long corridor and the power climbs gently, about a tenth of a dioptre per millimetre in the middle of the progression. Squeeze the same 2.00 D into a corridor a third shorter and the gradient steepens accordingly. Nothing is free: the power has to arrive either way, and shortening the distance over which it arrives concentrates the change.

That concentration has a geometric consequence described by Minkwitz's theorem, which states that the unwanted astigmatism at the edge of a progressive lens increases at roughly twice the rate of the power change along the corridor. A steeper corridor therefore produces higher surface astigmatism in the periphery, closer to the centre of the lens. This is why short-corridor designs feel faster to adapt to in the vertical sense and worse in the horizontal one: the blur zones crowd inward.

What corridor length actually decides

  • How tall the frame must be. A design with a longer corridor needs more vertical room between the fitting cross and the near zone. Short-corridor designs were invented for shallow frames, and their minimum fitting height is commonly a few millimetres lower than a standard design's.
  • How sharp the transition feels. A longer corridor gives a softer, more gradual change; walk down stairs in a long-corridor lens and the floor arrives more predictably. A short corridor reaches full reading power sooner, which suits a desk and punishes a staircase.
  • How wide the clear distance zone stays. Longer corridors generally preserve a wider distance area for the same addition, because the peripheral aberration is pushed further from the centre.
  • How much of the lens is usable for intermediate work. The intermediate band sits inside the corridor. A short corridor compresses it, which is why monitor work at arm's length can fall into a narrow strip that the wearer has to hunt for.

Reading the numbers honestly

Corridor lengths are commonly quoted somewhere between about 11 mm and 19 mm, and the same nominal figure does not mean the same thing across manufacturers, because the measurement conventions differ and because the design's inset, the position of the fitting cross and the way the addition is distributed along the corridor all vary. A 14 mm corridor from one supplier is not automatically comparable to a 14 mm corridor from another.

What is comparable is the relationship between three numbers: the addition, the corridor length, and the frame's B measurement. If the frame is shallow, the near zone has to fit below the pupil, and a long-corridor design either will not fit or will be edged so that reading power sits too low. If the frame is deep and the corridor is short, the wearer gets a large near zone and a small distance zone relative to what the frame could support. Neither outcome is a manufacturing defect; both are consequences of a design choice meeting a frame choice.

This is also why a perfectly made progressive can be "wrong" for a person without being wrong at all. If a wearer spends six hours a day at a monitor and one hour driving, a short corridor with a generous intermediate band is a better instrument than a long corridor with a wide distance zone. If the same person drives for a living, the priority reverses.

Claims worth discounting

  • "A shorter corridor is a more modern design, so it is better." Short corridors exist to solve a fitting constraint, not to advance optics. They trade peripheral clarity for vertical compactness.
  • "Corridor length is the same as the fitting height." They are different measurements. Fitting height is where the fitting cross sits relative to the frame; corridor length is the length of the power progression inside the lens. A tall frame with a low fitting height wastes the top of the corridor.
  • "Digital surfacing removes the trade-off." Free-form manufacturing lets a design be optimised for a specific prescription, frame and fitting position, which genuinely reduces the penalty. It does not repeal the geometry: the addition still has to be delivered over some vertical distance.
  • "Any progressive can be cut into any frame." Below a design's minimum fitting height, the near zone is truncated. The result is a lens that reads well only when the chin is lifted.

When to take the question to an optometrist

If a new pair forces you to tilt your head to read, if the intermediate zone seems to be missing, if the floor appears to rise when you walk, or if you find yourself taking the glasses off to use a screen, the fitting position and the corridor design are the first things to review — before assuming the prescription is wrong. Those are measurements, not opinions, and they are quick to check with the frame on your face.

Persistent adaptation failure beyond a few weeks, double vision, or a marked difference between the two eyes should be assessed rather than endured. A corridor that is too short for the way you use your eyes produces real symptoms, and the fix is usually a different design or a different frame, not a stronger prescription.
