How to Adjust Glasses at Home — Bend, Tighten, and Fix Without Breaking

August 13, 2026 · 8 min read

Frames do not go crooked on their own. Glasses are a spring-loaded structure on four contact points — two nose pads, two temple hooks — and taking them off one-handed rotates the front around a single hinge. Do that twice a day for a year and the frame keeps the memory of the load: what feels like a broken frame is usually one of three alignments drifting.

Everything below works on ordinary acetate and metal frames with a hairdryer, a jeweller's screwdriver and padded pliers — not on memory titanium, drilled rimless lenses or electronic temples.

Before you start: clean the frame and your hands — grit under a temple tip turns an adjustment into a scratch. Memory titanium, drilled rimless lenses and audio frames need a bench, not a hairdryer.

What actually drifts: three alignments, not one

A frame can be wrong in three separate planes, and fixing one never fixes the others. Check them in this order; each assumes the previous is already true.

AlignmentWhat it controlsHow you notice it
Frontal planeBoth lenses at the same heightTilted in photos; one brow weighted
Pantoscopic tiltLens plane against line of sightFloor looks sloped; chin tips up to read
Vertex symmetryEach lens-to-eye distanceOne eye tires first; one-sided headache

Why two millimetres matters: Prentice's rule

Mechanical errors are millimetres; the penalty is paid in prism dioptres. Prentice's rule converts one to the other:

Δ = c × F

where Δ is prismatic effect in prism dioptres, c the decentration in centimetres, F the lens power in dioptres. A +4.00 D lens sitting 2 mm low puts 0.2 × 4.00 = 0.8Δ base-up before that eye while its partner carries none. The two eyes now get vertically mismatched images — the mechanism behind a one-sided headache, and why a frame that looks only slightly crooked can be uncomfortable.

Two definitions follow. Decentration is the distance from a lens's optical centre to the centre of the pupil — the quantity the rule multiplies. Aniseikonia is the two eyes receiving images of different size, which a few millimetres of vertex asymmetry produces, because retinal image size scales with lens-to-eye distance.

Acetate and metal fail in opposite ways

Cellulose acetate is a thermoplastic with no sharp melting point: it softens progressively and then holds whatever shape it cools into. A hairdryer does not melt the frame — it pushes the polymer past the point where it resists, lets the chains slide, and freezes them in place. Too little heat and it springs back; too much and it goes glossy, whitens, then tears.

Metal is the reverse: elastic, deformed plastically in small increments. No heat and no memory — but it fatigues and snaps if you work one point back and forth. Acetate takes one decisive supported bend; metal takes many small ones.

Four rules that prevent breakage

  1. Heat only the part you intend to move — warming the bridge to fix a temple bend moves the wrong place first.
  2. Never grip the bridge or a lens edge. Hold the front by the rim above and below each lens.
  3. One or two degrees per attempt. A frame you can feel yielding is about to crack.
  4. Skip boiling water. It heats frame and lens together, and soaked acetate dries cloudy.

The five fixes

1. Crooked on a flat surface

Fold the temples and set the glasses on a table; both tips should touch, and a floating tip means the front is twisted. Acetate: warm the bridge for 20–30 seconds, press the high side down, hold until cool. Metal: no heat — counter-twist a couple of degrees holding a lens in each hand, then re-check.

2. Sliding down the nose

Grip is friction, and friction needs normal force from the hooks and pads. Warm the last 3–4 cm of temple and increase the curve so it follows the mastoid bone behind the ear; on metal, form it with taped pliers. Pinch the pad arms closer to raise and tighten, splay them to lower. Free first step: wash pads and tips with dish soap — oily acetate grips far less.

3. Pinching at the temples

Warm the temple arm from hinge to bend for about 30 seconds and spread it a degree at a time, holding until cool. On metal, open it at the hinge with taped pliers. Check width first: a front narrower than your head will always press.

4. One lens closer than the other

Sight along the top of the frame — the two lens planes should be mirror images. Move the near-side pad outward, or the far-side pad inward, in small steps. On acetate the bridge must be warmed and reshaped, so persistent asymmetry belongs to an optician.

5. Loose screws

Hinge screws loosen because every opening loads them. Use a #00 or #0 jeweller's driver; a household Phillips is too coarse and rounds the slot. A trace of blue, removable threadlocker breaks the cycle. Check hinges and pads monthly — a lost screw is the commonest frame failure.

Checking your work with numbers

"Feels better" is not a measurement. Three checks: the table test; pupil height, both pupils sitting level in their lenses; and frame width against your own temples.

Frame dimensions use the boxed system of ISO 8624: a marking of 52 □ 18 − 145 means A = 52 mm lens width, DBL = 18 mm bridge, 145 mm temple. The distance between boxed lens centres is frame PD = A + DBL = 70 mm, and comparing that with your own pupillary distance tells you how much built-in decentration you wear.

Myths worth clearing up

  • "Bend it back and it stays." Not on acetate: the polymer only keeps a shape it cooled into under load, and a cold bend springs back.
  • "Hot water is gentler than a hairdryer." It is less controllable, heats the lens too, and softens the whole frame instead of one point.
  • "Tighter means less sliding." Past a point it only creates sore spots; sliding is lost friction at the pads or a hook that no longer follows the ear.
  • "A loose screw means a worn hinge." Usually it means vibration and no threadlocker. Replace the screw before blaming the hinge.

When to stop and hand it over

Stop if a hinge has cracked, the lenses are drilled or rimless, the frame is a memory alloy, or vision is still uncomfortable once the frame is straight. Pantoscopic tilt normally sits around 8–12° and vertex distance 12–14 mm; a frame that cannot reach those values needs replacing, not bending.

Bottom line

Nearly every home adjustment is one of five moves: level the front, tighten the ear hook, open or close the temples, even out the pads, tighten the screws. Do them in that order and judge the result on a table, not in a mirror. If the frame is the wrong width or material, the answer is a different frame.
