Sun Behind You: How Light Reaches Your Eye Around the Lens

November 13, 2026 · 9 min read

Light has more than one way in

The usual mental model of a sunglass lens is a filter in front of the pupil: light travels from the sun, through the lens, into the eye. That model is correct for the light the lens is designed to stop, and incomplete for almost everything else. Between the lens and the eye there is an air gap, and the back surface of the lens is an optical interface facing the world behind you. Whatever it reflects, your eye receives.

This is why a pair of sunglasses worn on a bright day with the sun behind you can still feel glaring, and why the inside of a deeply tinted lens sometimes shows a faint reflection of your own cheek or of the scene behind. It is not a defect. It is the geometry of two surfaces and one eye.

The four percent you cannot see

At every boundary between air and a transparent medium, some light reflects. For normal incidence the fraction is given by the Fresnel relation: reflectance equals the square of (n − 1) divided by (n + 1), where n is the refractive index of the material. For CR-39 at about 1.498 that works out to roughly 4% per surface. For polycarbonate at 1.586 it is roughly 5%. A lens has two surfaces, so an uncoated pair reflects around 8% of the light reaching it — and reflects it in both directions, which is the part usually forgotten.

The angle matters more than the material. Reflectance at normal incidence is the minimum; as the angle of incidence approaches grazing, reflectance climbs steeply toward total reflection. Light arriving from the side, travelling almost parallel to the lens surface, is reflected far more efficiently than light arriving head-on. A lens edge, a steeply curved rim, or the far side of a wrapped lens is therefore a good mirror for exactly the light a wearer is trying to escape.

Why dark lenses make it worse

Reflected light does not change when the lens gets darker. Transmitted light does. A lens that passes 15% of visible light reduces the scene behind it by a factor of nearly seven, while the reflection off its back surface stays roughly the same in absolute terms. The eye adapts to the dimmer transmitted image and becomes more sensitive, so the reflected component occupies a larger share of what is perceived. This is the optical reason a very dark lens with no back-surface treatment can feel paradoxically glary, and why light leaking in from the side or behind dominates the experience.

What a back-surface coating does about it

An anti-reflective coating is a stack of thin dielectric layers whose reflected waves interfere destructively across a designed band of wavelengths. Applied to the back surface, it reduces the light that would otherwise bounce off the inside of the lens into the eye. Applied to both surfaces — the usual configuration on a good prescription lens — it raises transmission and cuts the veiling reflection that lowers contrast.

There is a visible side effect worth knowing about. A multi-layer stack does not suppress every wavelength equally; it is tuned, so the small residual reflection is coloured. That faint green or purple sheen on a lens is the signature of the coating doing its job, and on a sunglass it often appears more obvious from the inside than the outside because the tint behind it suppresses everything else.

The other half of the problem is geometry

No front-surface filter can block light that never passes through it. Eyewear has an aperture, and the aperture is bounded by the frame. Light arriving from behind and to the side enters the gap between frame and face and reaches the eye directly, without interacting with the lens at all. The practical variables are the wrap of the front, the face-form angle — the angle at which the lens plane turns away from the frontal plane toward the temple — the depth of the frame, and how closely the temples follow the head.

This is also why reflected light from below matters in some settings. Snow, water and pale sand reflect a large fraction of incoming sunlight upward, so the eye receives light from underneath the lens as well as from behind it. A frame with a low bottom rim and a lens with a shallow wrap does nothing about that path.

The claims that need correcting

A UV400 label protects the whole eye. A UV400 marking describes the transmittance of the lens material and its absorber across the ultraviolet band up to 400 nm. It is a statement about the lens, not about coverage. The skin of the eyelid margin, the conjunctiva and the cornea at the lateral canthus are exposed to whatever the frame does not cover, and that exposure is not tested by a lens transmittance specification.

A mirror coating on the front stops glare from behind. A mirror coating is designed to reflect light arriving from the front, which reduces transmission into the eye. It does nothing about the back surface facing you, and on a strongly mirrored lens the uncoated inner surface can be the brightest thing in your field of view.

Darker lenses protect more. Tint density controls visible light, and it can increase the relative impact of back-surface and lateral light as described above. Ultraviolet absorption depends on the absorber in the material or the coating, not on how dark the lens looks. The two properties are independent and are specified independently.

Wearing sunglasses indoors for comfort is harmless. Dark lenses indoors reduce retinal illumination without reducing the light that is actually causing discomfort, and the visual system adapts to the lower level, which can make the outdoor transition harsher. Comfort in high-glare conditions is better addressed with the right tint and coverage for the environment than with more darkness.

When this becomes a clinical question

Discomfort from light arriving behind or beside the lens is usually a fit and coating question, and an optician can answer it by looking at the frame in relation to your face. It becomes a clinical question when the sensitivity is new, when it is disproportionate to the light level, when it follows eye surgery or a change in medication, or when it comes with pain, tearing or a change in vision. Those are reasons to have the eyes examined rather than to buy a darker pair.

For everyday use, the practical summary is unglamorous: the front of the lens handles the sun in front of you, the coating on the back reduces what bounces off it, and the frame geometry decides what reaches the eye around the edges. All three matter, and only one of them is printed on the label.
