What 52-18-145 Actually Measures on a Pair of Glasses

November 11, 2026 · 9 min read

Three numbers, and only two of them decide how your lens is made

Look inside the temple of almost any pair of glasses and you will find something like 52□18-145 stamped or printed there. The numbers are millimetres, and they are not a size rating out of anything. They are three measurements of three different parts of the frame. The small square between the first two is not decoration either: it marks the convention being used, which is that the lens width is measured as a box rather than as a circle.

Understanding the three numbers matters more than it sounds, because two of them set the geometry the laboratory works to, and the third one, the number everyone quotes when they talk about ‘size’, is the one that has the least to do with whether the glasses fit.

What the boxing system actually measures

Modern frames are measured with the boxing system, adopted in the United States in 1962 to replace a set of competing conventions that made comparisons between manufacturers meaningless. An imaginary rectangle is drawn around the lens shape so that it touches the widest points horizontally and the highest and lowest points vertically. The horizontal width of that box is the lens width, the A dimension — 52 in the example. The vertical height is the B dimension, usually not printed, and it is what decides whether a frame is deep enough for a progressive corridor. The diagonal of the box is the effective diameter, or ED, which is the figure the laboratory needs when it checks whether a lens blank is large enough for the job.

The second number, 18, is the gap between the two lenses across the bridge, known as DBL, distance between lenses. It is a lens-to-lens measurement, not the moulded width of the bridge, and the two differ whenever the bridge has a decorative profile or a keyhole shape. The third number, 145, is the temple length: the full length of the arm from the hinge to the tip, measured along the bend. It describes the arm, not your head.

Frame PD, your PD, and the four millimetres that cost you thickness

Add the lens width to the DBL and you get the frame PD — the distance between the two optical centres the frame is designed to hold. In the example, 52 + 18 = 70 mm. Your own pupillary distance is measured separately and for most adults falls somewhere between about 58 and 68 mm. The difference between the two is the decentration, and it is split between the eyes: if your PD is 62 mm and the frame PD is 70 mm, each lens has to be moved inward by 4 mm to put its optical centre in front of your pupil.

On a minus lens, moving the optical centre inward means the temporal edge is cut from a thicker part of the blank, so the finished edge grows. The minimum blank size formula makes the cost explicit: MBS = ED + 2 × decentration. A frame with a 50 mm ED and 8 mm of total decentration needs a blank at least 66 mm across, and blanks that size in a high-index material are harder to source and more expensive. This is the mechanism behind the most common disappointment in online glasses buying: the prescription was made correctly and the frame was simply too wide for the face wearing it.

Height matters as much as width

Vertical position is usually treated as a cosmetic question and is not one. Prism induced by displacement follows Prentice's rule, P = c × F, with c in centimetres and F the power in the meridian concerned. A −5.00 D lens whose optical centre sits 3 mm too high delivers 0.3 × 5.00 = 1.50 dioptres of unwanted prism at the pupil, base up or base down depending on which way the error goes.

Both lenses being equally wrong is the tolerable case: the eyes are pushed in the same vertical direction and the binocular system manages. A difference between the two lenses — one 1 mm off, the other 3 mm — produces vertical imbalance, which is far harder to ignore and is usually reported as one lens feeling wrong rather than as double vision. This is why optical centre height is measured to the pupil rather than to the frame, and why a progressive needs a fitting height as well as a PD. A deep B dimension gives the corridor room; a shallow frame forces the near zone to be cut away.

Four things the numbers do not tell you

  • The first number is not the frame width. A 52 lens in an 18 bridge gives a 70 mm frame PD; a 48 in a 22 also gives 70. Two frames with completely different styling can have identical optical geometry.
  • Temple length is not a fit measurement. It describes the arm including the bend, and a longer temple does not make a frame wider across the face. Face width is a separate measurement taken at the temples.
  • A bigger lens is not more field of view in any useful sense. The usable field is set mainly by the frame aperture and the vertex distance, and enlarging the aperture mostly buys thicker edges on a minus lens.
  • Your PD is not necessarily one number. Monocular PDs, one measurement per eye, are what a laboratory actually uses, because faces are not symmetrical, and a single binocular figure hides the asymmetry that decentres one lens more than the other.

How to use the numbers when you order

Compare two figures before you choose a frame: your PD, and the frame PD printed on the frame or listed in the product details. Within a couple of millimetres, the lens will be centred with very little decentration, and the thickness you were promised is roughly the thickness you will get. If the frame PD is much larger than your PD, expect the edges to grow, especially beyond about −4.00 D, and consider a smaller eye size or a higher index material rather than a bigger frame.

It is also worth checking the B dimension against the corridor of any progressive you order, and the ED against the blank sizes your chosen index comes in. Neither figure appears in most product listings, which is part of why online frame buying goes wrong: the three numbers on the temple are a manufacturing description, not a fit guarantee.

If a pair arrives and the clear zone sits off to one side, the bridge leaves marks, or the edges look far thicker than the arithmetic suggested, the useful next step is a measurement rather than a return: ask for the PD, the optical centre height and the frame PD to be checked against what was ordered.
