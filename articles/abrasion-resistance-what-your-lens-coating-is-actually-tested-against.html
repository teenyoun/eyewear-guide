Abrasion Resistance: What Your Lens Coating Is Actually Tested Against

October 20, 2026 · 8 min read

Two words that get used interchangeably, and should not be

Abrasion and scratching are different failures. Abrasion is the slow accumulation of microscopic damage across an area: thousands of small contacts, each removing or deforming a little material, until the surface develops a fine haze visible against a dark background. A scratch is a discrete event with a visible line. Abrasion robs contrast and scatters light at night. A scratch produces localised glare and, if it crosses the optical centre, a streak that follows the light source.

The distinction matters because the standards, the tests and the warranties all treat them differently, and because most lens damage people complain about is abrasion rather than a single dramatic scratch.

What ISO 8980-5 actually establishes

ISO 8980-5:2005 is the part of the spectacle lens standard series that specifies the requirement and test method for lens surfaces claimed to provide a basic level of abrasion resistance. Three details in that sentence matter more than the number itself.

  • "Claimed to provide." The standard applies to lenses that make the claim. It does not require every lens to pass; it defines what passing means for those that do.
  • "Basic level." The words are deliberately modest. Passing is not a durability guarantee, a lifetime promise, or a ranking against other lenses. It is a floor.
  • "Surfaces." Both the front and the back surface are covered, and the test restricts which lens powers and surface forms are used, because a strongly curved surface behaves differently from a flat one under an abrasive.

What a test of this kind has to pin down is the abrasive medium, the load applied, the number of cycles, and how the result is measured. The measurement is haze: the proportion of light scattered rather than transmitted, read on a haze meter before and after. A lens that gains little haze under the specified conditions passes; one that hazes heavily fails. The exact parameters belong to the standard, and any lab quoting a result should be able to say which edition and which conditions it used.

Why a passing lens still hazes in your pocket

Because the test is a controlled comparison and your pocket is not. Real-world abrasion comes from dust and grit trapped in a cloth, from paper tissues, from a shirt hem, and from the same cloth being used for months without washing. Those particles are harder than the coating, and repeated wiping under hand pressure applies far more cumulative load than a standardised cycle.

There is also a structural reason. A modern lens is a stack: the substrate, a hard coat for abrasion, a multilayer anti-reflective stack for transmission, and a hydrophobic top layer for cleaning. Each interface is a potential failure point. The top layer is thin and soft by design, because it has to repel water and oil; the anti-reflective stack beneath it is also thin. When the stack fails, it usually fails at an edge or a junction first, which is why damage so often begins at the rim of the frame or at the boundary of a bifocal segment.

Four claims that do not hold up

  • "Scratch-resistant means scratch-proof." No coating on a plastic lens is immune to a key, a beach, or a dry tissue. Resistance is a rate, not a shield.
  • "The more expensive the lens, the harder the coat." Price tracks the complexity of the coating stack and the precision of the surfacing. A thicker anti-reflective stack can actually be more delicate than a simple hard coat, because there is more to delaminate.
  • "A little haze is cosmetic." Haze is measured as scattered light, and scattering reduces contrast exactly where contrast is already hardest to come by: night driving, low-light reading, and fine print. The effect is optical, not aesthetic.
  • "The warranty covers scratches." Coating warranties typically cover manufacturing defects such as crazing, cracking or delamination that appear without cause. They rarely cover abrasion from cleaning, because the cause is the user's cloth, not the factory.

What is actually in your control

  • Rinse before you wipe. Water carries grit off the surface; a dry cloth grinds it in.
  • Use a clean microfiber cloth and wash it periodically. A cloth that has been in a bag for a month is a sheet of abrasive.
  • Never use paper products, and never use a dry tissue on a dry lens.
  • Avoid heat and solvents. A hot car softens coatings and stresses the substrate; alcohol and ammonia attack the top layer of an anti-reflective stack.
  • Store in a case. Most abrasion happens in the twenty seconds a lens spends unprotected, not during wear.

Telling haze from dirt

Before assuming the coating has failed, clean the lens properly and look again under a bright light against a dark background. Dirt, skin oil and dried cleaning spray scatter light and mimic haze. A genuine abrasion haze remains after cleaning, is even across the area that gets wiped, and is usually worse in the centre of the lens than at the edge, because that is where the cloth spends most of its time. A crazed coating looks different again: a fine crack network, often radiating from the edge or from a drilled hole.

Substrate hardness also matters. The same coating formulation performs differently on different lens materials, which is why two lenses bought in the same order can age at different rates if one is a higher-index material with a softer surface.

When to go back to the optician

Return within the warranty period if you see a network of fine cracks — crazing — that appeared without a handling event, if the coating is lifting or discoloured at the edge, or if both lenses developed the same central haze over a few months. Those patterns suggest a coating defect or an incompatibility between the frame and the lens, not wear. A single lens with a discrete scratch, or even haze that accumulated over two years of cleaning, is ordinary use, and the honest answer is a replacement rather than a claim.
