Modulus and Water Content: The Two Numbers That Decide How a Soft Lens Feels

October 27, 2026 · 8 min read

Two numbers that answer two different questions

A soft lens has to do three things at once: pass oxygen, stay wet, and behave mechanically like tissue rather than like a piece of plastic. Dk/t covers the first. Modulus and water content cover the other two, and neither is printed on the box in a form most wearers can interpret.

Modulus: stiffness, and why soft is a spectrum

Modulus, properly the elastic or Young modulus, is the ratio of stress to strain: how much force it takes to stretch a material by a given proportion. It is measured in megapascals, and across soft lens materials the practical range runs from draping to noticeably stiff. A low-modulus lens conforms to the cornea and the limbus. A high-modulus lens holds its own shape and transmits mechanical load to the tissue underneath.

That transmission is the clinical point. A silicone hydrogel needs a silicone phase to reach high oxygen permeability, and the silicone phase is what makes the material stiff. The same chemistry that solves oxygen transport tends to raise modulus, so a stiffer lens can irritate the lid margin or the conjunctiva while the cornea is perfectly oxygenated. Oxygen and mechanics are separate failure modes, and a lens can fail one while excelling at the other.

Modulus is also what you feel as lens awareness at the end of the day. A draping lens moves with the lid and deforms over the limbus; a stiff one resists both, and the resistance is what the wearer reports as a lens that is "there".

Water content: a percentage doing two jobs

Water content is the mass of water in the lens as a percentage of total mass. It matters twice over. Oxygen dissolves and diffuses through water, so in a conventional hydrogel the water phase is the oxygen pathway, which is why older high-water lenses were the high-Dk ones. Water also plasticises the polymer: more water generally means lower modulus and a lens that drapes more readily.

In a silicone hydrogel the first job is taken over by the silicone phase, so water content stops being an oxygen proxy and becomes mostly a comfort and handling variable. This is the commonest source of confusion when a conventional hydrogel and a silicone hydrogel are compared on water content alone: the same number is doing different work in each family.

Why Dk/t has a t in it

Permeability Dk describes the material. Transmissibility Dk/t describes the lens. Dividing by thickness is the entire point, because oxygen crosses the lens that exists, not a hypothetical slab of material. A material with Dk 100 in a lens 0.08 mm thick gives Dk/t of 125 in the units manufacturers quote. Halve the thickness to 0.04 mm and the same material gives 250. Change nothing but centre thickness and you have doubled the oxygen reaching the cornea.

That is why a high-Dk material in a thick lens can underperform a lower-Dk material in a thin one, and why a -8.00 D lens, thicker at the centre, needs a more permeable material than a -1.00 D lens to reach the same Dk/t.

What neither number tells you

Wettability, surface treatment, edge design, base curve and diameter are not captured by modulus or water content. Two lenses with identical Dk/t, modulus and water content can behave completely differently on the same eye because their edges differ by a fraction of a millimetre. Fitting stays an empirical process for that reason, and a lens that works for someone else is not evidence about you.

Three misconceptions

  • "Higher water content means a healthier lens." In a conventional hydrogel, high water meant high oxygen and also greater dehydration-driven parameter change through the day. In a silicone hydrogel, water content is largely a comfort variable. The same number means different things in the two families.
  • "Dk/t is all that matters for oxygen." It is the right metric, but only at the thickness supplied. A lens specified at 0.08 mm centre thickness on a -3.00 D eye is not 0.08 mm on a -9.00 D eye.
  • "Silicone hydrogel is always the better choice." It solves oxygen. It can raise modulus and change the wettability profile, which for some wearers is a worse trade rather than a better one.

When to raise it with your fitter

If a lens is comfortable for two hours and irritating by hour six, the useful information is not the brand but the numbers: material Dk/t, modulus, water content, base curve, diameter, and how the edge sits under the lid. Ask for them by name. A fitter who can quote them can usually explain the symptom; one who cannot is guessing.

None of this replaces an examination. Redness, pain, light sensitivity or a sudden change in vision are reasons to remove the lens and seek professional assessment, not reasons to change materials.

Where the published numbers come from

A specification sheet quotes Dk/t at a reference power, typically -3.00 D, with a stated centre thickness. That is a convention, not a measurement of your lens. Order the same material at -9.00 D and the centre thickness rises, so Dk/t falls even though the material has not changed. Similarly, water content is quoted at equilibrium hydration, not at the end of a fourteen-hour day when a conventional hydrogel has lost water to the tear film and its diameter has shrunk.

This is why two wearers of the same lens can report opposite experiences. The published figures describe a reference lens, and the lens on your eye is a different thickness at a different power on a different cornea.

The short version

Oxygen is one problem and mechanics is another. Dk/t answers the first, and only for the thickness you actually wear. Modulus answers how the lens behaves against the lid and the conjunctiva. Water content answers how the material holds water, which in a silicone hydrogel is mostly a comfort question and in a conventional hydrogel is an oxygen question. Three numbers, three separate jobs, and no single one of them decides whether a lens will suit an eye.
