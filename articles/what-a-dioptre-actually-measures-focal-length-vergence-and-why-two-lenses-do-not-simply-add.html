What a Dioptre Actually Measures: Focal Length, Vergence and Why Two Lenses Do Not Simply Add

October 25, 2026 · 8 min read

The number on your prescription is a reciprocal

A dioptre is defined as the reciprocal of a focal length measured in metres: F = 1 / f. That is the entire definition, and most of what is confusing about prescriptions follows from it. A +2.00 D lens focuses parallel light 0.50 m behind it. A −4.00 D lens sends that light to a point 0.25 m in front of it. A +10.00 D lens, the kind you meet in a magnifier rather than a pair of glasses, focuses at 0.10 m.

Because the relationship is reciprocal rather than linear, equal steps in dioptres are not equal steps in distance. Going from +1.00 to +2.00 D moves the focal point from 1.00 m to 0.50 m, half a metre. Going from +9.00 to +10.00 D moves it from 0.111 m to 0.100 m, eleven millimetres. This is why a 0.25 D error that is invisible in a reading lens becomes a real problem in a strong plus lens, and why lens tolerances are written as percentages above a certain power rather than as a single flat figure.

Vergence is what actually travels

Light does not carry a dioptre value around with it; it carries vergence, the reciprocal of the distance to the point where the wavefront would converge or diverge, again in metres. Vergence is written L = 1 / l, and its sign follows the direction of travel: converging light is positive, diverging light is negative.

A wavefront leaving a point source 1 m away has vergence −1.00 D. It holds that value until something changes it. A lens does not add power to the light, it adds vergence. Put a +3.00 D lens into that beam and the vergence leaving it is −1.00 + 3.00 = +2.00 D, meaning the light now converges to a point 0.50 m beyond the lens. That single rule, add the lens power to the incoming vergence, is all of paraxial optics for thin lenses — and it is exactly what a phoropter is doing when the examiner clicks in another 0.25 D.

Why two lenses do not simply add

Two thin lenses in contact do add: +2.00 D against +3.00 D gives +5.00 D. Separate them and the arithmetic breaks, because the second lens now meets light whose vergence has already changed across the gap.

The equivalent power of two lenses separated by a distance d in metres is F = F&sub1; + F&sub2; − d · F&sub1; · F&sub2;. Two +10.00 D lenses 50 mm apart give 10 + 10 − 0.05 × 100 = 15.00 D, not 20.00 D. At high powers and real separations that correction term is not a rounding artefact, it is the dominant term.

Vertex distance does precisely this to your own prescription. A −10.00 D spectacle lens sitting 13 mm from the cornea is not delivering −10.00 D to the eye. It delivers about −8.85 D at the corneal plane, which is why a contact lens prescription for the same eye is written weaker than the spectacle prescription. The gap between lens and eye is a lens-free space that changes vergence, and the formula above is how you price it.

Where the dioptre stops being exact

Everything above assumes thin lenses and paraxial rays, meaning rays close to the optical axis. Real lenses have thickness, and real eyes use a pupil several millimetres wide, so rays cross the lens away from the axis and at an angle. Those rays do not come to the same focus. The result is spherical aberration, coma, oblique astigmatism and distortion, and the dioptre value is a statement about the paraxial power at the optical centre rather than a promise about the rest of the lens.

Three things people get wrong

  • “A dioptre measures how strong a lens is.” It measures wavefront curvature after the lens, which is the reciprocal of a distance. Strength is a serviceable shorthand at low powers and a misleading one at high powers.
  • “More dioptres always means a thicker lens.” Power sets the curvature required. Thickness is set by refractive index, base curve, blank size and how far the optical centre must be decentred. Two −6.00 D lenses can differ in edge thickness by several millimetres.
  • “Two +2.00 D lenses stacked make +4.00 D.” Only while they are touching. Held 10 mm apart they make about +3.96 D; held 100 mm apart, +3.60 D.

Reading a prescription with this in mind

SPH is a vergence change. CYL is the difference in power between the two principal meridians, and AXIS says which meridian is which. ADD is a vergence change applied to the near zone only. PD, in the same spirit, is a distance in millimetres rather than a dioptre, and it exists so that the optical centres can be placed where the eyes actually look.

When a pair of glasses “does not feel right”, the productive questions are therefore geometric rather than qualitative: how far is the lens from the eye, where does the optical centre sit relative to the pupil, and how much prism does looking off-centre generate. When to stop calculating and get measured

Vergence arithmetic explains why one prescription behaves differently in a different frame, but it cannot tell you whether your eyes are comfortable. If a new pair is sharp in the centre and soft at the edges, or clear straight ahead and doubled when you glance sideways, the cause sits in the geometry above and an optician can measure it in minutes. If vision is blurred everywhere, or one eye is worse than the other, that is a refraction or eye-health question rather than an optics one.

Why the step is 0.25 D, and why a prism dioptre is a different unit

Refraction steps in 0.25 D because that is roughly the smallest change most people reliably notice in a comparison test, and because trial lens sets physically run out of room before finer steps become useful. The step is a compromise between clinical resolution and manufacturing reality, not a law of optics. It also means that any calculation you do with a prescription is already quantised: the “ideal” power for your eye may sit between two available values.

The prism dioptre, which appears on any prescription carrying prism, shares the name and nothing else. One prism dioptre displaces an image by 1 cm at 1 m — it is a statement about angular deviation, defined as 100 × tan θ, not about focal length. The two units are not convertible, which is why a lens can hold +2.00 D of power and 2 Δ of prism at the same time without either number affecting the other.

The one-line summary worth remembering

Power is curvature of a wavefront, expressed as a reciprocal distance. Everything else — what happens when lenses separate, why thick lenses read differently front and back, why a strong minus lens minifies — is bookkeeping on top of that one relationship.
