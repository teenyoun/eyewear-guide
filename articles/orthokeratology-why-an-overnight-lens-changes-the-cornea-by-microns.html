Orthokeratology: Why an Overnight Lens Changes the Cornea by Microns

November 09, 2026 · 8 min read

What orthokeratology actually claims

Orthokeratology, almost always shortened to ortho-K, is the fitting of a rigid gas-permeable contact lens that is worn overnight and removed in the morning, on the understanding that the wearer will see without correction for much of the following day. The lens is not an ordinary rigid lens. It is a reverse-geometry design: instead of one base curve that follows the cornea, the back surface carries a central optic zone deliberately flatter than the corneal apex, a steeper reverse curve that lifts the lens away and forms a tear reservoir around it, and an alignment zone that rests on the mid-periphery and holds the whole thing steady.

Everything that follows depends on that shape. A lens that merely sat on the cornea would do nothing except correct vision while it was in place. A lens with a flat centre and a relieved mid-periphery sets up a pressure and tear-film gradient across the corneal surface, and the corneal epithelium — the living, renewable front layer, roughly 50 micrometres thick at the apex — responds to it by redistributing.

Where the dioptres come from

Refractive power of the front surface is set by curvature through a relation any optometry student can write from first principles: power in dioptres equals the refractive index difference divided by the radius of curvature in metres. For the cornea, with an index of about 1.376 against air, a radius of 7.8 mm gives roughly 48 dioptres, and a radius of 8.3 mm gives roughly 45.

So flattening the central cornea by half a millimetre of radius is worth about three dioptres of myopia correction. Half a millimetre sounds like a lot of tissue until you do the geometry. Over a 6 mm optical zone, the sagitta — the depth of the curve from centre to edge — falls from about 0.60 mm to about 0.56 mm as that radius change happens. The difference is roughly 40 micrometres. Less than the thickness of the epithelium itself.

That is the entire budget an ortho-K lens has to work with: a differential of a few tens of micrometres distributed across several millimetres of cornea. It is also why the effect is genuinely delicate. The tissue being moved is the outermost layer of the eye, it is being moved by a lens sitting on a film of tears a few micrometres thick, and the result is measured in the same units.

The precise force balance is still argued about in the literature. Compressive loading from the lens, the squeezing of the tear reservoir under the closed lid, and the epithelium's own tendency to migrate into regions of lower pressure have all been proposed, and imaging studies using optical coherence tomography find the same signature regardless: central epithelium thinned, mid-peripheral epithelium thickened, total central corneal thickness reduced by only a few micrometres. The observable pattern is consistent even where the explanation is not settled.

Why the mid-periphery matters more than the centre

Correcting the centre is the easy part and the least interesting part. The reason ortho-K is discussed at all in children is what happens outside the treated zone. If you flatten the central cornea but leave the mid-periphery steeper, rays entering the periphery are focused in front of the retina while the central rays land on it. This is peripheral myopic defocus, and it is the signal that a growing eye appears to read as an instruction to slow down.

The evidence for that is indirect but not trivial: randomised trials of myopia control strategies that create peripheral myopic defocus have generally found less axial length growth than single-vision spectacles over two to three years. The effect is real but modest, it varies between studies and between children, and it is not a cure. A child wearing ortho-K is still a myopic child; the goal is a smaller final prescription and a lower lifetime risk of retinal problems, not an absence of myopia.

What the routine actually demands

  • Wear is typically overnight, in the region of six to eight hours. Longer is not better; the cornea needs its daytime oxygen supply back.
  • The effect is reversible. Stop wearing the lenses and the cornea returns towards its original shape over days to a couple of weeks. That is a safety feature and also the reason the effect has to be maintained every night.
  • Fitting is iterative. The first lens is a starting point; topography after the first few nights tells the fitter whether the treatment zone is centred and whether the flattening is where it was intended.
  • Hygiene is not optional. The lens is in contact with a closed, warm, low-oxygen environment for hours, which is precisely the setting in which a contaminated lens becomes a corneal infection.

Common misconceptions, corrected

Ortho-K cures myopia. It does not. It temporarily changes corneal curvature. The eye's axial length — the thing that actually defines myopia — is unaffected by the lens itself.

It is just a hard lens worn at night. Conventional rigid lenses are made to correct while worn. Ortho-K lenses are made to change the cornea while worn and be removed. Putting a conventional rigid lens in overnight does not produce the same result and is not a substitute.

Any lens can be bought and tried. Reverse-geometry lenses are medical devices fitted against measured corneal topography, with a treatment zone that has to be centred on the pupil to within a fraction of a millimetre. A lens ordered from a photograph of someone else's eye is a foreign body with a designed pressure point.

Children are too young for it. Age is less important than the child's ability to cooperate with insertion, removal and cleaning, and the family's ability to keep the routine. Many practices fit children of primary-school age for exactly the reason the treatment exists.

Once it is working, the follow-ups can stop. The follow-ups are what detect a decentred treatment zone, corneal staining and epithelial problems before they become symptoms.

When to stop wearing and call the practice

Remove the lens and telephone the practice the same day if you have eye pain rather than mild discomfort, redness that does not settle within an hour of waking, unusual light sensitivity, discharge, or vision that stays blurred or hazy after the lens has been out for a while. Microbial keratitis in contact lens wearers is uncommon but it is the reason every instruction about cases, solutions and tap water exists; it progresses faster than most people expect and it is treatable when it is seen early.

Orthokeratology is a legitimate, well-established option with a specific and narrow mechanism. It works because a well-made lens moves a few tens of micrometres of epithelium, not because it pushes the eye into a new shape. Understanding that scale is the best protection against both inflated expectations and careless routines.
