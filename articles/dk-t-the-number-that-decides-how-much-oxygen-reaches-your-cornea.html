Dk/t: The Number That Decides How Much Oxygen Reaches Your Cornea

October 22, 2026 · 8 min read

﻿

The cornea has no blood supply to speak of

Most tissues get oxygen from capillaries. The cornea does not: it is avascular by design, because transparency requires an absence of blood vessels in the path of light. Its oxygen comes from the atmosphere, dissolved in the tear film, and a contact lens is therefore a barrier placed directly across a supply line that has no alternative route. Everything that follows — oedema, neovascularisation, the numbers on a lens box — is a consequence of that anatomy.

Three symbols that get confused

Dk is oxygen permeability: how readily a material lets oxygen diffuse through it, expressed in a unit called the barrer and conventionally multiplied by ten to the minus eleven. It is a property of the material alone, so it does not change when the lens is made thicker.

t is the centre thickness of the lens, in centimetres. It is not a fixed property; it depends on the prescription and the design, because a strong minus lens has to be thicker at the edge and a strong plus lens thicker in the centre.

Dk/t is oxygen transmissibility: permeability divided by thickness. This is the number that actually describes how much oxygen reaches the cornea through the lens you are wearing. Two lenses of the same material can have different Dk/t purely because one is thicker. A material with a high Dk can still underperform if it is made thick enough, which is why Dk/t is the number worth comparing and Dk alone is not.

Where the clinical thresholds come from

The familiar numbers trace back to Holden and Mertz, whose 1984 analysis linked oxygen transmissibility to corneal swelling and proposed critical values: a minimum Dk/t of about 24 for daily wear with the eyes open, and about 87 for overnight wear, both in the conventional ten-to-the-minus-nine units used clinically. Later review work argued that the extended-wear figure was too low and should be revised upward, to at least 125, which is one reason current practice leans towards silicone hydrogel materials for anything approaching overnight use.

The mechanism behind the threshold is osmotic. When the cornea is deprived of oxygen it switches to anaerobic metabolism, lactate accumulates, and water is drawn in. The result is corneal oedema: a swollen, slightly clouded cornea that scatters light, which the wearer experiences as hazy vision on waking, haloes around lights, or a lens that suddenly feels tight. Chronic hypoxia is worse than acute because the cornea eventually responds with neovascularisation — vessels growing in from the limbus toward a tissue that was designed never to have them.

Why water content is a misleading headline

In conventional hydrogel lenses, oxygen moves dissolved in water, so a higher water content generally meant more oxygen. Silicone hydrogels broke that relationship: they transmit oxygen through a silicone-rich polymer phase, so some of the highest-Dk materials on the market are comparatively low in water. Judging a modern lens by its water content, or by how "breathable" it feels, is therefore no longer informative. The material class and the measured Dk/t are.

Misconceptions

  • "Dk and Dk/t are the same number." One describes the material, the other describes the lens as made. Only the second tells you what the cornea receives.
  • "Higher water content always means more oxygen." True for conventional hydrogels, unreliable for silicone hydrogels, and irrelevant if the lens is thick.
  • "If it feels comfortable, the oxygen supply must be adequate." Comfort and metabolism are different signals. Hypoxia can develop in a lens that feels unremarkable, and the classic signs — limbal redness, faint vessel growth, morning haze — appear well before discomfort does.
  • "Overnight wear is just a convenience decision." Closed-eye wear removes the atmosphere as an oxygen source and raises the requirement several-fold. The threshold is a physiological limit, not a marketing preference.

What to ask, and when to stop wearing them

Ask which material class a lens belongs to and what its Dk/t is at your prescription, because thickness varies with power and a toric or high-minus lens is thicker than the same material in a low prescription. Follow the recommended wearing schedule rather than testing your own tolerance, and remove lenses at the first sign of redness, pain, light sensitivity or blurred vision that does not clear after removal. Those symptoms can indicate infection rather than hypoxia, and the difference is not one to diagnose at home.

The trade-offs that come with high permeability

Silicone hydrogel materials solved the oxygen problem and introduced a different set, because the silicone phase that carries oxygen also changes the mechanical and surface behaviour of the lens. Higher silicone content generally raises stiffness, and a stiffer lens can interact differently with the lid and with the cornea, which some wearers notice as awareness on blinking rather than as discomfort. Lipid deposition also tends to be greater, because lipids are more attracted to a hydrophobic phase, so the cleaning routine matters more than it did with conventional hydrogels.

None of this argues against high-Dk materials; it argues against treating Dk/t as the only variable. A lens has to transmit oxygen, stay wet, hold its shape on the eye and remain comfortable across a working day, and those requirements pull in different directions. When an optometrist selects a material, they are balancing them for your eye and your wearing pattern, not maximising a single number on a specification sheet.
