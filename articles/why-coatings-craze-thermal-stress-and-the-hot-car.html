Why Coatings Craze: Thermal Stress and the Hot Car

October 25, 2026 · 8 min read

What Crazing Looks Like

Crazing is a network of hairline cracks inside the coating rather than across the lens body. In reflected light it reads as a fine mesh, a frosted patch, or a faint milky sheen that shifts as the lens tilts; in severe cases the mesh becomes visible against a bright background and scatters a little light toward the eye. It differs from a scratch, which is a single linear groove, and from delamination, where a film lifts in sheets or flakes away from an edge. The three have different causes and different remedies, and confusing them leads to the wrong conclusion about who is at fault.

Almost every modern prescription lens is a composite. A typical stack on a plastic substrate carries a hard coat for abrasion resistance, an anti-reflection stack of alternating high- and low-index inorganic oxide layers, and a hydrophobic and oleophobic top layer that repels water and sebum. Each layer is deposited or cured under different conditions and each has its own coefficient of thermal expansion — the fractional change in length per kelvin of temperature change. Heat the assembly and cool it again, and the layers cannot expand and contract in step.

Why a Coated Lens Is a Stressed Multilayer

Think of the stack as bonded layers that must share a single in-plane dimension at every temperature. If the substrate wants to grow more than the film above it, the film ends up in compression after cooling; if the substrate wants to grow less, the film ends up in tension. Either way, residual stress stays locked into the interface at room temperature. The mismatch strain can be estimated to first order as Δε ≈ Δα × ΔT — the difference in expansion coefficients multiplied by the temperature excursion.

The oxides used in an anti-reflection stack are ceramics with expansion coefficients of a few parts per million per kelvin, while the organic substrate and the hard coat expand several times faster, in the tens of parts per million per kelvin. That is a large mismatch by thin-film standards, and brittle ceramics tolerate very little elastic strain before they crack. Manufacturers manage it through layer thickness, deposition conditions and intermediate layers, which is why a sound coating survives years of ordinary use.

A Worked Example Using Assumed Values

The numbers below are a hypothetical illustration, not a measurement of any product. Take a mismatch of Δα = 20 × 10⁻⁶/K between two layers and an excursion of ΔT = 60 K — roughly the difference between a lens left on a dashboard in summer sun and one returned to an air-conditioned room. Then Δε ≈ 20 × 10⁻⁶ × 60 = 1.2 × 10⁻³, about 0.12 percent strain. What matters is the shape of the relationship rather than the digits: the mismatch strain scales linearly with both the expansion difference and the temperature swing, so a hotter car or a more poorly matched pair of materials produces proportionally more stress. The calculation is not a diagnosis, and a crazed lens does not by itself prove that the coating was defective.

Scratches, Edges, Heat and Humidity

Stress concentrates at discontinuities. A scratch cuts through the hard coat and exposes an interface, and the crack tip at the bottom of the groove amplifies local stress, so crazing often begins along an existing scratch or radiates from it. Edges behave similarly: the coating terminates there, the interface meets air and moisture, and the geometric step concentrates stress. That is why crazing frequently appears first near the rim or around a mounting point, and why a lens that has been wiped dry with grit on it is more likely to craze later.

Heat and moisture act together rather than separately. Absorbed water swells the hard coat and softens its mechanical response; bonds at the interface can slowly hydrolyse; and repeated heating and cooling pumps the mismatch stress up and down, which grows a crack far more effectively than a constant load. A hot rinse under a tap also produces a steep through-thickness temperature gradient, bending the lens briefly and adding a second stress component on top of the first.

Four Misconceptions Worth Retiring

  • "Crazing means the coating is cheap or the lens is counterfeit." Crazing is a stress and environment phenomenon. Thermal cycling, hot water, aggressive solvents and dry wiping all contribute, and a well-made coating can craze under the wrong treatment.
  • "Wiping with alcohol is harmless." Alcohol dissolves skin oil efficiently, but it also attacks some hard coats, creeps under edges, and can accelerate cracking in a film already under tension. The maker's instructions should decide; where they are silent, lukewarm water with a drop of mild soap is the safer habit.
  • "A damaged coating always means new lenses." Some coatings can be stripped and re-deposited, but the process costs close to a replacement lens, carries risk to the substrate, and is not offered everywhere. Replacement is usually the economic answer — a choice, not a rule.
  • "Glasses are safe in the car if they are in a case." A case slows heat transfer and blocks sunlight; it does not stop the interior from reaching high temperatures. It protects against impact, not against thermal cycling.

Prevention That Actually Helps

  • Take glasses out of the car rather than storing them on the dashboard or in the glovebox.
  • Never rinse coated lenses in hot water; use lukewarm water and a mild soap, then blot with a clean microfibre cloth instead of scrubbing.
  • Rinse off grit before any wiping, and wash the cloth regularly — trapped particles cause the scratches that later become crazing origins.
  • Follow the cleaning instructions for the specific coating, and avoid ammonia-based household glass cleaners.

When to Take the Lens Back

Return to the practice or the seller if crazing spreads across a large area within a short period of ordinary wear, if the coating lifts or peels at an edge, if the surface feels rough or hazes in a way that affects vision, or if a deformed frame has left the lenses sitting badly. Widespread crazing soon after dispensing is worth documenting with photographs and the purchase date, because it may point to handling or manufacturing rather than to wear. If vision is blurred, doubled, or the eyes ache, that is a question for the optometrist rather than a question about coatings.
