Optical Transposition: Why the Same Lens Can Be Written Two Ways

October 11, 2026 · 9 min read

Two ways to write one lens

Hand the same finished lens to two opticians and ask them to write its prescription, and you may get two answers that look completely different. One might read +2.00 −1.00 × 180. The other might read +1.00 +1.00 × 90. Both describe the identical piece of glass. Neither is more correct, and neither will give you different vision.

The process that converts one into the other is called transposition. It is pure bookkeeping: it changes how a lens power is written, not what the lens does. That distinction sounds academic until you are holding two documents that do not match and trying to work out whether someone made a mistake. Usually nobody did.

Why any lens needs two numbers

A simple magnifying lens has a single power and can be described with one number. An eye that is astigmatic needs more than that, because it focuses light differently in different directions across the same pupil. Picture the eye as having two principal meridians — the two perpendicular directions of steepest and flattest curvature. Those two directions may need different corrections, and a lens that must supply two powers at right angles is a spherocylindrical lens.

Describing such a lens takes three quantities:

  • Sphere — the base power that applies in one principal meridian.
  • Cylinder — how much extra power the opposite meridian needs, relative to the sphere.
  • Axis — the direction, in degrees, in which that cylinder acts.

Here is the point that makes transposition possible: the sphere and cylinder are not two independent powers sitting side by side. They are a difference measured from a starting meridian. So you can choose to describe the lens starting from either meridian. Start from meridian A and the cylinder comes out positive; start from meridian B and it comes out negative. The underlying optics never move.

The rule, in three steps

Converting between the two conventions follows a fixed procedure:

  • Add the cylinder to the sphere. The result becomes the new sphere. This preserves the meridian that carried the full power.
  • Reverse the sign of the cylinder. A −1.00 cylinder becomes +1.00, and vice versa.
  • Rotate the axis by 90 degrees. If the axis is 90 or less, add 90. If it is above 90, subtract 90. An axis of 90 becomes 180, an axis of 180 becomes 90, and an axis of 5 becomes 95.

Applied to the example above: sphere +2.00 plus cylinder −1.00 gives a new sphere of +1.00; the cylinder flips to +1.00; the axis 180 becomes 90. That produces +1.00 +1.00 × 90, which is optically the same lens.

Two invariants are worth remembering because they let you sanity-check any conversion. The sum of sphere and cylinder stays constant — in the example, +2.00 added to −1.00 equals +1.00, and +1.00 plus +1.00 also equals +2.00. And the axis always moves by exactly 90 degrees, never by 45 or 180.

Why both conventions exist at all

Minus cylinder is the convention most modern refraction equipment and most automated lensmeters report, and it is the near-universal convention for contact lens prescriptions. When a phoropter or an autorefractor produces a reading, minus cylinder is usually what you see.

Where confusion actually causes harm

Transposition is a safe operation precisely because it changes nothing. Problems appear when it is done partially, or when someone tries to do it from memory.

The most common failure is rotating the axis in the wrong direction. An axis of 170 is not converted to 260; the rule caps the value at 180, so 170 becomes 80. Axes are recorded in a range that runs to 180 degrees and then repeats, because a cylinder at 180 and one at 0 describe the same orientation. Getting this wrong is invisible on paper — a converted axis of 260 looks superficially plausible — but it puts the astigmatic correction 90 degrees off, which is the worst possible error short of removing it entirely.

The second common failure is forgetting to flip the cylinder sign while also adjusting the sphere. Changing only the cylinder sign while leaving the sphere alone does not transpose the lens; it describes a genuinely different lens. This is the single most damaging shortcut, because the result reads like a valid prescription.

Common misconceptions

  • "The two prescriptions must be different corrections." They are one correction written twice. If the conversion was done correctly, the lens that arrives will be identical in power, axis and thickness.
  • "Plus cylinder means a stronger lens." The sign is a convention about where you start measuring, not a statement about magnitude. A −2.00 cylinder and a +2.00 cylinder can describe the same astigmatism in two different frame-of-reference conventions.
  • "The axis tells you which direction the astigmatism is in." The axis marks the meridian in which the cylinder has no effect — the meridian that already carries the sphere power. It is the meridian 90 degrees away that receives the full cylinder. Many people read the axis backwards and conclude their astigmatism is oriented the opposite way.

Sph and cyl are not the only things that can be rewritten

Prism and base direction. Prism is written with a magnitude and a base direction, and it too has more than one notational style. Prism is not transposable by the three-step cylinder rule; it lives in its own system. If a prescription contains prism, do not attempt cylinder transposition on the assumption that everything converts the same way.

What this means when you are checking an order

If the number on your new order confirmation does not match the paper in your wallet, the first question to ask is not whether one is wrong, but whether they are in the same convention. Check the invariants: does sphere plus cylinder come out the same in both? Does the axis differ by exactly 90 degrees? If both answers are yes, you are looking at one lens written twice, and there is nothing to correct.

If sphere plus cylinder does not match, or the axis differs by something other than 90 degrees, stop. Do not place the order and hope. Ask the prescriber or the dispensing optician to confirm which form is authoritative, because one of the two documents is then genuinely inconsistent.

When to ask a professional rather than work it out

  • Any prescription containing prism, or written with more than one prism component.
  • Any case where two documents disagree and you cannot reconcile them with the invariants above. The correct action is to get a single authoritative version, not to pick the one that looks more familiar.
  • High-powered prescriptions, where a transcription error compounds with thickness and weight decisions.
  • Contact lens prescriptions, which are written in their own convention and include base curve and diameter that have no counterpart in spectacle transposition.

None of this is a reason to be nervous about seeing a different-looking number than you expected. It is a reason to check it properly, in the way described above, and to ask when the check does not come out clean.
