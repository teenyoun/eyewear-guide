Sports Eye Protection: Why ASTM F803 Is Not ANSI Z87.1

October 21, 2026 · 7 min read

Two standards, two different jobs

Industrial safety glasses and sports eye guards both protect the eye, and the resemblance stops at the appearance. They are certified to different standards, tested with different projectiles, and evaluated against different assumptions about how the eye will be injured. A pair of ANSI Z87.1 safety glasses is excellent equipment for a workshop and is not a substitute for a sports protector, even though both are made of polycarbonate and both look robust.

What ANSI Z87.1 is for

ANSI Z87.1 is the American national standard for occupational and educational personal eye and face protection. It addresses impacts from flying debris, dust, chemical splash, molten metal, and optical radiation in a workplace, and it specifies marking requirements — the familiar "Z87" or "Z87+" stamp on the frame or lens. A high-impact-rated product has passed a projectile test, and the "+" means it was tested at the higher impact level rather than the basic one. What it does not address is coverage from the side and above during a basketball rebound, retention on the head during a sprint, or the direction from which a finger, elbow or ball arrives.

What ASTM F803 is for

ASTM F803, "Standard Specification for Eye Protectors for Selected Sports", is sport-specific by design. Rather than one test for all hazards, it sets requirements for particular activities — basketball, baseball and softball batting, field hockey, lacrosse, soccer and others — with impact conditions chosen to represent what happens in that sport. The current edition is F803-19. A product is described as compliant for a named sport, which is why the packaging usually lists sports rather than saying "sports approved".

Racket sports used to sit inside F803 as well. In 2019 ASTM published F3164-19, a separate standard for eye protectors used in racket sports, which replaced and superseded the older racket-sport requirements previously contained in F803. If you play squash, racquetball or tennis, the relevant current reference is F3164, and a product that cites only the older F803 racket language is working from a superseded document.

Why the difference is not bureaucratic

  • Direction of impact. Occupational hazards tend to arrive frontally; a basketball injury often comes from the side or from below, through the gap between lens and cheek.
  • Coverage. Z87.1 permits many frame shapes. Sports protectors must close the gaps — a lens that stops at the orbital rim leaves the eye exposed to a finger.
  • Retention. A protector that leaves the face on impact protects nothing. Sports designs use straps and flexible frames specifically to stay in place through contact.
  • Repeated use. Sports protection assumes the wearer will be hit, sweat, and move; the design has to survive that without fogging into uselessness.

Four claims that should be checked, not believed

  • "Z87.1 is fine for basketball." It is a workplace standard. It says nothing about lateral coverage during a rebound, and a safety frame with an open side is a poor choice for a sport played at close quarters.
  • "Polycarbonate is what matters." Material sets the impact ceiling, but geometry sets whether the eye is covered and whether the protector stays on. A well-shaped polycarbonate guard beats a premium lens in an open frame.
  • "My prescription inserts make it safe." An insert is not itself a protector, and a frame holding an insert is not automatically certified as a system. The combination has to be assessed as a unit, which usually means a purpose-built prescription sports frame.
  • "It says sports on the box." Marketing language is not a standard. Look for the standard number and the named sport printed on the product or its documentation.

What to do after an impact

Any protector that has taken a significant hit should be replaced, even if it looks intact. Polycarbonate absorbs energy by deforming, and a lens that has done its job may carry stresses that reduce what it can absorb next time. The same applies to a frame that has been bent back into shape. This is the one area where "it still works" is not a safe assumption.

Frames, straps and the paediatric case

Protection is a system: lens, frame, strap and fit. A wrap-style frame with a strap keeps the lens where it belongs during a jump or a rebound; a conventional temple arm does not. Venting matters too, because a protector that fogs is a protector the player removes. For children, the fit changes every season, and a guard bought two years ago is likely to be both loose and misaligned — the practical recommendation is to check coverage and retention at the start of each season rather than to assume last year's pair still fits.

It is also worth saying plainly that a protector reduces risk rather than eliminating it. Sports eye injuries happen through gaps a frame cannot close and through impacts a lens cannot absorb. The standard tells you the product was tested against a defined hazard; it does not tell you the hazard is gone.

When to involve an optometrist or optician

Talk to a professional before buying if you need prescription sports protection, if you have good vision in only one eye — where the threshold for protection is higher and a clinician's advice matters — or if you play a sport with a known high rate of eye injury such as squash or racquetball. An optician can also tell you whether a proposed frame can hold a prescription at your power without exceeding what the design allows, which is a fitting question that no standard answers for you.
