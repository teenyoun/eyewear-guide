Four-Point Touch: The Frame Alignment Checks That Decide Whether Glasses Feel Right

October 26, 2026 · 8 min read

What four-point touch actually tests

Put a pair of glasses on a flat surface with the lenses facing up. A well-aligned frame rests on four points: the two lower rims and the two temple tips. If it rocks, one of those four is out of plane, and the amount of rock tells you how far out. This is the oldest alignment check in optical dispensing, and it survives because it tests something a ruler cannot: whether the frame is still a rigid plane or has been twisted.

The four points matter because a frame carries two lenses that must sit at the same height and the same distance from the face. A rock of 2 mm at one rim means the two optical centres are no longer level with each other, and that is a prism problem rather than a cosmetic one. The relationship is Prentice's rule: prism in dioptres equals decentration in centimetres times power in dioptres. Two millimetres is 0.2 cm, so in a 4.00 D lens the misalignment produces 0.8 prism dioptres of vertical imbalance, and the wearer has to fuse it away every waking hour.

That is why a frame which looks fine in the mirror and rocks on the counter can still cause headaches. The rock is the visible symptom; the vertical prism is the mechanism.

The three angles behind the four points

Four-point touch is a planar test, but a wearable frame is not planar. Three angles determine how the lenses meet the face, and all three can be wrong while the frame still sits flat.

Face form angle is the wrap built into the front, the angle between the two lens planes when viewed from above. An optician's standard flat front is close to zero; a sport wrap can reach 15 degrees or more. Wrap looks harmless and is not: it adds base-in prism at the temporal edge of each lens and swings the optical axis away from the line of sight.

Pantoscopic tilt is the backward lean of the lens plane. A small amount, a few degrees, is normal and useful because it aligns the lens with the downward angle of gaze. Too much converts downward gaze into oblique astigmatism and changes the effective sphere.

Temple splay is the angle the arms make with the front. If the splay does not match the width of the head, the temples press outward, the front bows, and the face form angle changes as a result. This is why a frame that fits perfectly on the counter can distort on the face: the head loads the frame and the frame deforms.

What four-point touch cannot see

The test checks the frame against a plane. It says nothing about the relationship between the lenses and the eyes, which is where most real complaints originate.

  • Optical centre height. A frame can sit perfectly flat with both lenses 4 mm too high. The optical centre then sits above the pupil, and every downward glance runs through a part of the lens that was never designed for it.
  • Monocular centration. Four-point touch treats the two sides as symmetric. Faces rarely are. A 1.5 mm difference between the nasal and temporal distances, split evenly as though it were a single PD, puts a real horizontal error into each lens.
  • Vertex asymmetry. One lens 2 mm closer to the eye than the other changes the effective power on that side alone. At low powers this is invisible; above roughly 6 dioptres it is not.

Three things people get wrong

  • "If it sits flat on the table, it fits." Flatness is necessary and far from sufficient. It certifies the frame, not the fit.
  • "Temples should be tight so the glasses do not slide." A tight temple solves sliding by loading the frame, which bows the front and changes the face form angle. Retention should come from a correctly shaped temple bend behind the ear, not from clamping.
  • "Bending them back is easy, so I will do it myself." The adjustment is not difficult. Knowing where the optical centres ended up afterwards is, and that needs a reference mark placed before you start.

When to have the frame checked

Take it in if the frame rocks on a table, if one lens sits visibly closer to your cheek than the other, if the glasses slide forward when you look down, or if you notice a vertical double image that disappears when you close one eye. That last sign is the one people ignore longest, and it is the most informative: it points at vertical imbalance, which is measurable and correctable rather than something to adapt to.

Alignment is geometry, and geometry can be measured. If a pair is uncomfortable, the useful question is which angle or height is out, not whether you need to get used to it. For the thickness consequences of frame choice, our lens thickness calculator works from the same frame geometry.

How the check is done in practice

The bench test is the first step, not the last. After the frame passes on the counter, an optician places it on the face and marks the pupil centre on each lens, front and side, with a marker. The marks are then measured against the bottom rim of each lens. Equal distances mean the two lenses are centred at the same height; unequal distances quantify the error directly, which is why the marker method beats any visual judgement.

Vertex distance is checked with a rule held at the side of the lens; face form by laying a straight edge across the front; temple alignment by looking for daylight between both temples and the tops of the ears. Each check produces a number, and each number can be corrected. None of them appears in the four-point test, which is why the bench result alone should never be treated as a verdict.
