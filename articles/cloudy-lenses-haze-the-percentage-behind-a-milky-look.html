Cloudy Lenses: Haze, the Percentage Behind a Milky Look

November 17, 2026 · 9 min read

Clear is a measurement, not a look

Two lenses can transmit the same amount of light and still not look alike. One sends the light through in a straight line and forms a sharp image; the other spreads part of it in every direction, so the image arrives with a grey veil over it. Transmittance tells you how much light got through. It says nothing about whether that light still carries a picture.

That second property has a name and a number. Haze is the fraction of transmitted light that has been scattered rather than passed straight on, expressed as a percentage. It is the difference between a window and a sheet of tracing paper, and it is the reason a lens can be described as "clear" on a specification sheet and still look milky in a bathroom mirror.

How haze is defined and measured

The standard most optical and plastics laboratories work to is ASTM D1003, which covers haze and luminous transmittance of transparent plastics in one method. It is measured in a hazemeter built around an integrating sphere: the sample sits at the entrance, a light trap catches the unscattered beam, and the sphere collects everything that was deflected away from it. Total luminous transmittance is what passed through; haze is the scattered portion divided by the total, multiplied by a hundred.

The geometry contains a detail that matters more than it sounds. Light that deviates only slightly from the incident direction is not counted as haze — in the standard's arrangement, only flux scattered beyond a small angle, conventionally about 2.5 degrees, is included. So haze describes wide-angle scattering: the light that destroys contrast. A lens can have very low haze and still produce a slightly soft image, because narrow-angle scatter and aberrations sit outside the measurement. Clear, in other words, is not one number — it is one number plus everything the number leaves out.

Bulk haze and surface haze are different problems

Scattering can originate inside the material or at its surfaces, and the distinction decides whether anything can be done about it.

  • Bulk haze comes from the material itself: incomplete polymerisation, additives that did not disperse, moisture absorbed into a resin, or micro-cracks that developed through the thickness after years of thermal cycling. It cannot be cleaned off, because it is not on the surface.
  • Surface haze comes from the interfaces: a coating that has crazed into a fine network of cracks, a film that has begun to lift at its edge, abrasion from dry wiping, or simply a layer of skin oil, sunscreen and soap residue filling the microscopic texture of the surface.

Those have very different prognoses. Residue can be removed. Crazing and delamination cannot be reversed, and abrasive polishing makes both worse. Bulk haze is permanent.

Why a cloudy lens usually looks blue-grey

Scattering depends on the size of the scatterer relative to the wavelength of light. When particles are much smaller than a wavelength, scattering rises steeply as wavelength falls — the same relationship that makes the sky blue — so the short end of the visible spectrum is thrown off course far more than the long end. The result is a lens that looks faintly blue or violet when you hold it against a dark background.

When the scatterers are comparable to or larger than a wavelength — clumps of residue, crazed coating, actual micro-fractures — the wavelength dependence flattens and the scattered light stays white. A lens that looks milky-white against a lamp and faintly blue against a dark table is telling you something about the size of what is scattering inside or on it.

What actually happens to a pair over time

Three mechanisms account for most of the cloudiness people notice. Heat is the first: a car interior in summer reaches temperatures that stress a coating far beyond anything the lens sees on a face, and the resulting micro-cracking scatters light long before it is visible as damage. Chemistry is the second: repeated contact with solvents that the coating was never formulated for, including some glass cleaners and alcohol-based wipes used daily. Abrasion is the third and the most ordinary — wiping a dry lens with a paper towel or the corner of a shirt grinds dust across the surface and leaves a fine scatter pattern that no cloth will remove afterwards.

Residue deserves its own mention because it imitates all three. Sunscreen, hairspray, chlorinated water and hand cream leave a film that scatters light convincingly, and it comes off with warm water, a drop of mild soap and a clean microfibre cloth. Before concluding that a pair is damaged, it is worth washing one lens properly and comparing the two.

Common misconceptions

"Cloudy means the coating has worn out." Sometimes. Just as often the lens is dirty, or the haze is in the substrate. The test is simple arithmetic: clean one lens and look at the pair against a dark background. If the two still differ, the problem is not on the surface.

"You can polish haze out." Polishing removes material, and on a coated lens the layer that scatters light is the layer you would be removing — along with the anti-reflective stack underneath. Toothpaste, baking soda and polishing compounds leave a lens that is optically worse than the one you started with, with a thinner coating and a new set of fine scratches.

"A higher-index lens is always hazier." Refractive index is a design choice, not a haze rating. Haze is set by the resin, the curing process and the surface, and two lenses of the same index from different suppliers can sit at different points on the scale. Index predicts thickness and chromatic behaviour far more reliably than it predicts clarity.

"Haze and yellowing are the same thing." They are separate measurements. Yellowing is a change in the transmission spectrum — the lens absorbs more at the blue end, and the world takes on a warm cast. Haze is a change in direction, and the world loses contrast without changing colour. A lens can yellow with almost no haze, and a lens can go milky while staying perfectly neutral in colour.

When cloudiness is a reason to see someone

Replace or have the pair examined if the haze appeared suddenly rather than over months, if it affects one lens and not the other, if you see halos or starbursts around lights at night that were not there before, or if the cloudiness sits in a patch rather than across the whole lens. A patch usually means local damage or delamination, and delamination tends to spread.

See an optometrist rather than the lens supplier if the change in clarity comes with a change in vision — if letters are less crisp at distance as well as through the smeared area, if one eye is consistently worse, or if the haze is accompanied by pain, redness or a scratch you can feel. Haze is a property of a piece of plastic. A change in how you see is a question about your eyes, and it deserves an examination rather than a new pair of lenses.
