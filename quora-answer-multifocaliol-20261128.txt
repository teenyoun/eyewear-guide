I have not had the implant myself, so I am not going to pretend to report what your night driving will feel like. What I can tell you is what the halo physically is, because it is geometry rather than luck, and knowing the mechanism tells you which complaints are normal, which fade, and which are worth a phone call.

A diffractive multifocal implant is not a lens that focuses better than a monofocal one. It is a lens that divides light instead of focusing it. Its surface carries concentric microscopic steps, each only a few micrometres high. Every step is a phase discontinuity, and light crossing it is diffracted into several orders rather than one converging wavefront. The step height is chosen so most of the energy lands in two orders: one converging at distance, one at a nearer focus. In a two-focus design the split is commonly quoted as roughly 40% to distance and 40% to near, with the remaining energy spread into higher orders that never come to a focus at all.

That leftover energy has to go somewhere, and where it goes is your halo. The unused light forms a defocused image of the pupil on the retina. Because it is the pupil being imaged, the halo is a disc rather than a point, and its angular size scales with how wide the pupil is. In bright light the pupil is small and the ring collapses toward the bulb. At night the pupil dilates and the same energy spreads into a ring wide enough to sit over the road ahead. The ring is not a defect added to the design; it is the design's arithmetic. A perfectly centred lens still produces it.

The second cost is less photogenic and matters more. Both images, distance and near, arrive at the retina at the same instant — simultaneous vision, not the alternating vision of a bifocal spectacle lens, where the direction of gaze picks the zone. With an implant there is no gaze direction to pick. So every object is seen through a veil made of its own out-of-focus twin. Your visual system is good at suppressing one image once the task tells it which matters, but suppression takes time and it is incomplete in low light, where the out-of-focus contribution grows as the pupil opens. That is why the complaints cluster: night driving, dim restaurants, small print under a lamp that is not quite bright enough. All of them are situations where contrast was already marginal before the lens added a veil.

On the timeline, most surgeons describe the same arc. Halos and glare are expected, inherent, and usually worst in the first weeks. They typically diminish as the neural system adapts, but they rarely vanish completely — the usual outcome is a residual ring that becomes ignorable rather than absent. The honest ranking of what to worry about puts low-light contrast first, because it affects detection of unlit obstacles rather than the appearance of lit ones. After that comes dependence on light level for near tasks: the near image is a real image, not a magnified blur, but it needs enough light to compete. Then residual refractive error, because half a dioptre of uncorrected cylinder sits on top of the diffractive design and degrades both images at once. That is why refraction is properly measured once the eye has settled, commonly around four to six weeks, not in the first days while corneal swelling and wound healing are still moving the numbers.

Some things people are told do not hold up. "The ring means the lens is off-centre" — centration matters, and a displaced diffractive optic induces coma and makes the halo asymmetric rather than concentric, but a centred lens rings too, so a halo alone is not evidence of a surgical problem. "Extended depth-of-focus lenses have no halos" — those designs stretch one focal region instead of creating two discrete foci and generally reduce dysphotopsia, but reduced is not absent. "New glasses will remove the halos" — spectacles correct residual sphere and cylinder; they cannot recombine diffraction orders or change how the pupil sets the halo diameter. And the ring is not a reflection off the lens surface: internal reflections at the edge of a square-edged implant are real but look different, localised arcs or a crescent near the periphery of the field rather than a concentric ring around every light.

Two practical notes. First, most dysphotopsia is a conversation, not an emergency; but get same-day assessment for a sudden shower of new floaters, flashes or a curtain across the vision, for pain, marked redness or light sensitivity in the weeks after surgery, or for a sudden drop in vision. Those are not halo problems and should not be managed by waiting to adapt. Second, for persistent halos that stay intolerable beyond the first few months, the useful next step is a refraction and a dilated examination by the clinician who knows the eye — not a pair of glasses bought online. Some residual error is correctable; some dysphotopsia is design-inherent and is managed by changing the light around the task rather than the lens inside the eye.
