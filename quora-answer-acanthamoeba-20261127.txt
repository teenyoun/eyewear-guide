Yes — and I swam in lenses for years, which is why the useful answer here is mechanical rather than scary.

The risk is not "water is dirty". It is that one organism in that water is built to survive the thing we assume disinfects it.

Acanthamoeba is a free-living amoeba found in soil, tap water, swimming pools, hot tubs, lakes and household plumbing — and in coastal water too, so "the sea is salty, it must be clean" is not a defence. Salinity is not a barrier for it. Under stress (drying, temperature change, starvation, disinfectant) it converts into a cyst: a double-walled, dormant structure that is markedly resistant to chemical and physical insult, including the chlorine concentrations maintained in swimming pools and hot tubs. That is the crux. Pool chlorine is dosed to control bacteria and to protect swimmers from waterborne gut illness. It is not a sterilant for amoebic cysts, and it was never designed to be. Water can be legally and hygienically acceptable for swimming and still contain viable Acanthamoeba.

Now add the lens. A soft contact lens is not an inert window: its surface adsorbs organisms and organic material. A lens worn under a closed lid — which is what a shower amounts to, and what a blink underwater amounts to — holds a thin film of water and debris against the corneal epithelium for a long time, in a microenvironment warmer and less oxygenated than the open eye. The organism does not need to swim into your eye. It needs to be delivered to the surface it colonises and then kept there, and the eyelid supplies the incubation step. That is why the exposure that matters is water contact while wearing the lens, not water in the same room, and why this disease is so strongly associated with lens wearers rather than with swimmers in general.

How rare is it? The US CDC estimates roughly one to two new cases per million contact lens wearers per year. Rare — but not random, and not a lottery you can reason about by feel. It is one of the few eye conditions in which a single everyday habit is a well-established risk factor and in which the habit is entirely optional.

The rules that follow are short. No tap water on lenses or cases: not to rinse, not to wet them, not to top up solution. That is the highest-value change available and it costs nothing. No lenses in showers, pools, hot tubs, lakes or the sea. If you are going to swim in lenses anyway, use daily disposables, wear sealed swimming goggles over the top, and discard the lenses immediately afterwards — and understand that this reduces exposure rather than eliminating it. The case is part of the system: rub it, rinse it with fresh solution rather than water, let it air-dry open, and replace it every few months. And treat care products as what they are: tested to ISO 14729, which sets log-reduction requirements against specified bacteria and fungi. Passing that test is a real, regulated standard of performance; it is not a sterilisation certificate for every organism in the environment, and no solution compensates for water entering the system in the first place.

The reason "I have done it for years with no problem" is not evidence is that this is a low-probability, high-consequence event rather than a dose you accumulate and can feel. Pool water quality varies by facility and by hour, a lens that feels fine tells you nothing about what is adhering to it, and the years of nothing happening are the normal case right up until they are not.

What should actually change your behaviour is the symptom pattern, because early Acanthamoeba keratitis is easy to mistake for something trivial. Take the lens out and be seen the same day if you have pain that seems out of proportion to how the eye looks, marked light sensitivity, a foreign-body sensation that does not settle, redness that worsens, blurred vision, or watering that continues after the lens is out. Keep the lens and the case for the clinician. Do not restart lens wear while you wait, and do not reach for leftover antibiotic drops — it is not a bacterium, treatment is a prolonged specialist regimen, and the outcome depends heavily on how early it is recognised. The textbook ring-shaped infiltrate is a late finding, not an early one.

So the short version: yes, people swim and shower in lenses constantly and mostly get away with it, and no, that is not the same as it being safe. The water rule is the one rule worth keeping if you keep wearing lenses.
