Short answer: a smaller frame moves the rim closer to the optical centre, and thickness at the rim grows with the square of that distance. Nothing about the lens itself has to change for the edge to lose a couple of millimetres.

The quantity that governs edge thickness is sagitta, the depth a curved surface drops between its centre and its rim. For the small apertures used in spectacles, a spherical surface of radius R falls by s = y squared divided by 2R over a distance y from its vertex, and the radius follows from the power and the refractive index: R = (n - 1)/F, in metres. A -5.00 D lens in ordinary 1.50 plastic therefore has a surface radius of 0.5/5 = 0.100 m, or 100 mm. At 30 mm from the optical centre that surface has dropped 900/200 = 4.5 mm; at 35 mm it has dropped 1225/200 = 6.13 mm. One and a half millimetres of extra material from five millimetres of extra radius, and the growth is quadratic, so the last few millimetres of frame size are the expensive ones.

Worked example, same prescription, two frames. Take -5.00 D in 1.50 with a 1.5 mm centre thickness. In a frame where the furthest rim point sits 28 mm from the optical centre, the added sag is 784/200 = 3.92 mm. In a larger frame that puts the rim at 36 mm it becomes 1296/200 = 6.48 mm, roughly 2.5 mm more material on an identical prescription. A real lens is a meniscus and the front base curve subtracts a fairly constant offset, so these are single-surface approximations, but the way thickness scales with rim distance is captured correctly.

There is a second effect people notice long before they measure anything: the optical centre is not the middle of the lens. Lenses are decentred so that your pupil sits behind the optical centre, which is what keeps unwanted prism out of the line of sight. On a minus lens, decentring pulls the thinnest zone toward the pupil and pushes the surplus material out to the temporal edge. That is why one side of the rim can be visibly thicker than the other, and why a lens that looked acceptable as a loose blank can look bulky once it has been edged into an asymmetric frame. Frame shape, minimum blank size and decentration all feed the same arithmetic.

Does a higher index rescue it? Partly. A higher-index material bends light more per unit of curvature, so a given power needs a flatter surface and therefore less sag. At 1.67 the same -5.00 D surface has a radius of 0.67/5 = 134 mm, and the sag at 35 mm falls from 6.13 mm to 1225/268 = 4.57 mm, about a quarter less, which reads as clearly slimmer in the frame. The gain is real but not free: higher index usually means a lower Abbe number, so colour fringing away from the centre increases, and several high-index materials are denser, so a thinner lens is not automatically a lighter one.

For plus powers everything mirrors. The edge is thin, the centre is thick, and centre thickness is driven by the same sagitta: a +6.00 D lens needs a centre thick enough to leave a workable edge all the way round the blank. That is why strong plus lenses get heavy in the middle, why they are sometimes lenticularised, and why high plus prescriptions also tend to look better in a smaller, rounder frame. The geometry is symmetrical; only the direction of the problem changes.

Two myths worth dropping. First, that a higher index always means a thinner lens: minimum centre thickness rules exist for impact safety, especially in polycarbonate and in plus powers, and they set a floor that no index can go below. Second, that an uneven rim means the laboratory ground the lens badly: at -5.00 D, if the optical centre sits 4 mm nasal of the geometric centre, the temporal rim will be a couple of millimetres thicker than the nasal rim, and that is geometry rather than error. A single thick point on an otherwise even edge, or a visible step at the bevel, is the thing worth showing to your optician.

Practical version: above roughly plus or minus 4.00 D, frame dimensions matter more than the lens brand. Three millimetres off the eyesize usually does more for the edge than an upgrade in index, and it costs nothing. If a new pair arrives noticeably bulkier than you expected, ask whether the optical centre was placed where you intended before assuming the lens was made wrong.
