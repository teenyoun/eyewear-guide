Peeling at the rim is the most common coating failure there is, and it almost never starts in the middle of the lens. It starts at the edge, usually along the bevel, and it usually has a chemistry explanation rather than a manufacturing one.

An anti-reflective coating is a stack of metal oxide layers — commonly titanium dioxide or zirconium dioxide alternating with silicon dioxide, four to seven layers — sitting on a polysiloxane hard coat a few micrometres thick, topped by a hydrophobic silane a few nanometres thick. Each of those layers fails differently, and the edge is where they are thinnest and most exposed.

Salt is the usual culprit for what you are describing. Sweat and sea spray leave sodium chloride at the rim, where the film is thinnest, and salt crystallises as it dries. Crystals grow, and a growing crystal lifts the coating off the substrate. That is why the first sign is a fine bright line around the perimeter that creeps inward over months, not a sudden flake in the centre. Rinsing in fresh water after any salt exposure and drying with a clean cloth, rather than letting the lens air-dry, removes the salt before it can crystallise.

Hard water works more slowly and looks different. Tap water carries calcium and magnesium bicarbonate; when a droplet dries, the salts come out as carbonate and bond to the silica-rich top of the stack. One dried droplet wipes off. A year of daily dried droplets leaves mineral sitting in the microscopic pores of the coating, and then every wipe drags an abrasive mineral across a comparatively soft surface. Water is generally called hard above about 120 mg per litre of calcium carbonate, and a lot of the world's tap water is above that.

Solvents and the wrong soap do a third kind of damage. The hydrophobic top layer is a thin silane and silanes do not enjoy repeated solvent exposure: occasional isopropyl alcohol is tolerable, daily strong alcohol or ammonia-based cleaner is not. High-pH detergent is a separate problem, because silica dissolves in strongly alkaline solution, so a soap at pH 11 causes slow cumulative loss even while the surface still looks clean. Sunscreen and insect repellent are in the same category — the UV filters and solvents in sunscreen, and DEET in repellent, attack both the lacquer and the silane. Sunscreen on the fingers and then a thumb on the lens to take the glasses off is a routine summer event and a routine cause of hazy patches that will not wash off.

Heat is worth separating out, because people misread it as peeling. A car dashboard in summer gets well above the glass transition of the lacquer, and the mismatch in expansion between hard coat and lens produces the fine network of cracks called crazing. Crazing is a crack pattern; peeling is a lifted film with a visible edge you can catch a fingernail on. No cleaning routine prevents crazing — only keeping the glasses out of a hot car does.

Why an earlier pair survived the same sea water is not mysterious. Coating stacks differ between laboratories and between generations, the top silane differs, and damage is cumulative — the pair that lasted had fewer total insults, or a harder top layer, or was replaced before the salt had done its work. It is not that the new pair is defective by default.

What actually protects the stack: rinse under lukewarm water before touching the lens, so grit leaves with the water instead of being ground in; one drop of neutral soap worked with fingertips rather than a cloth; rinse; dry with a clean microfibre. Never dry-wipe a dusty lens, never use hot water, never use paper towels or clothing, never leave the glasses on a dashboard, and store them in a case rather than a pocket. Vinegar is the one home remedy to skip: it does dissolve calcium carbonate, but it also attacks the oxide layers and the lacquer, and the etched patch it leaves is permanent.

If the peeling is at the edge and the lenses are inside their warranty period, ask your optician to look at it — coating defects within warranty are usually a manufacturing claim, and a photo taken in oblique light against a dark background shows the pattern far better than a description does.
