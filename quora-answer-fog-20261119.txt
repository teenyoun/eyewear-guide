Fog on a lens is condensation, and it is governed by one condition: the surface has to be colder than the dew point of the air sitting on it. Nothing about the lens is broken when it happens. Your breath leaves the body close to 37 °C and saturated with water vapour, so the air arriving at the lens carries far more moisture than room air can hold at 15 °C. The lens, having sat in the cold, is below that dew point, and the excess water has to go somewhere. It goes onto the glass.

What decides whether you can still see is not how much water lands there but what shape it takes. On a surface with a high contact angle, water beads into thousands of tiny droplets, each one a little lens that scatters light in every direction, and the world turns into a bright blur. On a surface with a low contact angle, the same amount of water spreads into a continuous film a few molecules thick, and light passes through almost undisturbed. That single distinction explains most of the products sold as anti-fog, and most of the advice that fails.

The physics also explains why the problem is worse in some situations. Going indoors on a cold day is the classic case: the lens is cold-soaked and the indoor air is warm and humid, so condensation starts within a second or two and clears only when the lens warms up. A face mask is the other case, and a more stubborn one, because the leak along the top edge of the mask sends a continuous stream of warm exhaled air straight up across the lenses. Polycarbonate and other plastic lenses conduct heat poorly compared with glass, so they warm slowly and stay fogged longer once it starts.

The fixes divide into two groups. The first changes the air: seal the mask across the nose bridge with a strip of tape or a moulded nose wire, lift the frame slightly off the cheeks so air can move behind the lens, and let the lens warm up before you need to see through it, which is why people push their glasses up into their hair on the way in from the cold. The second changes the surface. Anti-fog sprays and cloths are surfactants: they leave a molecular layer that lowers surface energy so water spreads instead of beading. They work, they are temporary, and they are easily destroyed by wiping the lens with a dry cloth or washing it with an abrasive soap.

Worth knowing that ordinary anti-reflective coatings are frequently hydrophobic by design, because a surface that repels water is easy to clean and resists smudging. That is the opposite of what fog demands, which is why a premium AR lens can fog as readily as a cheap one. Dedicated anti-fog coatings are hydrophilic and are a different chemistry, not a stronger version of the same thing.

Three practical notes. A trace of plain dish soap rubbed onto the lens and then buffed clear leaves a surfactant film that genuinely works for a few hours; use a drop, not a smear, and never rub a dry lens, because dust under the cloth is what scratches coatings. Do not use hot water to warm a lens: thermal shock can craze some coatings, and the crazing is permanent. And for sport or winter cycling, the geometry matters more than any treatment, since a vented wraparound with airflow behind the lens keeps the surface closer to ambient and stops the film forming in the first place.
