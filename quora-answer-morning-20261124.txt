Short answer: your cornea absorbs a little water while your lids are closed, and a couple of per cent of extra water is enough to scatter light before it ever reaches the retina. Nothing about your prescription or the length of your eye changed overnight. The pump that keeps the cornea dry restarts when you open your eyes, the extra water leaves over the next twenty minutes to two hours, and the image comes back.

The cornea is about ninety per cent stroma, a stack of collagen lamellae in a matrix of proteoglycans that binds water. Central thickness averages roughly 0.55 mm in an adult. Transparency is not the absence of structure, it is the regularity of it: the fibrils are spaced uniformly and well below the wavelength of visible light, so the wavelets they scatter cancel in every direction except forward. Add water, the spacing opens up, and the cancellation stops working. Forward transmission drops and side scatter rises. That is haze, and it is why the symptom feels like a veil or smoke rather than the simple defocus of a wrong prescription. Patients who say "it's foggy" and patients who say "it's blurry" are usually describing two different optical problems.

The water content is set by the endothelium, a single layer of cells about five micrometres thick on the back of the cornea. It does not regenerate in adults, and it runs ion pumps that pull fluid out of the stroma against the constant leak inward. It is metabolically expensive, which matters, because the pump needs oxygen and glucose and it is running against the conditions that sleep creates.

With the lids closed, three things change at once. Evaporation stops, so the tear film no longer carries heat and water away. Oxygen at the corneal surface falls sharply, because supply comes from the palpebral capillaries instead of the air. And local temperature rises slightly, which raises metabolic demand exactly when supply has fallen. The result is a small, temporary swelling, typically a few per cent of corneal thickness in someone who does not sleep in contact lenses, and the pump clears it once the eye is open and oxygen is available again. Most people are back to baseline within an hour or so. Blinking and normal tear flow speed it up.

Why does such a small change matter optically? Two effects run together, and the smaller one gets the blame. The thickness change alters the curvature of the front surface, so the power of the eye shifts a little, but the shift is modest. The larger effect is scatter: light that should have passed straight through is deflected at thousands of small refractive index discontinuities, so contrast falls before resolution does. That is why you can still read the clock across the room while it looks washed out, why lights carry halos in the first minutes after waking, and why the letter chart at an eight in the morning eye test can read 20/20 while the patient insists the vision is not right.

The tear film adds a second layer to the same story. After sleep it is thick, mucus-rich and irregular, and it takes several complete blinks to redistribute into a smooth optical surface. Both effects resolve on roughly the same timescale, which is why the first ten minutes after waking are the worst possible moment to judge a new pair of glasses, and why a refraction done early in the morning is not automatically comparable with one done mid-afternoon.

Contact lenses turn the normal version of this into an extreme one. Closed-eye oxygen demand is low, but so is the supply through a lens, and the Dk/t of a lens predicts how much swelling it produces overnight. A high-transmissibility lens worn on its intended schedule produces a little more than the no-lens baseline. Sleeping in a lens that was not designed for it, or wearing a daily lens past its replacement date with a compromised surface, produces considerably more, and that is the classic route to chronic edema rather than a one-morning curiosity.

Where the everyday explanation stops working: blur that now takes three hours to clear when it used to take twenty minutes is a trend, not a nuisance, and it can be the earliest sign that the endothelial pump has less reserve than it used to. Blur that lasts most of the day, that is far worse in one eye, that persists after blinking, or that follows cataract surgery or comes with a family history of Fuchs endothelial dystrophy points at the endothelium rather than at sleep. Pain, redness, light sensitivity or a change in the colour of the eye is a different list entirely and needs to be seen, not explained.

Practical version: judge a new prescription after half an hour of normal blinking, not on waking. If your morning vision matters, keep a note of how long it takes to clear, because the duration is the number that changes meaningfully over years while the blur itself stays the same. And if you sleep in lenses, that is the first variable to change before anyone starts rewriting your prescription.
