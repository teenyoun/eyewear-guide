Sunglasses: Choosing by Lens, Not by Brand

Updated October 27, 2026

A pair of sunglasses is three decisions stacked on each other, and only one of them is visible in a shop. The first is how much visible light gets through, which is what the category number 0 to 4 describes. The second is whether the lens filters by orientation, which is what polarization does and what makes glare behave differently from brightness. The third is which wavelengths survive, which is what a tint selects and what decides whether a scene looks clearer or just dimmer.

Brand, frame shape and price sit on top of those three and cannot compensate for getting them wrong. An expensive pair in category 4 is illegal to drive in, a very dark lens without a UV absorber is worse than no lens at all on a bright day, and a brown-tinted lens that flatters a photograph may flatten the contrast you needed on a green court. The articles linked on this page work through each layer with the actual numbers.

Start with the category number, because it is regulated

The number from 0 to 4 on a sunglasses arm is not marketing copy. It is a transmission band defined in the European standard EN ISO 12312-1, and it exists so that a driver, a skier and a welder can each pick a lens that suits the task and the law. Category 0 passes most of the light and is meant for fashion and indoor glare, category 1 and 2 cover ordinary daylight, category 3 is the general-purpose outdoor lens, and category 4 is for extreme brightness such as high altitude or open water and is prohibited for driving.

The number says nothing about ultraviolet. That is the single most useful fact on this page: transmission and UV blocking are separate properties measured by separate tests, and a lens can sit at category 3 while transmitting ultraviolet freely. Standards require UV blocking separately, but an unlabelled online purchase has no one checking, which is why the UV400 claim deserves to be read as a claim rather than an assumption.

Read the category breakdown for the transmission bands and the driving limits, and the UV400 explainer for why a dilated pupil behind a dark lens is the mechanism that makes an uncoated lens worse than none.

Polarization is a different mechanism, not a stronger one

A tint reduces every wavelength by roughly the same proportion. A polarizing filter reduces light by orientation: it passes one axis of vibration and blocks the perpendicular one. Glare reflected off a horizontal surface such as water, a wet road or a car bonnet is largely polarized along the horizontal, so a vertical polarizing axis removes it while leaving the rest of the scene almost untouched. That selective removal is why polarization makes a scene look calmer rather than darker.

The practical costs are worth knowing before buying. A polarizing filter can interact with liquid crystal displays, turning a dashboard, a phone or an instrument panel black at certain angles, and it can reveal stress patterns in laminated windscreens as mottled patches. Neither is a defect; both follow from what the filter does. The physics of reflected light article covers the angle dependence, and this one explains the screen interaction.

The tint chooses which wavelengths you keep

Once category and polarization are settled, the tint decides colour balance. Grey attenuates evenly and keeps colours honest, which is the right default for driving and for anything where a signal light or a team kit matters. Brown and amber pass the long wavelengths and cut the short ones, which reduces the veiling haze that scattered blue light creates and raises apparent contrast against green, sky and water. Green-grey sits between the two.

Gradient and mirror coatings change where the density sits rather than the overall colour: a gradient is darker at the top, which suits driving and reading instruments, and a mirror reflects a narrow band to reduce total transmission without changing the tint underneath. Both are covered in the tint guide, and the surface-level consequences for durability appear in the hard coat and scratch resistance article.

Photochromic, prescription tint, or clip-on

For a prescription wearer the question is not which lens but which workflow. A photochromic lens darkens in ultraviolet and clears indoors, which makes it the only option that adapts without a second pair, but it barely activates behind a car windscreen because laminated glass absorbs most ultraviolet. A prescription tint is optically the cleanest solution and does not adapt at all. A clip-on keeps one pair of glasses and adds a second surface to clean, which is a maintenance cost rather than an optical one.

The comparison with real numbers, including how dark a photochromic lens actually gets and how long it takes to fade, is in photochromic vs prescription sunglasses. Fit decides whether any of it reaches your eye

Ultraviolet arrives from above and from the side, not only from straight ahead. A frame with a small eye size and a flat front leaves a gap at the temple through which light reaches the eye around the lens, and that light is not filtered by the lens at all. This is why coverage and wrap matter more than the category number for anyone who spends hours outdoors, and why a category 3 wrap usually protects better than a category 4 pair of small flat-front glasses.

The same logic applies to children, whose eyes admit more ultraviolet per unit of body size and who are less likely to keep a poorly fitting frame in place. The kids roundup treats fit and retention as the primary specification rather than an afterthought.

Three misconceptions worth retiring

A darker lens protects more. Darkness is a comfort setting. Protection comes from the ultraviolet absorber and from the geometry of the frame, and a very dark lens with no absorber is the worst case because the pupil dilates and admits more of what the lens failed to block.

Expensive lenses are optically superior by default. Above a modest price the differences are in coating durability, frame material and fit, not in optical quality. A well-fitted inexpensive pair in the right category beats an expensive pair in the wrong one.

Sunglasses weaken your eyes. Dark adaptation is a temporary state, not a loss of function. Wearing sunglasses outdoors does not reduce your eye's capacity; it reduces the luminance your retina is adapted to, and that reverses within minutes of removing them. Frequently asked questions

Is a darker lens always more protective?

No. Darkness controls visible light transmission, which is a comfort and safety category question, while ultraviolet blocking comes from the lens material and its UV absorber. A lens can be very dark and block no UV at all, which is the worst combination because the pupil dilates behind it.

Do I need polarized lenses for driving?

Polarization removes the horizontally polarized glare reflected off a wet road, a bonnet and the dashboard, so it usually helps. It can also interfere with reading certain LCD displays and with some windscreen patterns, so test a pair on your own car before relying on it.

What lens category is legal for driving?

Category 0 to 3 lenses are used for driving, with category 4 excluded because it transmits too little light for road use. For night driving, only category 0 or a clear lens is appropriate.

Can sunglasses be worn over prescription glasses?

Yes, as clip-ons or fit-overs. The trade-off is a second optical surface to keep clean and a small increase in weight, not an optical penalty, provided the clip-on sits flat against the frame front.

Do cheap sunglasses damage your eyes?

A cheap pair that genuinely blocks UV400 is not harmful. The risk is an unlabelled dark lens with no UV absorber, which reduces visible light while leaving ultraviolet to reach the retina through a dilated pupil.
