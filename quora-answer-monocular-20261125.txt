Short answer: when the second image only appears with the glasses on, the glasses are the more likely author of it. Either the lens is genuinely making a second image, or the correction has raised contrast enough that a ghost your eye was producing all along has become visible. Night makes both worse, and the reason is partly your pupil and partly the kind of target you are looking at.

Start with the light source. A small, bright point on a dark background is the hardest thing in optics to look at cleanly, because any faint second image has nothing to hide behind. A streetlight, a headlight, a phone LED at arm's length. Under a cloudy sky at noon the same eye and the same lens produce the same ghost, and nobody notices, because the ghost falls on a bright background and the contrast is too low to register.

Now the lens itself. Every spectacle lens has a front and a back surface, and both reflect a few per cent of the light that hits them. Light entering the lens can reflect off the back surface, bounce forward to the front surface, reflect again, and travel on to your eye. It arrives slightly displaced, because the two bounces are separated by the thickness of the lens and the angle you are viewing through. That is a real second image, made by the lens, carrying a few per cent of the energy of the first. Against a bright point source it is perfectly visible as a faint duplicate, typically offset a millimetre or two at the plane of the lens, and it moves as your eye moves behind the lens because the geometry of the bounce changes with viewing angle.

This is exactly what anti-reflection coating exists to suppress. Uncoated, a lens reflects roughly four per cent per surface in CR-39 and closer to eight per cent per surface once you get to a high-index 1.74 material, and a minus lens in 1.74 has a genuinely thick edge. Coat it properly and surface reflectance drops well below one per cent, and the ghost goes away with it. Coat it badly, or let the coating craze and haze after a couple of years of cleaning, and the ghost comes back without the prescription changing at all. If the duplicate is a soft, low-contrast one and it is strongest around small lights, look at the lens under a desk lamp at a shallow angle before you blame your eye.

The second mechanism is off-axis power error. A lens only behaves as prescribed on its optical axis. Look through a point ten or fifteen millimetres away from that axis, which is what happens with a wide frame and a pupil that sits high, and the lens develops oblique astigmatism plus a power error. In a strong minus lens this is a few tenths of a dioptre by the time you are twenty or thirty degrees off axis, and a few tenths of a dioptre is enough to split a point source into two lines a couple of arcminutes apart. Tilt adds to it: a lens that is not perpendicular to your line of sight, because of extra pantoscopic tilt or a wrapped frame, has cylinder induced in it that your prescription never accounted for, and the amount grows with the square of the tilt angle and with the power of the lens.

There is a subtlety worth knowing about why your eye might have been doing this all along. An uncorrected myope does not see a clean point of light, they see a blur circle several arcminutes wide. Any small ghost is buried inside that disc. Put the correct prescription in front of the eye and the primary image snaps to a point, but the ghost does not disappear, it was never about the refraction. It simply stops being hidden, because now there is a sharp edge to compare it to. That is why people notice monocular ghosting for the first time in the weeks after a first pair of glasses, and why it feels like the glasses caused it.

Night supplies three amplifiers at once. The pupil opens from about two and a half or three millimetres in daylight to six or seven in a dim room, and aberrations of this kind do not scale linearly with pupil size, the defocus-like terms scale with the square and the higher-order terms with the cube, so a doubling of pupil diameter can multiply the visual effect several times over. The targets get brighter and smaller relative to their background, which is the ideal condition for a faint secondary image. And the tear film gets less cooperative in the evening: blink rate falls at a screen, indoor heating dries the air, and meibomian output is not constant through the day. A tear film that breaks up in three seconds instead of ten turns the front surface of your cornea into a weak irregular prism, which is a third source of a second image that also gets worse as the evening goes on.

Four checks separate these without any equipment.

Rotate the glasses ninety degrees, keeping your head still. If the ghost rotates with the lens, the lens is responsible. If the ghost's direction stays put relative to your eye, it is not.

Look through a different part of the lens, deliberately off-centre. If the ghost moves, changes separation, or appears only near the edge, you are looking at off-axis error or decentration, and the fix is centration and frame choice, not a new prescription.

Take the glasses off and look at the same light. A ghost that is still there, fainter but present, means your eye or tear film was already producing it. A ghost that vanishes completely means the lens was making it.

Blink firmly several times and look again immediately. If the duplicate clears for two or three seconds and then returns, the tear film is the cause and the glasses are innocent.

What actually helps, in the order it is worth trying: a properly applied anti-reflection coating in good condition, a frame whose tilt and face-form match how you actually wear it, monocular pupillary distance so the optical centre sits over the pupil rather than over the geometric centre of the frame, and a smaller eye size when the prescription is strong, because edge thickness is what makes both the internal reflection and the off-axis error worse. Do not overtighten a thin lens into a frame; flexure bends the surface and induces cylinder that was never prescribed.

Where this stops being a nuisance: a ghost that persists with the glasses off, that comes with halos or starbursts around lights, that is much worse in one eye, or that has appeared alongside a change in your prescription, points at the cornea or the crystalline lens rather than at the spectacle lens, and needs a slit lamp rather than a new pair ordered online. Keratoconus and early nuclear cataract both announce themselves this way, at night, with a large pupil, while the letter chart still reads 20/25. And the one thing not to do is confuse this with binocular double vision: if the two images only appear with both eyes open and collapse when you cover either one, that is a different problem with a different urgency.
