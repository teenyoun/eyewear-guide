You do not need an optician's tools to figure out your face shape — a mirror, a marker, and five minutes are enough. Getting it right matters more than most people expect: the same frame that looks sharp on an oval face can look heavy on a round one, and the difference is almost entirely about proportions, not trends. Here are three methods, from easiest to most accurate, plus the frame rules that follow from your result.

Method 1: The Mirror and Marker Method (Easiest)

Stand close to a mirror in even light with your hair pulled back. Using a washable marker or a bar of soap, trace the outline of your reflection's face directly on the mirror — forehead, cheekbones, jawline, chin.

Step back and look at the shape you drew:

  • Length roughly equals width, soft curves → round
  • Length noticeably greater than width, gently curved jaw → oval
  • Forehead and jaw similar width, angular corners → square
  • Forehead wider than jaw, pointed chin → heart
  • Cheekbones widest, narrow forehead and jaw → diamond

This method is quick and surprisingly accurate for the five main shapes. Its weakness: it captures your outline but not the fine measurements that separate, say, a soft square from a rectangle.

Method 2: The Photo Method (Most Accurate at Home)

Take a selfie at arm's length (or ask someone to take a photo from 1.5 meters) facing the camera directly, hair off your face, neutral expression, no glasses.

  1. Print the photo, or open it in any app that lets you draw lines (even a phone photo editor works).
  2. Draw four horizontal lines: widest point of the forehead, widest point of the cheekbones, widest point of the jaw, and the chin.
  3. Draw one vertical line from hairline to chin to get the face length.
  4. Compare: which horizontal line is longest? How much longer is the face than it is wide?

The widest zone tells you the most. Widest at the cheekbones with a narrow forehead and jaw is a diamond; widest at the forehead tapering to the chin is a heart; forehead and jaw nearly equal with a strong line is a square.

Method 3: The Card Trick (Real Measurements, No Printing)

Hold a standard credit card (85.6 mm wide) horizontally against your face and photograph yourself doing it — one photo at the temple, one at the cheekbone, one at the jaw.

Because you know the card's exact width, you can measure the photo in card units and convert: if your cheekbone span is 1.6 cards wide, that is roughly 137 mm (85.6 × 1.6). This gives you three real numbers to compare instead of a rough silhouette, which is what you actually need when ordering glasses online.

For temple width in particular, this beats guessing: frame width should roughly match your face's widest point, plus a few millimeters of comfort allowance. Frames narrower than your face press on the temples; frames much wider slide down.

Three Numbers Worth Knowing

1. Frame width. Should match your face's widest point within about 5 mm. If your temple span is 140 mm, look for 135-145 mm frames — printed on the inside of every temple arm as the first number (e.g. "52-18-145" means 52 mm lens, 18 mm bridge, 145 mm temple).

2. Bridge width. The gap between lenses should sit comfortably on your nose without sliding. A narrow bridge on a wide nose pinches; a wide bridge on a low nose bridge lets frames slip.

3. Temple length. 135-145 mm covers most adults; 150 mm+ suits larger heads. If your current glasses leave marks behind the ears, the temples are too short.

FAQ

Can I measure my face shape with a ruler? Yes — you need three widths (forehead, cheekbones, jaw) and one length (hairline to chin). The ratios between them identify the shape; absolute numbers matter mainly for frame width.

Do face shapes change? Your bone structure stays, but weight change, aging, and hairstyle shift the outline you actually present — re-check every few years, especially after a big change in weight.

What if I sit between two shapes? Most people do. Follow the rules for the shape that dominates your widest zone — that is where the frame has to fit.

The Bottom Line

Trace your outline at the mirror for a quick answer, use the photo method when you want confidence, and use the card trick when you are ordering online and need real millimeter numbers. Then choose frames that contrast with your dominant feature: curves to soften angles, angles to structure softness. Fit beats fashion every time — a well-fitted frame in last year's style looks better than a fashionable frame that presses your temples.
