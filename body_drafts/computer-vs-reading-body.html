Many people assume reading glasses work for computer screens. They don't — or at least, they shouldn't. The distance matters, and using the wrong strength can actually cause more eye strain than wearing nothing at all.

The Distance Problem

Reading glasses are optimized for a focal distance of 14-16 inches (35-40 cm) — the typical distance for books and phones. Computer screens are usually 20-26 inches (50-65 cm) away. That's 1.5x the distance, which requires approximately 0.50 to 0.75 less magnification than your reading prescription.

How to Calculate Your Computer Glasses Strength

Take your reading glasses strength and subtract 0.50 to 0.75:
If your reading strength is +2.00, your computer strength is approximately +1.25 to +1.50.
If your reading strength is +1.50, try +0.75 or +1.00 for computer use.

What About Progressive Computer Glasses?

Progressive (no-line bifocal) computer glasses — sometimes called office lenses or near-variable focus — are designed specifically for intermediate distances. The top portion is set for screen distance (20-26 inches), the middle for mid-range (arm's length), and the bottom for reading (14-16 inches). They're more expensive ($150-300 online), but eliminate the need to swap glasses at your desk.

The Optical Difference: Near Power Versus Intermediate Power

Reading glasses are single-vision lenses with one power ground across the whole lens — the near correction. Every part of the lens is tuned for something 14-16 inches away, and anything further is slightly blurred by design.

Computer glasses are built differently depending on what you buy. Non-prescription pairs sold as computer readers are still single-vision, just at a lower power matched to screen distance. Prescription versions often use a slightly different design where the power changes gradually across the lens, so the upper area is set for the screen, the middle for arm's-length work, and the lower area for close reading. The distinction that matters when shopping is whether the lens has a single intermediate power or a graduated corridor.

What Twenty Inches Does to Your Eyes

At 20-26 inches your eyes must still accommodate and converge, just less than at reading distance. Two things make screens harder than books even so. First, the eyes hold a fixed intermediate focus for long stretches instead of varying it, and sustained static focus is tiring. Second, blinking rate drops noticeably during screen work, which dries the surface of the eye and produces the burning, gritty feeling people describe as eye strain.

Changing focus regularly helps more than any lens coating. The common 20-20-20 rule — every 20 minutes, look at something 20 feet away for 20 seconds — targets exactly that problem.

Blue Light Filtering: What the Evidence Supports

Blue light filters are marketed heavily, so it is worth being precise about what is and is not established. Screen blue light is far weaker than daylight — you receive more blue light standing outside on an overcast day than from an entire day at a monitor. Systematic reviews, including a 2023 Cochrane review of blue-light filtering spectacle lenses in adults, have generally found only low-certainty evidence that they reduce short-term eye strain, and no good evidence that they protect the retina from screen exposure.

Where the case is somewhat stronger is sleep timing. Blue light is a primary signal your body uses to set its internal clock, and bright screens late in the evening can delay melatonin release and make falling asleep harder. A filter on an evening screen may soften that effect slightly, though dimming the screen and putting it down earlier is the more reliable fix.

Practical takeaway: if a tinted lens makes your eyes feel less tired, that is a legitimate reason to wear it — comfort counts. But do not pay a large premium expecting retinal protection that has not been demonstrated.

Buying Checklist

  • Measure your actual screen distance. Sit normally and measure from your eyes to the glass. Two inches of error is enough to make the power wrong.
  • Start half a step below reading strength. Move down 0.25 first, then 0.50, and keep whichever lets you read the smallest text on screen without leaning in.
  • Check the return policy before buying. If you are guessing at power, an exchange window matters more than a few dollars of saving.
  • Prefer an anti-reflective coating over a blue-light tint if your budget only stretches to one. Less reflected light means less squinting.
  • Consider a separate pair if you also read print for long stretches — one lens rarely serves both distances well.

FAQ

Can I just use my reading glasses at the computer?

You can, and many people do, but the power is usually too strong for a screen 20-26 inches away. The result is that you see clearly only by leaning in or tilting your head back, which creates neck strain on top of the eye strain.

Do computer glasses need a prescription?

No. If you have no distance prescription, over-the-counter computer readers work fine — they are simply a lower-power version of reading glasses. If you have astigmatism or a distance prescription, a proper prescription is worth it.

Is blue light filtering necessary?

Not strictly. The evidence that it reduces eye strain is weak, and the evidence for retinal protection from screens is absent. It may slightly help if you use screens late in the evening, but treat it as a comfort option rather than a health necessity.
