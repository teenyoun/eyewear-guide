Glasses accessories are the safest gift category in eyewear, and the most neglected. Nobody needs a measurement to receive a case, and almost nobody buys these things for themselves — they use whatever came in the box and then complain about it for two years. Everything here is under $30, and every item solves a specific, nameable annoyance.

Rather than list products, this guide is organised by the problem each accessory solves, what separates a good one from a bad one, and what it should cost. Where a deeper comparison already exists on this site, it is linked.

Cases: The One Place to Spend

A case is the only accessory standing between a $200 frame and a pavement. The distinction that matters is not leather versus fabric, it is rigid versus crushable. A clamshell with a hard shell and a soft lining absorbs pressure from a bag; a folding slip case does not, and it is also the thing that scuffs lenses because it presses them against the frame.

What to check before buying: does the case have room for a folded cloth (if not, the cloth ends up loose in the bag); is the lining lint-free; does the closure stay shut without needing two hands. If you want this decided for you, our comparison of case types and materials walks through which shells actually protect a frame.

Clip-Ons and Fit-Overs: Sunglasses Without a Second Prescription

For anyone who wears prescription glasses, the cheapest route to comfortable daylight is not a second pair of prescription sunglasses — it is a clip-on or a fit-over that goes over the pair they already own. Two forms exist and they behave differently.

  • Flip-up clip-on: clips to the frame, and the lenses flip up when you go indoors. Best for driving and for anyone who moves between indoors and outdoors constantly. It needs to match the frame's width and shape, so measure.
  • Fit-over (OTG) sunglasses: a larger sunglasses shell worn over the prescription pair. Bulkier, but it is genuinely universal and it also blocks light from the sides, which matters for driving and for light sensitivity.

The pitfall is the "universal" clip-on: one size does not fit prescription frames well, and a clip that lands on the lens rather than the rim is a scratch waiting to happen. Buy a clip-on matched to your frame shape, or a fit-over — not a compromise between the two.

Cleaning Kits: Small, Cheap, Constantly Used

Everyone under-owns these. A microfibre cloth has a working life measured in weeks if it is used daily, and a cloth washed with fabric softener stops absorbing oil and starts smearing it around. So the useful gift is not one cloth — it is a pack, plus a spray.

What to look for: cloths sold in multiples, a spray that is alcohol-free or explicitly labelled lens-safe, and a small brush for the crevices around nose pads and hinges, which is where the grit that scratches lenses actually hides. Our cleaning guide covers the routine that keeps coatings intact, and it is worth pairing with the gift because most lens damage is caused by wiping dry.

Straps, Retainers and Stands: Cheap Fixes for Daily Annoyances

Retainer straps do two jobs. For sport they stop glasses sliding down a sweaty nose; for children they are the difference between owning glasses and losing them. The types differ by how they attach: a silicone end-grip slides onto the temples and works with almost any frame, while a fabric band that wraps the temples is more secure but bulkier behind the ears. If sport is the use case, a strap plus a snug frame beats a strap alone — our guide to stopping slippage explains why fit comes first.

Desk and bedside stands exist to stop one specific habit: putting glasses face-down. Lenses rest on the table, grit gets pressed into the coating, and the scratch appears a week later with no obvious cause. A stand costs less than a lens replacement and removes the habit entirely.

Car visor clips are the most under-bought item here. Sunglasses that live in the car get used; sunglasses in a bag do not. A clip is the cheapest way to make sunwear actually habitual for a driver.

If You Would Rather Give a Pair

Accessories are the safe choice, but a cheap pair of UV400 sunglasses is a perfectly good gift too — the only rule is to buy a stated rating rather than a dark tint. Our under-$50 sunglasses picks cover the two specifications that matter and how to spot a listing that quietly omits a UV claim.

What Not to Buy

  • "Universal" clip-ons. They fit almost no frame properly, and a clip resting on the lens surface will mark it. Match the frame or buy a fit-over instead.
  • Ultrasonic or "professional" home cleaner gadgets. Genuinely useful for jewellers, unnecessary for glasses — the same result comes from lukewarm water, a drop of mild soap and a cloth.
  • Anti-fog products promising permanence. Anti-fog works by lowering surface tension, and that layer wears off with cleaning. A spray you reapply is honest; a "lifetime coating" claim is not.
  • Hard cases with no cloth room. The cloth then floats loose in a bag and picks up the grit it was meant to remove.

FAQ

What is the single best accessory gift?

A rigid hard case with room for a cloth. It requires no measurement, almost nobody buys one for themselves, and it protects a much more expensive purchase.

Do clip-on sunglasses damage prescription lenses?

Not if the clip grips the frame. The risk comes from clips that rest against the lens surface, and from repeatedly taking them on and off a tight frame. Check where the contact points land before buying.

Are expensive cleaning cloths worth it?

No. The material matters (microfibre, not cotton), the quantity matters more, and a cloth stops working once it is loaded with oil or washed with softener. Buy several cheap ones rather than one premium one.

How much should a full accessories set cost?

Under $30 for a case, a cloth multipack and a lens spray if you buy sensibly. That combination covers the three failures that actually damage glasses: crushing, dry wiping and face-down storage.

The Bottom Line

Buy by problem, not by product listing. Crushed frames need a rigid case, smeared lenses need cloths in quantity, lost glasses need a strap or a stand, and a driver who never uses sunglasses needs a visor clip. Every one of those fixes costs single-digit to low-double-digit dollars, which is exactly why they get postponed forever — and why they make unusually good gifts.
