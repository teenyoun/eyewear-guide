The Three Specs That Actually Matter

1. UV400. UV400 blocks wavelengths up to 400 nm, which covers essentially all UVA and UVB. Dark tint without UV400 is worse than wearing nothing: your pupil dilates behind the tint and absorbs more ultraviolet light. If a listing does not explicitly say "UV400" or "100% UV protection," assume it has neither. We break down the difference between polarization and UV rating here — they are two separate things and cheap listings routinely blur them.

2. Polarization. Polarized lenses cut horizontal glare bouncing off asphalt, water and snow. If you drive or spend time near water, this is a genuine comfort and safety upgrade. Two caveats: polarization can make some car dashboards and phone screens look striped or dark, and it does nothing extra for casual city wear.

3. Coverage and grip. Lens size and temple fit decide whether you actually wear the pair. A frame that slides down your nose gets left at home no matter how good the lenses are. Look for silicone nose pads, spring hinges, and a frame whose total width sits slightly wider than your cheekbones.

Where to Spend and Where to Save

Save on the frame. Frame material matters far less than the internet suggests. TR90 and polycarbonate frames are lighter and more flexible than acetate; they also cost less. The one frame feature worth paying for is adjustable nose pads, because they are what keeps a low-cost pair from sliding.

Spend on the lens rating. The single upgrade that changes your actual eye protection is knowing your lenses are UV400. Everything above that is comfort, not safety.

Skip the extras that add nothing. "Blue light blocking" on a pair of sunglasses is meaningless — dark tints already block most blue light. Mirrored coatings look sporty but scratch visibly and add no UV benefit. Fancy branded hard cases are worth having, but a $6 case protects lenses just as well as a $30 one (our case guide compares materials).

How to Spot a Fake UV400 Claim

  1. The listing never says UV400. Instead it says things like "UV protection," "anti-glare," or "blocks harmful rays." Vague wording is the clearest warning sign.
  2. The price is under $8 and the lens is very dark. Extreme tint at an extreme price usually means an uncoated dyed lens with no UV filter — the worst combination for your eyes.
  3. The bullet points claim "100% UV400 protection" but the description contradicts it. Look for a stated protection level, not a marketing phrase. Reputable listings put "UV400" in the title or the first bullet.
  4. "Polarized test card not included." Not a red flag by itself, but if a seller claims polarization and the listing mentions no test method, treat the claim as unverified until you can check it in person.

FAQ

Are expensive sunglasses safer for my eyes? No. UV400 is UV400 at any price. Above roughly $50 you are paying for materials, fit and brand — real benefits, but not better UV protection.

Is polarization worth paying more for? If you drive, fish, ski or spend time on water, yes. For walking around a city, standard UV400 lenses are fine and polarization is optional.

How do I know if my sunglasses are really polarized? Hold them in front of a screen or reflective surface and rotate them 90 degrees. Polarized lenses will visibly darken or clear up as you turn them. Non-polarized lenses stay the same.

How long should a $50 pair last? Two to three years of regular wear with a case. Replace them when the coating scratches — scratches scatter light and your clarity drops even though the lens still looks fine at a glance.
