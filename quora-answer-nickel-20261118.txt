There are two different questions hidden in that one, and only the first has a real answer. The second — whether a frame can poison you systemically — is not what happens. A frame in contact with skin does not deliver nickel in any quantity that matters internally. What it can do is sensitise the skin, and the dermatitis that follows is unpleasant enough to deserve a straight explanation.

The reaction is allergic contact dermatitis, a type IV delayed hypersensitivity. Nickel in an alloy is not in a form your immune system recognises. It has to leave the metal as Ni²⁺ ions, cross the outer skin layer and bind to your own proteins; only that combined structure is read as foreign. The first exposure produces nothing visible, but the immune system files it away. On later exposures, memory T cells answer 24 to 72 hours after contact. That delay is the diagnostic clue: a rash appearing a day or two after a new frame goes on, not the same afternoon, sitting behind the ear, along the temple line or across the bridge of the nose. You can wear metal frames for twenty years and react for the first time in your forties, because the sensitisation may have come from earrings, a watch back or a belt buckle years earlier.

What determines whether a frame causes trouble is release rate, not nickel content, and this is where most shopping advice goes wrong. In the EU, REACH Annex XVII entry 27 limits articles intended for direct and prolonged skin contact to 0.5 micrograms of nickel per square centimetre per week, with a tighter limit for earrings and piercings. The release test is EN 1811, run on a sample that has first been through the simulated wear and corrosion procedure of EN 12472; spectacle frames, readers and sunglasses have their own method, EN 16128, because a frame is a different geometry from a flat metal disc.

That is why two frames with similar nickel content behave completely differently. Passivated stainless steel contains nickel and still releases very little, because the chromium oxide layer on its surface re-forms when scratched. A plated zinc alloy frame — common in inexpensive metal eyewear — is a stack of base metal, a nickel interlayer and a decorative chromium topcoat. While the plating is intact, release is low. Once it microcracks at the ear bend, the nickel layer is exposed and the local release rate can sit far above the limit. Same model, fine for one wearer, a problem for another, and often fine for a year before it starts to itch.

Anatomy explains the rest. Temples press on the side of the head and the mastoid, usually under hair, where sweat collects and evaporates poorly. Temple tips curl behind the ear against thin skin, pad arms sit on the sides of the nose, and the bridge crosses skin with almost no padding over bone. Occlusion matters as much as the alloy: trapped sweat lowers pH and raises chloride concentration, and chloride is exactly what drives pitting corrosion in nickel-bearing metals. Friction adds a second route, since abraded skin lets ions through more easily, which is why a heavy sliding frame is worse than the same metal fitted properly.

On materials, titanium and beta-titanium release negligible nickel; TR90 and acetate are polymers with no metal in the load-bearing skin-contact structure, though the core wire inside an acetate temple can still be nickel-bearing at the tip. The word hypoallergenic has no legal definition in eyewear and no test behind it, so a declared EN 16128 or EN 1811 result is worth more than any adjective. Clear lacquer over metal works as a barrier while it lasts, but the ear bend is usually where it wears through first.

Two things worth not doing. Do not assume the rash will settle if you keep wearing the frame: sensitisation does not fade with continued exposure, and repeated reactions tend to become more vigorous and to spread, sometimes to the eyelids, which react to nickel carried there on your fingers after handling keys, coins or a phone. And do not buy another alloy by guesswork if the skin is blistered, weeping or broken — that is a dermatologist's patch test, which identifies nickel sulfate sensitivity specifically and separates it from a reaction to a coating, to a preservative in a lens cleaner or to a rubberised temple grip.

Short version: harmful to your skin, yes, in a well-defined and largely avoidable way; harmful to your health in the toxicological sense, no.
